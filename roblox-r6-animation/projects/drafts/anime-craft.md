# Anime craft: timing, acting and action on six parts

What acclaimed TV anime does with timing, acting and action, taken as taste and translated to R6 clips in Poser: smooth against stepped playback, tame and tsume, ones, twos and threes, contrast across a scene, action beats, restrained acting, personality in movement and small gestures. Frieren: Beyond Journey's End is the main quality reference; nothing here copies a shot. Every finding was checked against its source (sources.md, "Anime craft: timing, acting and action on six parts research"). Film timings are at 24 fps; 60 fps conversions multiply by 2.5 and are marked "our conversion". Numbers marked "our choice" or "our reading" are starting values, not the source's. Quotes from Japanese sources are our translations. Hold lists, impact frames and quantised twos for stepped clips are in stylized-and-roblox.md and combat.md, "Anime-style stepped strikes"; this file does not repeat them. In this file a step is one held pose of a stepped clip, a key gap is the frames between two keys, and frames are 60 fps frames unless marked film frames.

## Smooth playback or stepped

The rule under test: anime timing sense works best inside smooth 60 fps playback, with full stepped timing only where it suits, such as emotes and finishers. For it: modern action animators work at high drawing rates and build rhythm from spacing, and stepped drawings misalign against a moving camera, which on Roblox is every gameplay frame. Against it: anime staff find full-rate motion too slick for many shots, and limited animation deliberately hides subtle motion. The sources agree on what decides the result: even timing with even spacing fails at any rate. Verdict: keep gameplay clips smooth and put the anime contrast into spacing and holds; build stepped versions for fixed-camera emotes and finishers and let the requester pick per clip.

- **Modern action anime gets its rhythm from spacing, not from stepping.** Animétudes on Hiroto Nagata: "an even timing, most often on 1's and 2's". On Ryuki Hashimoto's run and jump: timing mostly between 1s and 2s, with the 3DCG background constantly on 1s, so "it is the spacing which creates the real sense of rhythm, very close when the action slows down and much faster as it accelerates." Yutaka Nakamura "focuses on rhythm rather than timing" (Animétudes, "The Kanada style now"). On R6: keep `curve = "spline"` and put the anime rhythm into the key gaps: in a lunge, torso keys whose gaps shrink as the body accelerates (8, 5, 3, 2 frames) and widen into the settle (3, 6, 10 frames; our example). The world and the camera already update every frame, like that 3DCG background.
- **Smooth motion fails only when it is even.** Animétudes on Yasuomi Umetsu: "the uniform timing on 1s or 2s makes the character animation too fluid and regular, to the point of tedium"; with timing that regular and spacing that even, the animation is "completely uninteresting", and "without the sense of spontaneity built by the timing, the animation ends up mechanical and jarring." Wave Motion Cannon reports Richard Williams' view that ones look better in nearly every case; he grants that ones can look "mushy" and do not look as good with "dumb, mechanical inbetweens". The article shows a cut on smooth ones whose evenly spaced in-betweens look "soulless and artificial". On R6: compute the leading joint's speed per frame from `Poser.posesAt` samples. Six or more frames at near-constant speed, or a key list with equal gaps, is this failure even in a smooth clip. Break it with a favour key 1 to 2 frames from one end (lesson-videos.md, "4. Bluebiscuits: the practical tricks"), not by stepping. A `Poser.check` contrast under 2, below the one-shot range in SKILL.md, step 4, flags the same fault.
- **Full-rate motion can look too slick, so anime staff pick the rate per cut.** Naoki Miyahara, episode director on The First Slam Dunk (a film that fuses CG and hand-drawn animation), in MOVIE WALKER PRESS: the film used everything from full animation to threes, point by point, mainly at a threes tempo; the team first showed director Takehiko Inoue that full animation looks slick ("nurutto"), then showed frame-adjusted versions and let him choose per cut. The article explains that full animation allows delicate expression but often moves too smoothly and looks wrong, and costs many more drawings, which is why Japanese animation mostly uses threes. Takashi Nakamura put The Portrait Studio on twos because "24 frames per second would be too fluid for the mood in the film, and 8 frames per second would weaken the appeal in the image" (Animation Obsessive). On R6: R6 is a 3D body, so the slick risk is real in slow, stylized acting. For a fixed-camera moment (an emote with its own camera, a finisher cutscene), build two labelled versions, smooth and stepped (mostly 7 to 8 frame steps, the threes of film by our conversion, with 2 to 5 frame steps on the fast beats), and let the requester pick per clip (process.md, "Picking references and options").
- **A moving camera needs a moving body.** Same interview: where camera movement and character movement do not mesh, even twos produce a slight misalignment, so those cuts were drawn in full while avoiding a slick look. Wave Motion Cannon: when the camera pans across a background, the animation needs ones, "or else the movement of the background and the animation will not match up". On R6: the player camera follows HumanoidRootPart on every rendered frame. A body that steps every 5 to 8 frames while the root and the camera glide shows this mismatch on every gameplay frame. Keep gameplay clips smooth; step only when the camera is fixed or cut to, and keep root travel smooth even then (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Stepped anime timing").
- **Limited animation hides subtle motion; an R6 hold must not freeze.** Kitamura, Kanamori, Mitani, Fukui and Tsuruno (University of Tsukuba, IWAIT 2014) model limited animation by two features: it "does not show subtle motion because it omits almost-static inbetween frames", and it exaggerates speed by omitting in-betweens (nakanashi). Their converter holds a pose for at most three frames at 24 fps where motion is smallest, because repeating more "makes the motion completely stop", and found about a third of all frames held to be adequate. On R6: this contradicts the living-hold rule (SKILL.md: frozen 0%, `life` 0.6 to 1.5 degrees; principles.md, "Moving holds and settles"). Keep both, by clip type. In smooth clips keep the drift and `life`. In a stepped emote or finisher, keep each step to at most 7 to 8 frames (three film frames, our conversion) outside named holds, and change the whole pose a little on every step, as Mitsuo Iso's "full limited" animation treats "every single pose as key" (Sakuga Blog). Set `life = 0` and no springs on a stepped clip: stylized-and-roblox.md, "Stepped anime timing", already bars springs on stepped keys, and `life` noise would make every step drift (our reading).
- **Roblox caps the finest step at the simulation rate.** Creator docs: with `Workspace.UseFixedSimulation`, the Server Authority setting, "character controller updates and joint transforms are performed at a fixed frequency rather than once per frame". Developers report that frequency as 60: one saw the character at 60 FPS in a game running at 165 FPS. On R6: author and bake at 60 fps and treat 1 frame as the smallest step. In such places a high-refresh screen already shows the character steppier than the world, so do not add stepping to gameplay clips there. Remote characters can update less often under Animator LOD (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Level of detail and masks").
- **Export steps as dense keys, not as Constant keys in a curve animation.** A DevForum bug report (October 2025, still reproducing for the reporter in July 2026): Constant keyframes in curve-editor animations "interpolate as if they were linear", so parts visibly slide between steps; Constant keys in normal keyframe animations still worked. On R6: deliver a stepped clip through `Poser.bake`, which writes a key on every 60 fps frame, so each `step` hold plays exactly. Do not rebuild it as a curve animation with Constant keys, and check the played track per frame in Play.

## Tame, tsume and nakanashi

- **Tame and tsume name the key the in-betweens crowd toward.** A Ritsumeikan University paper (Entertainment Computing Symposium 2019): limited animation in-betweens a move with few drawings and, instead of even in-betweens, uses tame and tsume. With tame the drawings near the first key are drawn close to it; with tsume the drawings near the last key are drawn close to it. A strong tame stresses the acceleration, a strong tsume the deceleration; the exaggeration breaks physics but reads as natural "mental motion". On R6: the same is a split of frames against travel. For a 120 degree arm swing over 20 frames, a strong tame puts a key at frame 8 at 10% of the travel, a strong tsume a key at frame 12 at 90%, all `auto` (our example). Between those keys the arm turns 96 degrees in 4 frames, a key gap of 24 degrees per frame, inside the 25 degree cap for key gaps (principles.md, "Strike speed and retiming").
- **Write the spacing, not the word.** The terms are not used one way. Toru Yoshida (mecha and effects animator, CGWORLD) uses tame for the slowing before a stop. The Ritsumeikan paper uses tame for crowding at the first key. A Japanese encyclopedia entry on in-betweening (douga) warns that "tsume" means the opposite in drawn animation and in 3DCG. On R6: in a clip plan or a review note, never write "more tame"; write "frames 0 to 8 cover the first 10% of the swing" or "the last 45 degrees take the last 8 frames".
- **A fast arrival still slows before it stops.** Yoshida: a 3D robot that spins round lacks weight when it moves at the same speed throughout; "even when it flies in with momentum, just before it stops, tame it", and drawn animation spends the most drawings on the parts that move little by little. On R6: a turn, spin, slide or dash that ends in a held pose with nothing to hit spends its last quarter of travel over about a third of its frames (our choice): a 180 degree turn over 24 frames covers its last 45 degrees in the last 8 frames, `auto` keys, `e = "flat"` only on the final pose. When the stop is a contact, the impact rule wins: no ease into an impact (SKILL.md, the "Spacing, slow in and slow out" row); the cushion goes after the rebound.
- **Nakanashi drops the in-betweens at peak speed, a few at most.** The Tsukuba converter deletes the frames with the most motion: 4 to 6 consecutive film frames from the fastest stretch of a pitch, a kick, a tennis swing and a baseball swing. Its clips of 88 to 129 film frames used 4 to 6 deletions; "around five is just right", and above five "the motion no longer looks continuous". On R6: in a smooth clip, play the fastest stretch faster than authored with `warp`, so fewer real frames show it, and slow the load slightly to keep the length. Here the 0.08 s before a contact key at 0.4 plays in about 2.4 real frames instead of 5:

  ```lua
  clip.warp = {{0, 0.9}, {0.3, 0.9}, {0.32, 2}, {0.4, 2}, {0.41, 1}}
  ```

  Read the real hit time with `Poser.realTime(clip, 0.4)`. On a melee hit, replace the last point with the hitstop segment, which starts on the contact key (combat.md, "Hitstop"). Check the sampled joint turn per real frame against principles.md, "Strike speed and retiming", and use one or two such bursts per clip, never on every beat. In a stepped finisher, delete the strike's middle steps so the fist goes from the load step to the step one frame before contact.
- **Two poses can carry half a second.** Yoshida on Yoshinori Kanada: an explosion drawn with two key drawings and no in-betweens fills 12 film frames (0.5 s): the first drawing shows for 5 frames, the motion jumps, the next shows for 7, "and with two drawings it looks like an explosion". On R6: in a stepped finisher, a 30-frame beat can be two steps of about 12 and 18 frames (our conversion of 5 and 7) joined by a jump with no in-between; the longer second step is the result and gets the stronger silhouette. In a smooth clip the same beat is a 2 to 3 frame change between two moving holds of those lengths.
- **Find the timing by acting it, filming it and saying its sound.** Yoshida: act the motion yourself, film it, and write both the real timing and the timing that feels right on the timesheet; "there is always a difference", for example "move sharply only at the start, then slowly". Mecha animators "talk in onomatopoeia": a robot that rises with a "juiiin" is 18 frames, and the sound alone sets the timesheet. On R6: keep two beat tables in the clip plan, the reference's real frame counts and the stylized ones, and report the difference next to the reference at hand-off (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference", step 5). An 18-frame rise is 45 frames at 60 fps (our conversion). When a move has a sound effect, make the move as long as the sound's main body and put the key on its peak; pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays", gives the `PlaybackLoudness` method for cutscenes.

## Ones, twos, threes and modulation

- **Threes are the base; ones are spent where they count.** Yoshida: 2D anime runs at 24 fps with threes (each drawing shot three times) as the basic, and ones, twos and fours chosen by direction; for a missile launch he prefers all-key fours: ones are possible when drawings are plentiful, but usually they are not, and for feel fours are enough. The Ritsumeikan paper: mainstream limited animation draws 8 or 12 drawings a second. Wave Motion Cannon: in a Hayao Miyazaki scene from Samurai Giants most of the action is on threes and only the speed of the pitcher's throw is on twos. A CLIP STUDIO tutorial gives the general split: ones for fast action, twos or threes for normal movement. On R6: the lesson is where anime spends motion, not its rate. In a smooth clip, most beats change slowly (key gaps of 8 to 12 frames) and only the one beat that carries the read gets keys 1 to 3 frames apart (our choice).
- **Change the rate inside one move to give it texture.** Animétudes ("Animation and subjectivity"): in a Miyazaki shot a pitcher's slow, deliberate wind-up stretch runs on a 3-4 rhythm, and when he raises his leg the timing oscillates between threes and twos with the acceleration. The rate "may sometimes change two or three times" in a second; viewers rarely notice, but it "gives the movement its individuality and most of its texture". Wave Motion Cannon: Kanada's irregular rate makes motion "feel more lifelike and organic than they would be if evenly timed". On R6: in a smooth clip, change the key gap two or three times a second inside one move: for a wind-up and throw, keys every 8 to 10 frames through the wind-up, 5 to 7 through the acceleration, 2 to 3 through the release, then 6 and 10 into the settle (our reading). Stepped hold lists are in stylized-and-roblox.md, "Holds and contrast".
- **Give the finest timing to the part the eye should follow.** Wave Motion Cannon: modulation can "establish a visual hierarchy of motion"; in Mamoru Hosoda's Digimon Adventure the effects move at different rates from the characters, "which draws your eyes to the most important movement", and the electric effects switch from ones to threes, which "pop out on the screen and makes us feel the power of the impact". Keiichiro Kimura's Tiger Mask opening runs on twos and switches to ones on the cape swirl as the hero transforms. Takashi Nakamura put the water on ones in a film on twos "to give it more dynamism" (Animation Obsessive). On R6: name the part the player should watch in each beat and give only it the dense keys and the overshoot: in a cast, the casting arm sweeps on key gaps of 2 to 3 frames while the torso and head move on gaps of 8 to 10 (our example). The read is a difference of texture as well as speed; SKILL.md already requires the strike to be the fastest motion in its window.
- **Two subjects, two timings: the difference shows who is who.** Animétudes: in a cut by Hisashi Mori a fireball moves on ones and a giant bird on twos; at the hit the bird, writhing, switches to ones, and the fireball, which has lost energy, drops to threes. In Mushishi the mushi creatures are always on ones, "which emphasizes their otherness". Animétudes ties this to the principle "if you want to show the difference between two characters, animate them doing the same thing". Yasuo Otsuka used ones "to emphasize the speed of a thrown axe" and threes "to emphasize the scale of a giant" (Animation Obsessive). On R6: give a summoned companion or stand a timing texture unlike its user's: for example, a spirit on continuous spline motion with `life = 1.5` and `drag` springs on its arms, never settling, against a user whose poses settle into short moving holds; a mechanical summon on crisp short key gaps with `lead` springs (our reading). Poser plays one clip per rig, so each rig carries its own clip and timing. At a hit, the victim's motion becomes the fastest on screen while the attacker creeps (combat.md, "Hitstop").
- **The strike is fastest, the slowdown widens, the fall goes uneven.** Animétudes on Hashimoto's dropkick: "the animation begins on 1s, switches to 3s when the movement slows down, and then suddenly becomes irregular when the orc falls, oscillating between 4s, 3s and 2s." On Shingo Yamashita: mostly ones, some twos, "exceptionally 3s on some of the impacts". On R6: in a smooth clip, the last 2 to 3 frames before contact are the densest; after contact the key gaps widen (4, 6, 8 frames) as the limb slows; a victim's fall gets an uneven key rhythm (gaps of 10, 6, 9, 5 frames through a stumble, a bounce and a settle) instead of one long ease (our numbers). In a stepped finisher: 2 to 3 frame steps on the kick, 7 to 8 on the slowdown, mixed 5 to 10 on the fall (our conversion).

## Contrast across a scene

- **Keep the burst at real speed and put the time into the preparation.** Sakuga Blog on Hyakuemu (2025): director Kenji Iwaisawa limited each race to "barely over 10 seconds" to put viewers in the athletes' headspace, although longer races would have been easier excitement; the manga's author "treasured the contrast between lengthy preparation and the blink-and-you-miss-it races". On R6: for a showcase move (an ultimate, an emote's payoff), keep the decisive action at real speed and short (a dash or strike of 6 to 12 frames) and give the reading time to the preparation before it (60 to 120 frames) and the moving hold after it (our choice). stylized-and-roblox.md, "Holds and contrast", allows slow motion around the contact; use slow motion for spectacle and real time when the player should feel inside the action. Player attacks keep the budgets in combat.md.
- **Quiet clips make the loud ones land.** Real Sound on Frieren: a long TV run needs contrast of pace across episodes, not only flashy battles but how ordinary daily life is shown, and Frieren's works very well. Animation producer Takashi Nakame noted that episode 10 had "quite literally half the drawings" of episode 9 and still had to hold viewers (Sakuga Blog). The Sakugabooru Animation Awards 2023 named Frieren best show: what left the strongest impression "isn't so much the certainly impressive action sequences, but rather its character animation". On R6: plan a character's clip set as a whole: quiet, specific idles, walks and interactions, and full intensity only for a few signature moves. Print each clip's peak joint speeds (SKILL.md, step 4) and rank the clips; if most sit near the top, the set has no contrast.
- **Set the real scale, then break it.** Sakuga Blog on Frieren #06, storyboarded by action director Toru Iwazawa: Stark's "weighty walk builds up the tension", and the boards establish "the objective scale of the battle… just to progressively betray it as it explodes into a spectacular setpiece that goes far beyond that physical reality." On R6: in a boss entrance or a finisher, open with grounded motion at real weight: a slow, heavy walk (locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk"), the COM over the support (body-mechanics.md, "Balance and weight shift"), jumps no higher than the Humanoid's. Only after 1 to 3 s of that (our choice), let one move break physics: a leap far above JumpHeight 7.2 or a spin faster than body-mechanics.md, "Flips and spins", allows.
- **A ritual stays slow and even; one burst breaks it.** Sakuga Blog on Tsurune (Kyoto Animation): episode 1 shows a sports festival in boisterous animation, then the same artists "dial up the restrain to capture the nobility of kyudo"; the shots keep "bursts of explosive animation" for impact, but that "comes second to the solemn, ritualistic depiction". On R6: a ceremonial action (a bow, a slow sword draw, a shot) keeps even, slow key gaps of 10 to 20 frames, symmetric held poses and `life = 0.6`, spends its one fast moment on the release in 2 to 4 frames, then holds the follow-through 30 frames or more while it drifts (our numbers).

## Action beats

- **Surprise may drop the anticipation, in a cutscene only.** Animétudes on Takeshi Maenami: "the way the character suddenly approaches the camera without anticipation and swings her cat is quite impressive and unexpected." On R6: this conflicts with the Anticipation row of SKILL.md and with combat.md, "Telegraphs and reaction time" (an unexpected fast action is missed without a bigger anticipation). Keep both: drop the pull-back only in a cutscene or an emote where surprise is the point and a set camera frames the move; in gameplay keep at least one frame of pull-back.
- **The length of the impact sets the weight.** Yoshida: the biggest factor in weight is speed; "if the shock of the collision is short, 'don', it looks light; if it is slow, 'dooon', it looks heavy." Weight also shows through surrounding information, such as the ground cracking under a robot's foot and fragments flying. On R6: after a landing or a stomp, the length of the sink and recovery sets the weight: a light body sinks for 3 to 4 frames and recovers in 6 to 10; a heavy body sinks for 5 to 8 frames and recovers in 20 to 30, the arms settling last (our starting numbers; check the landing timing in body-mechanics.md, "Takeoff and landing"). Cracks and debris are VFX: offer them as a question (SKILL.md).
- **Effort shows where the head holds still.** Kouta Morie (Transistor Studio, CGWORLD column): in his in-between before a leg swings down, the hips move but the head stays almost where it was two frames before, which acts "straining to swing the leg down"; the same pose sets up the speed of the foot, the drop of the hand and the head lift that follows. On R6: in the 2 to 4 frames before a stomp, kick or overhead swing, let the torso drop or rock while the Head key counters it, so the head's facing changes by under 2 degrees, then release the head 1 to 2 frames after the torso moves on. The head rides on the torso, so only its angle can hold, not its position. Key it on the torso's frames, never with `lag` (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain"). Make each breakdown point at the next beat, as Morie's does, rather than sit halfway.

## Acting with restraint

- **Restraint is a dial set per beat.** Miku Kadowaki (Kyoto Animation), as animation director: "I try to approach my work like a stage director for theater, as if I'm telling the characters, 'You need to cry more in this scene!' or 'Show some more emotional restraint here!'" On Hibike! Euphonium S3, Mayu's voice actress found the animation said so much that she worked out "how much to subtract when necessary so that things weren't spelled out too clearly before their due time" (Sakuga Blog). On R6: write one number per beat in the clip plan: the share of the full gesture that shows (a sad beat at 40% early and 100% only on the beat that pays it off; our example), and scale the channels about the first frame to that share (principles.md, "Strike speed and retiming", "More range without losing the posture"). keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", already holds that stifled emotion can hit harder; this makes it a number.
- **Temper each gesture to the person.** Keiichiro Saito, Frieren's director, on Bocchi the Rock! (Sakuga Blog interview): the performance scenes are built on live-action footage, and the performers kept the characterization in mind. Hitori's performer tempered the gestures, "with fairly subdued posture and motions, to give the impression of not being used to performing"; Ryo, normally quiet, plays "with a lot of enthusiasm"; Nijika is characterized through gestures with Ryo, a silent conversation mid-performance with emphasis on eye contact. On R6: author one action at a different size per character: a shy or unpractised character at 50 to 70% of the reference's arm and torso range, arms kept within about 15 degrees of the body sides, head pitched down 5 to 10 degrees; a reserved character who comes alive in action at 110 to 120%, with more torso roll (our ranges). Two characters who know each other exchange a head turn at a beat change, with no other gesture.
- **Perform the reference bigger than life.** Sakuga Blog on Hyakuemu: Iwaisawa had the actors perform "in emphatic, somewhat exaggerated fashion that would feel more lifelike once stylized". On R6: when filming or choosing reference for an acting clip, ask for a bigger performance than natural, because R6 loses the hands, the face and the spine, then copy it as authored (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference", step 4). A bigger performance keeps its own timing; pushing a quiet reference afterwards changes only the poses.
- **Bend textbook gestures with the feeling.** Sakuga Blog on Yubisaki to Renren: the sign language supervisor said not to portray signs "like a series of gestures and poses you'd see in a textbook"; director Murano added "imperfections like making fingers tremble nervously, or even distorting the signs a bit so that they capture the emotion of the characters at the time." On R6: treat a catalog gesture (a wave, a point, a salute) as a starting shape and bend it with the mood: a nervous wave lower (the fist near shoulder height), half the swing, with a small arm tremble ramped in (principles.md, "Moving holds and settles", gives tremble sizes and ramps); a proud salute a few degrees past square and held longer.
- **A hidden feeling leaks through one small part.** Sakuga Blog on Tsurune: the young Nikaido, "not used to suppressing his feelings yet", is shown with "nervous mannerisms when he can't bring himself to tell his master how he feels". In Eupho S3, Kumiko "actually pulls away" when Mayu approaches; the rejection reads "despite neither of them actually speaking their mind". On R6: a character hiding a feeling holds the main pose steady and lets one small part leak for 8 to 15 frames: the free arm pulses 3 to 6 degrees, the head leaves its target and returns, or the weight shifts (our numbers). An unwelcome approach first gets a small lean away, torso `px` 0.1 to 0.2 studs away from the other character with `Feet.flat` keeping the soles, before any larger reaction.
- **Act with the body; the face may not be seen.** Sakuga Blog on Pokemon Sun & Moon: Kai Nanba's acting "gained articulation in a more traditional way, making it convey the right emotion even when his amusing faces aren't visible." On R6: the stock face is a static decal and the player camera usually sits behind, so carry each emotion in torso pitch and roll, head angle and arm shape. Check it from behind: the `rear34` strip with the face decal removed (SKILL.md, Appeal row) must still show the emotion to a fresh reviewer (keyframe-workflow.md, "Review with fresh eyes").

## Personality in movement

- **Give each character signature mannerisms and repeat them.** Sakuga Blog on Eupho S3: over nearly a decade Kyoto Animation built "perhaps the most impressive acting library in anime altogether"; Mayu fiddles with her hair when anxious, "in a distinct way than Sally does", and has a "tendency to sidestep when fleeing"; five episodes after Kanade's habitual poke, Kumiko blocks it. Behavior that characters and audience learn to recognize "adds a lot to the experience". On R6: give each character two or three signature gestures (a hand raised beside the head when anxious, a sidestep before turning away, a habitual weight shift) and reuse the same keys across its idles, emotes and reactions, changing size, not shape. Solve the raised arm from the wanted direction (r6-mechanics.md, "Direction and rotation checks") and keep the fist within the overhead reach (SKILL.md, "Overhead reach").
- **Show the baseline before the change.** Sakuga Blog on Frieren #09 (Kouki Fujimoto): Fern is deadpan on the surface; "by seeing her normal walk and even some hesitation", tied to her feelings of inferiority, "her confidence and outright swagger in this fight hits even harder." On R6: when a character's arc has a confident payoff, keep its default walk and idle modest: a shorter stride, the head 3 to 5 degrees down, the arms close (locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk"). In the payoff, change the same parts the other way: head level, chest up 5 to 8 degrees bent at the waist (SKILL.md, "Waist rule"), arms out, a slower, heavier step (our numbers). Build both from the same keys so the attitude is the only difference.
- **An awkward walk reads as innocence.** Sakuga Blog on Pokemon Sun & Moon: Yasushi Nishiya animated "with more restraint, emphasizing Mao and Suiren's childlike innocence via details like their awkward walking." On R6: for a childlike or awkward walk, make the left and right steps differ by 1 to 2 frames, place the feet closer to the centre line and give the arms a smaller, less regular swing (our reading). Keep double support at 55 to 60% (SKILL.md, "Walks need double support") and author it from a measured child or toddler reference, not by breaking a symmetric cycle.
- **Broad moves make a simple body feel rich.** Sakuga Blog on Pokemon Sun & Moon: Masaaki Iwane, the team's ace for 22 years, has "admitted he uses" broad and exaggerated movements "in an attempt to make the audience feel like the animation is more elaborate than it really is." On R6: six rigid parts show little detail, so an outgoing character's gesture travels far: an arm arc of 90 degrees or more with the torso turning and the weight shifting under it, not a small precise move the player's camera cannot see. Keep the restraint findings above for reserved characters.

## Small gestures that keep living

- **Small actions are where Frieren lives.** The Sakugabooru Animation Awards 2023 on Frieren: "There is something so graspable and tactile in a lot of the carefully depicted smaller motions. Every tiny, seemingly unimportant action suddenly becomes precious." In episode #04, an early-morning village job is "an excellent opportunity for animators to compete to draw the cutest slob of an elf" (Sakuga Blog). On R6: give idles and emotes small, task-like actions with a full arc: a sleepy slump that settles in three stages (torso pitch 4, then 7, then 9 degrees, the head trailing by 2 to 4 frames), a shoulder stretch, a strap hitched; each with its own anticipation, travel and settle of 3 to 10 degrees (our numbers). `life` noise does not replace them (principles.md, "Moving holds and settles").
- **Small irregular secondary motions make a body feel alive.** Animétudes ("The rise of realism") on Mitsuo Iso's mobile suits in War in the Pocket: "a lot of little gestures and secondary movements which managed to create an impression of well-oiled machinery and life at the same time. The way the arm of the mecha recoils as it lands feels very natural". Toshiyuki Inoue showed a "sensibility for small, irregular movements". On R6: on a landing or a stop, let each arm recoil and settle on its own timing: different spring presets per arm (`follow` on one, `drag` on the other) or hand keys where one arm overshoots 4 to 8 degrees and returns over 5 to 8 frames and the other moves 2 frames later and smaller (our numbers). Vary these small motions between clips so no two repeat exactly.
- **A tremble that stops is a decision.** Sakuga Blog on Frieren #06: the match cut between Stark's trembling hands and his master's shows he inherited "the philosophy to embrace fear as a necessary aspect when facing a battle"; his hands convey resolve and fear. On R6: R6 has no fingers, so tremble the whole arm: key alternating small offsets on its lift and side every 2 to 3 frames, or add them in `post`, ramped in and out as principles.md, "Moving holds and settles", describes. Stop the tremble on the decision beat (a step forward, a grip on the weapon): the stillness after it is the acting beat, and the hold that follows still drifts.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Lip-sync and mouth shapes (Frieren #09's lip-sync; three mouth shapes alternated by timing, Ritsumeikan paper): R6 has no face rig; a face decal swap is runtime code outside Poser.
- Blinks drawn as three eye shapes, open, closed, half, open (Ritsumeikan paper): only as a face decal swap, which needs new runtime code; keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", says when a blink belongs.
- Hair loops and fluttering hair that link cuts (Frieren #09): a stock R6 rig has no hair joints; it needs a rigged accessory (custom-rigs.md).
- Fabric moved by personal mannerisms (Akiko Takase's corrections on Frieren): Poser has no cloth.
- Faces that melt for comedy (Bocchi the Rock!): no face geometry.
- Distorted outlines and limbs smeared under speed (Nagata, Animétudes): parts cannot scale; use trails (combat.md, "Smears and trails").
- Shading folds that merge with smears (Hashimoto, Animétudes): rendering on drawings, not motion.
- Multi-perspective drawing and space twisted for emphasis (Yoshida; Tsurune's assistant CG director Mitsuki Takaki): a 3D view has one perspective; only a cutscene camera can cheat.
- Cutting between fights, hair in one and an explosion in another, to connect them (Frieren #09): editing; the player owns the gameplay camera, and SKILL.md excludes cutscenes unless asked.
- Drawn effect forms (fire, water, lightning, Yoshida): VFX, not body motion; out of scope unless asked.
- Trembling fingers and signing hands (Stark, Yubisaki to Renren): no fingers; the arm-level tremble above is the substitute.
