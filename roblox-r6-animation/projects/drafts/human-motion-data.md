# Human motion data: gait, running, strikes and jumps

Measured human joint angles, timings, speeds and weight shifts for walks, runs, starts, stops, turns, punches, throws, kicks and jumps, each translated through the six R6 parts into Poser keys. Real data is the target for timing, weight and balance; the translation decides what reads on rigid blocks. Every finding was checked against its source (sources.md, "Human motion data: gait, running, strikes and jumps research"). Human-to-R6 scaling uses 5 studs for a 1.75 m body (2.86 studs per metre), as [locomotion.md](locomotion.md) does; numbers marked "our derivation" are computed here from the sourced ones. The knee rule used throughout: an R6 leg is 2 studs from hip to sole, so a human leg with equal thigh and shank folded at knee angle k reads on R6 as a whole-leg `lift` of (hip angle - k/2) and a slide up into the torso of 2(1 - cos(k/2)) studs (our derivation: 0.03 studs at 20 degrees, 0.12 at 40, 0.27 at 60, 0.59 at 90, 1.0 at 120). Fighting-game frame budgets stay in [combat.md](combat.md); the walk and run recipes stay in [principles.md](principles.md).

## Walking: the legs through the cycle

- **The leg swings further back than forward, because the knee folds at push-off.** Brunnekreef et al. (2005) tabulate normal sagittal angles by phase: hip 25 degrees flexed in early stance (0 to 10% of the cycle), 0 in midstance (10 to 30%), 10 extended at 30 to 50% and 0 at 50 to 60%, 15 flexed in early swing (60 to 70%) and 25 flexed in late swing (70 to 100%); knee 20 flexed, 0, 40, 60 and 0 over the same phases; the trunk stays above the hip throughout. On R6, with the knee rule above, the whole leg sits about 25 degrees forward at full length through late swing into contact, about 15 forward as the knee gives in early stance, 0 in midstance, 20 to 30 degrees back at push-off (with a 0.12 stud slide), and still 15 back in early swing (slid up 0.27) (our derivation). Key the swing leg's `lift` and its `p` y slide on those phases: in a 60-frame (1.0 s) cycle the percentages are frame numbers. The thigh never goes more than 10 degrees back, but the R6 leg must, which is why hand-keyed legs swing further back than forward (principles.md, Walk).
- **Show the swing-knee bend; skip the stance-knee bend.** In the same table the knee bends twice: 20 degrees just after contact and 60 degrees in early swing (60 to 70% of the cycle). On R6 the stance bend is a 0.03 stud slide, below what reads, so put that give into the torso `py` dip instead (body-mechanics.md, Walk mechanics). The swing bend is the one to show: slide the swing leg up about 0.27 studs, peaking at 60 to 70% of the cycle (frames 36 to 42 of a 60-frame cycle), and back to full length before contact. The R6 references measured 0.32 to 0.37 (principles.md, Walk; r6-mechanics.md, Walk and run legs), a little past the human value, in line with pushing poses past natural (SKILL.md, Exaggeration).
- **A slower walk is mostly a smaller knee.** The meta-analysis of Fukuchi, Fukuchi and Duarte (2019; 20 studies, 587 people) found that in young adults walking slower than comfortable cut knee flexion with a large effect (effect size -0.90), hip flexion and extension with small effects (-0.34 and -0.45) and ankle plantarflexion with a moderate one (-0.54); walking faster raised hip flexion and ankle plantarflexion with small effects (0.41 and 0.32), while cadence and step length changed with large effects. On R6, when you slow a walk, shrink the swing leg's slide harder than its `lift` range, and change the cadence and stride as well; a stroll keeps most of its hip swing but loses its knee. When you speed a walk up, add hip swing and step length rather than more slide.
- **Keep a walk's legs low and its torso high.** On the Roblox DevForum, a reply to an R6 animation set (2022) said any R6 walk that raises the legs above the waist instead of a normal swing looks very weird; two others said the walk's torso sat too low. Human hips flex only 25 degrees in a walk (Brunnekreef et al., above). On R6, keep a walk's forward `lift` near 25 to 30 degrees and keep high knees for runs; keep the torso near standing height with only the small bob of the next section.

## Walking: pelvis, chest, head and arms

- **The pelvis turns forward on the stepping side; put that turn in the legs.** Brunnekreef et al. (2005) give 5 degrees of forward pelvic rotation in early stance and late swing and 5 backward in late stance and early swing. The Oklahoma gait notes (Saunders, Inman and Eberhart's determinants) explain why: in double support the pelvis is forward on the loading side and back on the push-off side, which lengthens both legs and limits the drop of the centre of mass. An inertial-sensor study of 155 young adults (Jung and Kang, 2026) measured about 20 degrees of pelvic rotation range in walking, fast walking and running alike (20.4, 20.5, 21.8). On R6 the torso carries the hips, so do not twist it for the pelvis: slide each leg's `p` z forward at its forward extreme and back at its back extreme by about 0.04 to 0.09 studs (our derivation: 5 to 10 degrees on a 0.5 stud hip radius). This sizes the rule in locomotion.md, Walk body motion, and lengthens the step without a bigger leg angle.
- **The chest turns about 8 degrees, nearly opposite the pelvis, and the head turns less.** Pontzer et al. (2009) measured at 1.5 m/s: shoulder rotation 8.1 degrees, head yaw 5.2 degrees, and a pelvis-to-shoulder phase difference of 149 degrees (nearly opposite). On R6: torso `twist` about plus or minus 4 (matching locomotion.md, Walk body motion), and a `Head` `twist` of about -0.36 times the torso twist on the same frames, so the head turns about two thirds as far as the chest (our derivation from 5.2 / 8.1, assuming the head turns with the chest).
- **Walking arms swing by themselves, opposite the legs.** Collins, Adamczyk and Kuo (2009; 10 walkers at 1.25 m/s) found that normal arm swing needed almost no shoulder torque, with the arms' angular momentum opposite the legs'; holding the arms still raised the metabolic cost by 12% and swinging them in the wrong phase by 26%, because the feet had to resist more twist. In their passive model, slow walking produced a low swing at twice the leg frequency, which the authors note humans also show. Pontzer et al. (2009) found the front and back deltoid firing together rather than in turn to drive the arm. On R6, let the arms follow the torso twist and the legs as pendulums (a `lag` of 1 to 2 frames, 0.017 to 0.033 s, principles.md, Walk) rather than as separately driven sines. When the arms are busy in a walk (a two-handed carry), the torso twist can stay as it is: Pontzer et al. found no significant change in walking (the run is different, see Running). A very slow shuffle may give the arms a small swing once per step instead of once per stride.
- **Arm swing grows with speed: about 30 degrees walking, 40 brisk, 45 jogging.** Jung and Kang (2026) measured shoulder sagittal range of 27 to 32 degrees in walking, 38 to 42 in fast walking and 43 to 48 in running, and elbow range of 21 to 27 degrees walking and 30 to 36 fast walking. On R6, size the arm `lift` swing to 30 degrees for a stroll, 40 for a brisk walk and 45 for a realistic jog; the elbow range has no joint and stays hidden. The R6 runs that read well used a free arm near plus or minus 55 to 62 (principles.md, Run), about two and a half times the human jog: use that for a stylized run read from the player camera, and the human range for a realistic jog.
- **A slow walk sways more and bobs less; a fast walk bobs more and sways less.** Orendurff et al. (2004; 10 adults at 0.7 to 1.6 m/s) measured side-to-side centre of mass motion falling from 6.99 cm at the slowest speed to 3.85 cm at the fastest, while the vertical motion rose from 2.74 to 4.83 cm. On R6 this sizes the rule in body-mechanics.md, Walk mechanics: a slow walk gets about 0.20 studs of total torso `px` sway and 0.08 of `py` bob; a fast walk about 0.11 of sway and 0.14 of bob.

## Running: timing by speed

- **A jog's cycle barely shortens with speed; stride and stance do the work.** Jiménez et al. (2026; 25 recreational runners, median men) measured at 7, 9, 11 and 13 km/h: cycles of 0.75, 0.74, 0.72 and 0.69 s; stance of 0.34, 0.30, 0.28 and 0.26 s (46, 42, 39 and 37% of the cycle); strides of 1.46, 1.85, 2.19 and 2.50 m; vertical oscillation near 0.06 m at every speed. On R6 a jog cycle is 41 to 45 frames, each foot down 16 to 20 frames; build faster jogs with a longer stride and fewer stance frames, not a shorter cycle, and keep the bob near 0.17 studs at every speed. These speeds scale to 5.6 to 10.3 studs/s, and a stance of 1.9 to 2.4 studs covers them up to about 11 km/h (8.7 studs/s), so a jog at real duty plants inside the R6 stance limit (our derivation). The R6 run rule of feet down about 25% of the cycle (SKILL.md, Runs are judged by the legs) matches a sprint, not a jog: keep 25% for the fast, stylized run that rule describes and use 37 to 46% for a jog.
- **Up to about 7 m/s runners lengthen the stride; above it they swing the legs faster.** Dorn, Schache and Pandy (2012; 3.5 to 9.0 m/s) found stride length growing more than stride frequency up to 7 m/s and the reverse above it; ground contact time fell steadily with speed and flight time peaked at 7 m/s. Weyand et al. (2000; 33 runners with top speeds from 6.2 to 11.1 m/s) found faster runners pressing on the ground 1.26 times harder while the swing time did not change. On R6, within one character, lengthen the stride and shorten the stance up to a fast run and add cadence only for the sprint, as locomotion.md, Runs, already says. Between characters, make the faster one press harder (a quicker, sharper stance dip and a shorter contact) rather than whip its swing leg through faster; keep the dip no deeper (Jiménez et al., above, found the bob unchanged with speed).
- **A sprint touches the ground for 5 frames.** Nagahara et al. (2014; 12 sprinters over 60 m) measured the shortest support time, 0.090 s, at the 20th step, a step frequency up to 4.87 Hz, a top speed of 10.04 m/s at the 23rd step and steps up to 2.14 m. On R6 a sprint step is about 12 frames with 5 to 6 contact frames, so each foot is down about 22% of the cycle. At that contact time the 2.4 stud stance plants cleanly at 25 to 27 studs/s (our derivation). pipeline.md, Locomotion and overlays, says a 25 studs/s sprint cannot plant at a natural cadence and should be built near 15 studs/s and played faster; that holds at a jog's cadence of about three steps a second. A 12-frame step with 5-frame contacts plants at 25 to 27 studs/s, but it reads as an all-out sprint: use it when that is the read wanted.
- **Sprint acceleration changes at the 4th and the 14th step.** Nagahara et al. (2014) found two shifts, at step 4.4 and step 14.1 on average. Step frequency rose until the 4th step and then held; support time fell fast to the 4th step and then slowly; until the 4th step the foot landed behind the centre of gravity and the knee did not flex during support. The centre of gravity rose fast to step 4, slowly to step 14, then held; the head angle rose until about step 15; the upper trunk reached 75.7 degrees from horizontal (about 14 from vertical) by step 25. On R6, for a dash start: steps 1 to 4 keep the stance leg at full length (no slide up after contact), land each foot behind the torso centre and raise the cadence each step; steps 5 to 14 plant ahead of the torso, start the stance slide and rise gradually; from about step 15 run the steady cycle with the head level. locomotion.md, Runs, covers the low, leaning first steps.

## Running: pelvis, chest, arms and head

- **A running pelvis turns 14 to 19 degrees and drops 14 to 17; keep most of it out of the torso.** Kovše et al. (female recreational runners at 10 to 14 km/h) measured pelvic rotation range of 14 to 19 degrees, obliquity range of 14 to 17 degrees and tilt range of about 8 to 9 degrees around 20 to 24 degrees of forward tilt; rotation and obliquity grew with speed, tilt range did not, and toe-off came near 40% of the cycle. On R6 a torso roll of 15 degrees would tilt the shoulders as much, so keep the run's torso roll near the 4.5 degree start in principles.md, Run, and let the swing leg's slide carry the drop. Show the pelvic rotation as each leg's `p` z slide of about 0.06 to 0.08 studs (our derivation: 7 to 9.5 degrees on a 0.5 stud hip radius).
- **In a run the chest turns three times as far as in a walk, and the head barely turns.** Pontzer et al. (2009) measured at 3.0 m/s: shoulder rotation 23.75 degrees (8.1 walking), head yaw 6.1 degrees, and a pelvis-to-shoulder phase difference of 94 degrees (149 walking), with a 0.74 to 0.76 s stride. On R6: torso `twist` about plus or minus 12, with its extremes about a quarter cycle (10 to 11 frames of a 42-frame cycle) away from the legs' extremes rather than on the contact frames; the source gives the size of the offset, not which part leads, so try both and compare them in the strips. Give the `Head` a `twist` of about -0.74 times the torso twist, so it turns only about plus or minus 3 (our derivation from 6.1 / 23.75). On the Roblox DevForum (2019), reviewers of an R6-style run said the near-still torso and head made it look blocky and stiff and suggested twisting the torso left and right.
- **When the arms cannot swing, the chest and head turn more.** In the same study, running with the arms restrained raised the shoulder rotation from 23.75 to 35.68 degrees and the head yaw from 6.1 to 11.3 degrees; in walking the differences were not significant. On R6, a run with the arms busy (a two-handed weapon, a carried object) gets about 1.5 times the torso twist and a stronger `Head` counter-twist to keep the eyes steady; a walk does not need the change.

## Starts, stops and turns

- **Starting a walk takes half a second of lean before the first heel lifts.** Storniolo et al. (2025; 16 trained men starting from a stand) measured the centre of pressure moving back by 46.6 mm, peaking 449 ms after it began, before the leading heel lifted; most showed a forward "diving" strategy. The backward push tips the body forward. On R6, for an NPC starting from a stand, spend about 27 frames (the time to the peak of the backward shift; the heel lifts after it) tipping the torso forward with `pz` and a small lean, together with the sideways shift onto the stance foot (body-mechanics.md, Balance and weight shift), before the first foot lifts. A player's start cannot wait that long: compress the tip to a few frames (a starting value).
- **The first braking step works hardest.** Hitchens et al. (2026; 19 team-sport athletes stopping from 5.1 to 7.1 m/s) measured early decelerations of 4.3 to 5.1 m/s^2 and peak vertical forces of 4.1 to 5.3 body weights; the authors note that the first three steps make up about half of a stop from high speed. In step 1, more hip and knee flexion went with more braking force; in the later steps, a lower centre of mass at touchdown, the foot further ahead and the shank leaning back helped most. On R6, give the first braking step the deepest leg slide, the most hip flexion and the biggest torso drop, then plant each later step further ahead of the torso with the torso lower and leaning back. locomotion.md, Starts, stops and turns, gives the number of steps and the duration.
- **A turn on the spot starts at the head, then the chest, the hips and the feet.** Khobkhun and Thanakamchokchai (2023; 50 adults turning 180 degrees in 1.5, 2 and 3 s) measured median onsets at the fast speed of 0.328 s for the head, 0.366 for the thorax, 0.430 for the pelvis, 0.459 for the leading foot and 0.540 for the trailing foot; the head led the thorax by up to 24 to 27 degrees, and the fast turn took 2.5 steps. On R6: start the `Head` `twist`, then the torso about 2 frames later, then turn the foot targets of `Feet.flat` (a target may be a function of time) about 4 frames after the torso, lift the leading foot about 2 frames after that and the trailing foot about 5 frames after the leading one; keep the head up to about 25 degrees ahead of the torso and turn 180 degrees in two or three steps.
- **A sudden turn is a step turn off the outside foot.** Tillman, Molino and Zaferiou (2023; young adults making 90 degree walking turns) found that late-cued turns were 73% step turns while pre-planned turns split evenly between step and spin turns. In step turns, the largest push toward the new direction came during the outside foot's single support; in spin turns, walkers leaned so their centre of mass was often outside the inside foot. On R6, for a turn the player triggers mid-walk, push the torso toward the new heading while the outside foot carries the weight, and step the inside foot out into the new direction; keep the spin turn (a pivot on the inside foot with a lean into the turn) for planned, flowing turns.
- **The head leads a walking turn by 10 to 20 degrees, more when the turn is a surprise.** Weiss, Moudy and Wood (2025) report that for planned 90 degree walking turns the head led the trunk by 10 to 20 degrees (citing Sreenivasa et al.), and measured a median of 38.4 degrees in their own cue-driven 90 degree turn. On R6, keep the `Head` `twist` 10 to 20 degrees ahead of the torso in a planned turn and up to about 38 degrees for a sudden, reacting turn; locomotion.md, Starts, stops and turns, has the head-first rule for run turns.

## Punches

- **A full-power straight takes 27 frames with the lead hand and 40 with the rear.** Wan and Liu (2026; 17 elite boxers) measured 0.442 s for lead and 0.667 s for rear straights from the lowest knee bend to contact, a trunk rotation range of 42.7 against 73.0 degrees, a peak trunk speed of 220 against 270 deg/s, and a slower loading turn of 91 against 105 deg/s. On R6, a full-effort lead straight runs about 27 frames and a rear straight about 40 from the load to contact; turn the torso 43 degrees for the lead and 73 for the rear, at a peak of about 3.7 and 4.5 degrees per frame, and load it at less than half that speed. A high-speed camera study of boxing and sanda athletes (Xu, Mao and Xi, 2024) measured 10.4 to 13.0 rad/s (600 to 740 deg/s) for the hip line, trunk and shoulder line in a cross, so the torso's peak speed depends on the method: 4 to 12 degrees per frame. These are human timings for an NPC power punch or a cinematic one; a player's light attack stays on the frame budgets in combat.md, Frame budgets: startup, active, recovery.
- **The fist peaks before contact and arrives about 20% slower.** Wan and Liu (2026) measured peak fist speeds of 6.76 m/s (lead) and 7.75 m/s (rear) and contact speeds of 5.24 and 6.18 m/s, a 20 to 23% loss before the hit. Xu, Mao and Xi (2024) measured 7.3 to 8.5 m/s in the cross and 9.9 to 11.0 m/s in the uppercut. On R6 the fist peaks near 19 to 24 studs/s in a straight (0.32 to 0.40 studs per frame) and up to 31 studs/s in an uppercut (0.52 per frame; our scaling). Print the fist speed by forward kinematics (SKILL.md, Measure before you look): the fastest frame comes before contact, and the contact frame keeps about 80% of that speed on an `auto` key, never a `flat` one.
- **The elbow drives the cross; the shoulder drives the hook and the uppercut.** Dinu and Louis (2020; 15 elite and 8 junior boxers) found the elbow contributing most to the cross (39% elite, 27% junior), and the shoulder most to the hook and uppercut (50 to 71%); juniors used more shoulder (29% against 16%) and trunk (16% against 7%) in the cross; hook velocity tended to be highest. On R6, put the cross's speed into the arm's piston slide over the last frames with the arm angle nearly held (principles.md, Attack or combo, 0.3 to 0.6 studs); put the hook's and uppercut's speed into the arm's swing (`lift` and `side`) with the torso. A cross that swings the whole arm from the shoulder reads like a junior's.
- **A cross moves the weight onto the lead foot; an uppercut keeps it centred.** Dinu and Louis (2020) measured the ground force split lead to rear at 61 to 39% in the cross, 59 to 41% in the hook and 50 to 50% in the uppercut (elite). Xu, Mao and Xi (2024) measured the centre of mass moving 56 to 86 mm forward, about 118 mm side to side and 43 to 47 mm up and down during a cross. Wan and Liu (2026) found each punch loading the leg on its own side harder (rear leg 1132 N in rear punches, lead leg 1005 N in lead punches). On R6, move the centre of mass (body-mechanics.md, Centre of mass on R6) about 0.16 to 0.25 studs forward, about 0.34 studs across and about 0.12 studs up and down over a cross, mostly with torso `pz`, `px` and `py`, and end with it about 60% of the way from the rear sole to the lead sole; keep an uppercut's centre of mass between the feet.
- **The rear leg is straight on the cross's fastest frame; the uppercut stays bent.** Xu, Mao and Xi (2024) measured the knee at 168 to 176 degrees (nearly straight) at peak cross speed and 152 degrees at peak uppercut speed; the shoulder angle between upper arm and trunk was 105 degrees in the cross and 64 to 65 in the uppercut. On R6, on the cross's fastest frame give the rear leg no slide (the drive is done) and the striking arm about 90 to 105 degrees of `lift` against the torso (pointing forward, slightly up); on the uppercut's fastest frame keep the legs slightly slid up (about 0.06 studs, our derivation from a 28 degree bend) with the torso still rising and the arm still low, near 65 degrees of `lift`, so it is still climbing at its fastest frame.

## Throws

- **A pitch peaks the pelvis first, the trunk second and the arm last.** Escamilla et al. (2025; 18 college pitchers) measured, on a scale from lead-foot contact (0%) to ball release (100%), a peak pelvis speed of 569 to 581 deg/s at 24 to 25% and a peak upper-trunk speed of 1088 to 1096 deg/s at 46 to 47%; elbow extension reached 2541 to 2563 deg/s and shoulder internal rotation 6415 to 6468 deg/s, all during arm cocking and acceleration. The stride was 78 to 79% of body height and the lead knee rose to 56 to 61% of body height. On R6 the torso is pelvis and chest at once: start the turn at foot contact by pivoting the rear foot's `Feet.flat` target yaw toward the target while the torso still faces back, peak the torso `twist` near half of the interval at up to about 18 degrees per frame, and put the arm's fastest frames last, just before release. A human stride scales to 3.9 studs, far more than planted R6 legs reach with flat soles (SKILL.md, Feet rule): take the stride the rule allows and sell its length with the torso's travel toward the target and its lean. For the leg kick, lift the lead leg to about 90 degrees of `lift` with the run's knee slide (0.7 to 0.95 studs).
- **Only the order pelvis, trunk, arm is reliable.** Scarborough et al. (2021; 14 pitchers, 71 curveballs and the fastballs of 12) found that no pitch followed the full five-segment order pelvis, trunk, arm, forearm, hand; the common orders kept pelvis, trunk, then arm, with the forearm and hand reversed or together, and 43 to 49% of pitches had an altered distal order. On R6 the forearm and hand live inside the Arm part, so only legs, torso, then arm must stay in order: the torso's peak strictly before the arm's. A held prop may peak with the arm.
- **A throw coils the chest 40 degrees back, keeps the hips 20 degrees ahead, then turns 60.** Labbe et al. (2025; a pilot study of 8 quarterbacks with inertial sensors) measured the trunk turned 40 degrees toward the throwing side at foot contact, reversing during the acceleration phase to 21 degrees the other way (about 60 degrees in all), a hip-to-shoulder separation of about 20 degrees, a trunk leaning 2 to 9 degrees away from the throwing arm, and a lead knee that stayed bent (29 to 46 degrees). On R6 the hips and chest are one part, so show the separation in the legs: at foot contact the planted legs' `Feet.flat` target yaws face about 20 degrees further toward the target than the torso (inside the 45 degree toe-yaw limit, SKILL.md, Toe yaw), then the torso turns about 60 degrees through release with a 2 to 9 degree roll away from the throwing arm, and the lead leg keeps its slide. principles.md, Attack or combo, has the R6 pose of a wide throw.

## Kicks

- **Speed grows down the leg: hip about 2.4 m/s, knee about 6, foot 10 to 14.** Vagner et al. (2023; systematic review) report front-kick foot speeds into a target of 7.7 m/s (novice) to 9.6 m/s (elite) and roundhouse-kick foot speeds of 6.5 (novice), 11.4 (sub-elite) and 14.3 m/s (elite), with the elite roundhouse hip at 2.4 m/s and knee at 6.7 m/s and knee extension at 1516 deg/s; a whole roundhouse took 0.84 s (elite) to 1.06 s (novice) as the review defines it. On R6 an elite roundhouse foot scales to about 41 studs/s, which a 2-stud leg reaches turning about 20 degrees per frame; a front kick near 27 studs/s needs about 13 degrees per frame (our derivation). Both stay under the 25 degrees per frame cap (principles.md, Strike speed and retiming).
- **The thigh stops so the shank can whip; on R6 the slide does the whip.** Kellis and Katis (2007; review of the instep kick) describe the thigh reaching its peak angular speed (about 516 to 573 deg/s) just before the knee starts to extend, then slowing to almost zero at ball contact while the shank and foot reach their peak; the backswing extends the hip up to 29 degrees. A rigid leg cannot slow its top while its end speeds up. On R6, back-swing the leg about 29 degrees behind the torso, chamber it by sliding it up into the torso (0.7 to 0.95 studs, the run's knee slide) as the hip `lift` swings through, then in the last 2 to 3 frames before contact slide it back to full length while the `lift` slows, so the foot gains speed along the leg as the swing eases. Never slide it past full length: the leg must touch the torso on every frame (r6-mechanics.md, Joint gaps).
- **A pivoting support foot powers the hip turn.** Razm et al. (2026; narrative review of 23 studies) found that pivoted support-leg positions, as in taekwondo, allowed more hip rotation and joint power, while non-pivoted stances limited force transfer and balance; the pivot also loads the support knee. On R6, turn the support foot's `Feet.flat` target yaw with the hips through the kick (a target may be a function of time) so the support leg's twist against the torso stays under 45 degrees (SKILL.md, Toe yaw); a support foot locked straight while the hips turn 90 degrees breaks that limit and reads as a twisted leg.
- **A round kick lands about 34 frames after it starts; a double kick's second hit 15 frames after the first.** Chen et al. (2025; 28 experienced taekwondo athletes) measured a lead-leg round kick at 560 ms from muscle onset to contact on its own and 610 ms when more kicks were to follow, a double kick's second contact 251 to 269 ms after its first, a turning kick at 885 ms, and a premotor time (from the light cue to the first muscle activity) of 149 to 191 ms. On R6: a real-speed round kick is about 34 frames to contact (37 when it opens a chain), a double kick's second hit lands 15 to 16 frames after the first, and a spinning kick about 53 frames. An NPC that reacts to a cue starts its response about 9 to 11 frames after it. These are human timings for NPCs and cutscenes; a player's kick stays on combat.md, Frame budgets: startup, active, recovery.

## Jumps

- **A countermovement drops about a fifth of body height, more than R6 legs can show.** Ho et al. (2026; 62 active adults, arms crossed) measured a countermovement depth of 0.36 m for a 0.32 m jump, 0.78 s from the start of the dip to takeoff, a 0.29 s push, a takeoff speed of 2.48 m/s, a peak downward speed of 1.43 m/s, a hip range of 85 degrees and a peak hip extension speed of 512 deg/s. On R6 the depth scales to about 1.0 stud, more than the 0.75 to 0.86 stud deep crouch that still reads (r6-mechanics.md, Planted feet on rigid legs): drop the torso about 0.8 studs and get the rest of the load from a forward torso lean and the arms swinging back. The dip falls at most about 58% of the takeoff speed (1.43 / 2.48), so drive down firmly but leave faster; at 60 fps the dip and push take about 47 frames and the push alone about 17 (body-mechanics.md, Takeoff and landing, has the phase split).
- **Swinging the arms adds height.** Spires et al. (2026; 30 recreationally active adults) measured jumps of 39.7 with arm swing against 30.7 without, about 30% higher, and cite earlier work reporting 10 to 20%. On R6 a Humanoid jump's height is set by physics, so the arms sell the power: swing them back in the dip and throw them up on the push frames (body-mechanics.md, Takeoff and landing); a jump with still arms reads weaker at the same height.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Ankle rocker and toe-off (10 degrees of plantarflexion at contact, 10 of dorsiflexion in midstance, 20 of plantarflexion at push-off; Brunnekreef et al.): there is no foot joint, and planted soles stay flat (SKILL.md, Feet rule).
- The stance-knee bend of a walk (20 degrees): a 0.03 stud slide is below what reads; the torso bob carries the give.
- Running pelvic obliquity as a torso roll (14 to 17 degrees): the single torso part would tilt the shoulders as much.
- Separation between pelvis and chest inside the body (about 20 degrees in throws, the pelvis peaking before the trunk): one torso part; only the legs' twist can show it.
- Pitching shoulder internal rotation (about 6400 deg/s) and elbow extension (about 2500 deg/s): there is no forearm, a twist about the arm's long axis barely moves the hand, and the speeds are 1.7 to 4.3 times the 25 degrees per frame cap.
- The order of forearm and hand peaks in throws: both live inside one Arm part.
- The thigh braking while the shank whips in a kick: one rigid leg; the slide in Kicks stands in.
- The foot's strike angle at contact in running (10 to 15 degrees in Jiménez et al.): no foot joint.
- Lumbar extension separate from the chest (23 degrees at the quarterback's maximal shoulder rotation, Labbe et al.): there is no spine; a whole-torso lean back swings the hips (SKILL.md, Waist rule).
- Elbow range in walking (21 to 36 degrees in walking and fast walking): there is no elbow, and a slide that small does not read.
