# R6 craft: making six parts read human

How R6 animators make six rigid blocks read as a human body: why motion carries the read, how slides and pistons stand in for knees, elbows, a hunch and toes, when a limb may leave the torso, how the head acts without a face, how far to push a pose, what R6 keeps and loses against R15, how battlegrounds games build and time their move sets, how locomotion, idle and emote sets are structured, and the tools R6 animators use. Every finding was checked against its source (sources.md, "R6 craft: making six parts read human research"). Most sources are R6 animators' posts on the Roblox DevForum, and each claim is stated as strongly as its poster stated it. Frame counts are at 60 fps; conversions from seconds are ours.

## Why six blocks can read human

- **Joint motion, not body shape, carries the human read.** A 2025 review of biological motion research (Frontiers in Psychology) restates Johansson's 1973 result: observers immediately recognise human motion from sparse point-light displays that show only the movement of the major joints. Spatially scrambled displays keep each joint's own path and still carry animacy cues, and inverted displays are rated less animate, which the review says may reflect the direction of gravity in the local motion. On R6: the six parts and the root give seven paths, and they read human when each one moves like the joint it stands for. Print the hand, foot and head-top paths with `Poser.posesAt` (SKILL.md, method step 3, "Arcs and paths") and check the two cues a viewer reads first: the arms in counter-phase to the legs, and parts that drop speeding up toward their low point while parts that rise slow toward their high point (`auto` keys through a fall, a turn-around key at the top; our reading of the gravity result). A still frame may look blocky; the paths decide whether it reads as a body.
- **Embrace the rigid parts and set each character's range of motion.** Rob Coleman, head of animation at Animal Logic on The LEGO Movie, says the team embraced the limits of hard bricks (no bending, no squash and stretch, no motion blur) to build a faux stop-motion style, and that The LEGO Batman Movie changed its animation style because Batman needed a greater range of motion than Emmet. On R6: do not fake bends with extra parts or scaling; use the substitutes in this file. Set each character's range in the clip plan before keying: an everyday character stays near the walk ranges (torso twist about plus or minus 3.5 degrees, locomotion.md, "Walk body motion") with short pistons; an agile fighter uses the range of the decoded stand strikes, whose torso twists through 110 to 124 degrees per strike (the-world-clips.md, "Recorded interpretation of these clips").
- **Sell a bend on the move, not in the held pose.** Matt Everitt, animation director on The LEGO Ninjago Movie at Animal Logic: "You can't really bend the elbows of a minifig, say, but we can make the 'feeling' of a bent elbow when you watch it on the run." Each frame was hand-crafted, and instead of motion blur the animators added extra legal bricks to exaggerate a move ("brick blur"). On R6: put the fake bend on the fastest frames. The striking arm slides 0.2 to 0.4 studs back along its own length over the 3 to 5 frames before a strike and shoots forward on the strike frames (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", no elbows or knees); a stepping leg slides up into the hip only around its passing frames. Keep the key poses on straight, joined limbs so the bend lives in the motion (our reading of the quote). The R6 stand-in for brick blur is a Trail, not an extra part (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Smears and impact frames").
- **Straight limbs are part of the R6 look.** Under a 60 fps R6 dance emote on the DevForum, a developer noted that animators usually add elbows and knees to a character that lacks them ("Minecraft animations always have those weirdly bendy limbs"), but R6 animators keep the limbs straight, "and the funny thing is... it works". On R6: never add bend parts, bent meshes or extra joints to a player character for a clip. A rig with extra joints is a custom rig (custom-rigs.md).

## Faking joints with slides and pistons

- **The knee sits at the hip: pose the leg as a shin.** An R6 developer who makes all the animations for their own games advises pretending that the R6 knee is located at the hip, and says the same applies to the arms to a degree. A procedural R6 foot-planting answer on the DevForum does the same in code: when the foot comes closer to the hip than the 2 stud leg length, displace the hip joint, which "will make it look like as if the leg is 'bending' it's knee"; its pseudo-code offsets the hip joint by up to 0.5 studs up and 0.05 forward, blended by the foot's distance over the leg length. On R6: give the leg the reference shin's angle, not the thigh's (our reading: the visible leg is the shin). In a crouch or a ready stance the legs stay near vertical while the torso drops and `Feet.flat` slides them up into it (r6-mechanics.md, "Planted feet on rigid legs", crouch depth). A stepping or kneeing leg slides up by `p = Vector3.new(0, 0.5 * k, -0.05 * k)`, with k from 0 at full reach to 1 when the foot is tucked under the hip (our mapping); a run's swing leg goes further, 0.7 to 0.95 studs (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", runs).
- **Pose the arm as the forearm when the elbow matters.** Answering a claim that R6 cannot show a punch being prepared and an elbow extending, a developer wrote that it can: "move the character's full arm based on the position of a real person (or R15 character)'s forearm", which "just requires being willing to disconnect the 'shoulder' of the arm from the torso". On R6: for a cocked hook, an uppercut load or a forearm guard, take the forearm direction from the reference, solve the arm to it (r6-mechanics.md, "Direction and rotation checks"; a raised forearm uses the up form), then slide the arm until its shoulder end rests on the torso's side or front. On the strike, turn it to the punch line and slide it home as the fist travels. A 120 degree change needs at least 5 frames under the 25 degrees per frame limit (SKILL.md, "4. Measure before you look"). Check the path from the player's camera with `_G.editStrip`. The source's open shoulder conflicts with this skill's contact rule; see "Limbs on the body: when a gap is allowed".
- **Lower the arm to put a hand at the belt.** The unofficial Moon Animator 2 guide on the DevForum (323 likes) warns R6 beginners not to raise an arm too high in a sword idle, because it then reads as lifting the sword; its fix moves the part down, with "no need to bend an R6 physically". The four decoded community R6 walks carry their arms 0.07 to 0.36 studs below rest (walk-cycles.md, "Ranges per clip"). On R6: for a hand at the hip or belt (a sheathed hilt, a relaxed guard, a hunch), slide the arm down `p.y` -0.2 to -0.35 instead of lifting it further, and keep its lift inside the decoded walks' -46 to +61 degrees. Most of the arm still overlaps the torso side, so it keeps contact.
- **Recoil is a torso turn plus an arm piston.** Reviewing an R6 firing animation, a developer suggested rotating the torso a bit and moving the arm back "a stud or two" and then forward again for a better recoil. On R6: on each shot, turn the gun shoulder back (torso twist -4 to -8 degrees for a right-hand gun) and slide the gun arm 0.2 to 0.4 studs back along its length over 2 to 3 frames, then return over 6 to 10 frames on `auto` keys with the head 1 to 2 frames behind (`lag = {Head = 2/60}`). The sizes are our starting values for a pistol; the source's "stud or two" is the upper bound for a heavy gun.
- **A hunch sinks the head and drops the arms.** Reworking an R6 walk after critique, its author lowered the arms, placed the head lower on the torso looking slightly down, exaggerated the up and down motion of the torso and arms, and slowed the clip to the character's in-game speed. For the same author's hunched animation, a reviewer asked for both arms very slightly angled into the torso and slightly lowered, which "implies more weight", and another asked for the head to look down more. On R6: for a hunched, tired or wary posture, key the head at `p = Vector3.new(0, -0.15, 0)` (0.1 to 0.2 down: the head sinks into the torso, so the shoulders read raised) with head lift -8 to -15, slide both arms down 0.1 to 0.2 with a few degrees of forward lift so the hands hang in front of the thighs, and hold these offsets through the whole clip. For sadness, use the head drop in locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk" (about 19 degrees).
- **The decoded professional clips never translate the head.** Measured on the eight decoded clips in `references/decodes/` (a stand's seven strikes and its idle, on a standard R6 rig): the Head's `p` is 0.00 on every key, so all head motion is rotation; the torso moves at most 0.46 studs on any axis and the limbs up to 1.64. The four decoded community walks agree (head y 0.00 to 0.02, walk-cycles.md, "Ranges per clip"). On R6: keep the `Head` keys' `p` at zero in combat and locomotion; turn the head and let the torso carry it. Reserve a head sink for a held posture (the hunch above), never per beat.
- **No toes: a rise onto the toes becomes a small hop.** Critiquing an R6 sword swing copied from a video, a reviewer pointed out that the reference extends her legs and rises onto her tiptoes at the end, which Roblox cannot do ("no toes"), "so a hop might work instead". On R6: at the extreme where the reference rises, lift both feet and the torso 0.15 to 0.25 studs and land into a compact pose. At the default gravity of 196.2 studs/s^2 (body-mechanics.md, "Gravity, jumps and falls on Roblox") such a hop lasts 2 x sqrt(2h / g) = 0.078 to 0.101 s, about 5 to 6 frames (our calculation). A foot target may be a function of time that returns a lift (pipeline.md, "Planted feet"):

  ```lua
  local function hop(x, z, yaw, t0, h, d)
  	return function(t)
  		local u = math.clamp((t - t0) / d, 0, 1)
  		return {x, z, yaw, 4 * h * u * (1 - u)}
  	end
  end

  post = Feet.flat({r = hop(0.5, 0, 0, 0.4, 0.2, 0.09), l = hop(-0.5, 0, 0, 0.4, 0.2, 0.09)})
  ```

  Key the torso `p.y` on the same arc (0 at 0.4 and 0.49, 0.2 at the apex, 0.445) so the legs do not slide up into it.
- **A foot that slides on purpose must leave the ground.** In critique of R6 skill animations, a reviewer said a planted leg should stay still unless the move is a jump; when the author explained that the back foot's slide was a push-off for power, the reviewer answered that if the slide is on purpose, the player should hop off the floor while sliding. On R6: a planted sole never slides (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", feet rule). For a push-off drag, raise that foot's target 0.05 to 0.1 studs with its `lift` value on the frames it travels, or keep it planted and move the torso instead.
- **A rigid leg leaves the floor on the front edge of its sole.** Josh Whitehouse (Go Chatter Studios; Best of Brickworld Chicago Film International Award, 2024) on a minifigure walk: after the leading leg moves off its stud, "balance the minifigure's back foot on the stud, while keeping the torso upright", then place the front foot on the next stud; opposite hands and legs always move in the same direction. An R6 reviewer gave a beginner whose walk looked like "a sad person is moonwalking on ice" the same reference: for R6, "the best practice is lego stop motion". On R6: a rigid leg swung back rests on the front edge of its sole. In the last 3 to 4 frames of a stance, return nil from that foot's `Feet.flat` target function so the keyed leg shows (key it to match the solved pose on the hand-over frame, read with `Poser.posesAt`, so it does not pop), and key it swinging back with its lowest corner on the floor (`_G.feet` prints the lowest sole corner; r6-mechanics.md, "Planted feet on rigid legs"), while the torso stays upright (lift within the walk's plus or minus 2 degrees, locomotion.md, "Walk body motion").

## Limbs on the body: when a gap is allowed

- **R6 animators split on detached limbs; a player character keeps contact.** Some R6 animators detach limbs on purpose: one says limb detachment "is only way I know how to make good animation for r6"; another calls detaching a limb "a good option when exaggerating"; a third says that in almost any game with R6 animations the arms "99% of the time will disconnect from the torso at some point", because with flat colours seen from one angle an arm must be separated for the player to see how it is posed. Reviewers in the same threads push back: "quite a lot of obvious limb detatchment", "the arms are too far off the body", and, under a showcase set of four M1s with an uppercut and a downslam, "Pretty good for anything except actual gameplay. To make it compatible and gameplay friendly, I would not disconnect the limbs and drag them too far away from the torso." This conflicts with the skill's contact rule (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", feet rule; r6-mechanics.md, "Joint gaps"), which stays the rule for player characters, because other players see them from every side. A gap is allowed on a floating stand or companion (the-world-clips.md, "Recorded interpretation of these clips", limbs float) and in a cut-scene seen from one fixed camera, where one reviewer's fix was to move the camera to hide the floating arm.
- **Make an arm read by its pose, not by daylight at the shoulder.** The reason given for detaching (flat colours, an arm lost against the torso) is a staging problem. On R6: swing the arm so it sits outside the torso's outline from the player's camera with a visible gap between the arm and the torso, while its top still touches the torso (SKILL.md, "Appeal" row; principles.md, "Poses first: the posing checklist", negative space). Measure the shoulder contact per frame before the first strip (r6-mechanics.md, "Joint gaps").

## The head and acting without a face

- **Look, look, look, then act.** For The LEGO Ninjago Movie, Animal Logic studied how Jackie Chan, in the middle of a fight, "has an idea, looks at an object, looks at an opponent, looks back at the object and then figures out how to use the object", and applied that to its scenes. On R6 the default head has no face rig, so the head turn is the eye-line. Key `Head` twist and lift to the object, to the opponent and back to the object, each look a quick turn followed by a hold that keeps drifting (principles.md, "Moving holds and settles"), and start the body's action only after the last look. A turn of 4 to 6 frames and a hold of 10 to 20 frames are our starting values.
- **Animate on a head with a face.** A reviewer of R6 walks advised that it is "much easier to animate if you use a ROBLOX avatar with the standard ROBLOX head so you can see exactly what's happening". On R6: keep the face decal on while judging acting and head direction in strips and captures; remove it only for the silhouette check (SKILL.md, "Appeal" row).
- **Do not stare at the floor in a walk.** Feedback on a first R6 walk named its faults as "bouncy horror look at the floor syndrome" and "a sad person is moonwalking on ice". On R6: in a neutral walk keep the head lift inside the decoded walks' -4 to +8 degrees (walk-cycles.md, "Ranges per clip") and the bob at the measured walk size (torso `py` range about 0.14 studs, locomotion.md, "Walk body motion"), and plant the soles with `Feet.flat` so nothing slides.

## How far to push on six parts

- **Push the torso and the timing, not the limb lift.** R6 animators call for exaggeration: one ties it to rigs with fewer joints than a human and to common practice in Bionicle stop-motion. But over-lifted limbs read wrong: "any R6 walk animation that raises the legs above the waist like that instead of just doing something like the normal 'swinging' motion just looks very weird" (a personal opinion with 13 likes); a sword-idle arm raised too high reads as lifting the sword (the Moon Animator 2 guide); and walk legs that "jutt extremely far out" read as a crouch. On R6: put the exaggeration into torso twist, lean and drop and into the timing contrast; keep walk legs inside the four decoded walks' -54 to +51 degrees (walk-cycles.md, "Ranges per clip") and under the torso (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", feet rule).
- **One style per clip: a single-frame jump in smooth motion reads as an error.** In feedback on a beginner's dive animation, a reviewer pointed out that at 0:05 to 0:06 "the arms seems to move in just about 1 frame while the other animations are very smooth", and said a 1-frame move can depict a style (like stop motion) but looks odd here. On R6: choose smooth (spline, springs, `life`) or stepped (`e = "step"` keys with a hold list, stylized-and-roblox.md, "Stepped anime timing") for the whole clip. In a smooth clip, give a 90 degree change at least 4 frames (25 degrees per frame, SKILL.md, "4. Measure before you look").
- **A crouch walk holds the arms and tucks the legs.** Advice on an R6 crouch walk: the torso's "Local-X orientation" should be lower, the legs "more huddled in to the chest", the head angled up from the torso or down, and "keep the arms at a fixed position rather then moving them with the legs". On R6: lean the torso forward 15 to 25 degrees from the waist (our reading of "Local-X lower"; `waist`, r6-mechanics.md, "Lean from the waist"), drop it so `Feet.flat` slides the legs up into it, hold the arms in one guard pose with only `life` drift, and take the cycle numbers from custom-rigs.md, "A crouch walk".

## R6 against R15

- **R15 needs its middle joints; R6 motion does not.** A reviewer of a stiff R15 walk advised using all the joints, "instead of just the ones connected to the torso" (knees and elbows), to bring flow. Going the other way, replies in another thread say R15 games that want the R6 look animate only the UpperArm to mimic the whole R6 arm, or convert R6 animations with a plugin. One reviewer said a crouching R6 walk "would be hell to animate with R15", citing overlapping parts and fluidity "quite hard to replicate compared to R6". On R6: an R15 reference with bent elbows and knees maps onto R6 through the shin and forearm rules above, not by copying the upper-segment angles. Drop R15 clips as direct sources for an R6 rig (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference", step 3).
- **Battlegrounds games pick R6 for one consistent body.** Asked whether The Strongest Battlegrounds and Deepwoken run R15 rigs that look like R6, one developer was "fairly certain" that both games use R6 and another confirmed it for The Strongest Battlegrounds in game; the first added that R6's collision and weight are the same whatever bundle a player wears, and that R6 suits a game that must be completely fair. On R6: every player shares one silhouette and hitbox, so the poses and the timing carry the character's whole identity; author on a clean R6 rig and apply avatars on spawn (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays").
- **Roblox's own R15 content does not serve R6.** Roblox's emote pilot FAQ says R15 emotes with additional joints "will technically play back" on R6 avatars, but "the result won't look correct. R6 support is on our future roadmap." Animation Capture - Body, the editor's video capture, generates animations for R15 rigs. On R6: catalog emotes and capture output are not R6 sources; author R6 sets by hand from references, or copy a video with video-rotoscope.md.

## How battlegrounds sets are built and timed

- **A battlegrounds M1 set: four hits with three endings.** The Jujutsu Shenanigans fan wiki documents its M1: startup 0.16 to 0.23 s, often 0.2 or 0.23 s; a landed M1 stuns about 0.75 s and pulls the attacker about 1 stud toward the target; a blocked M1 has double the endlag; up to four hits chain (3 + 3 + 4 + 4 damage), then 2 s of downtime. The fourth hit ragdolls and has three variants by vertical movement: neutral (knockback, the shortest ragdoll), uppercut when rising or holding jump, and downslam when falling (unblockable). A player's summary of battlegrounds games on the DevForum gives the same shape (four M1s, the fourth ragdolls, a downslam from jump then M1, an uppercut from holding jump), and an R6 animator's showcase set holds "4 m1s an uppercut and a down slam". On R6: author M1_1 to M1_4 with contacts on frames 10 to 14 (our conversion), each ending in the next one's load (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", combo flow), plus an uppercut and a downslam as alternative fourth hits. Drive the 1 stud advance per landed hit from a root travel curve (pipeline.md, "Networked actions, hits and knockback", root travel from the clip). These contacts are later than the fighting-game light attack in combat.md, "Frame budgets: startup, active, recovery" (contact on frame 2 to 7) and than SKILL.md's 1 to 8 frame player anticipation: match the game's own hit time.
- **The dash set: a travelling jab forward, quick-steps to the side.** In Jujutsu Shenanigans the forward dash slides about 25 studs while winding up a jab, then punches (6 s cooldown); side and back dashes are quick-steps of about 17.5 studs that the player can steer by turning and can use during the startup of an M1 or ability (2 s cooldown). The DevForum summary lists front, side and back dashes, with front and back sharing a cooldown. On R6: the forward dash is one clip whose travel pose already holds the cocked jab (torso twisted away, arm slid back) and whose strike lands at the end of the travel; a side or back quick-step is a short push, a lean into the travel and a braced stop (SKILL.md, method step 3, "Push before speed, stopper before stop"), short enough to sit over an M1 startup.
- **A short combo keeps the feet planted; the free arm guards or loads.** A commission animator explained an R6 three-hit M1 combo: the legs stay in place because switching them would happen too often in only three hits, and with five or more hits the legs would switch; the non-punching arm "is either guarding the head or charging up for the next punch", and the punches are fast "because it's for an anime game". Another reviewer asked for faster punch startup on basic mouse-click attacks. On R6: in a three or four hit set keep both `Feet.flat` targets fixed and take the reach from torso twist; in a chain of five or more, switch the lead foot. Keep the free arm in a guard near the head or already loading the next punch.
- **Ultimates use a camera part animated with the rig.** A VFX artist's breakdown of a recreated "Serious Punch" move welds a part to the player's HumanoidRootPart, animates it in Moon Animator 2 as the camera (its AttachToPart property previews it), exports it with the animation, and sets the client camera to that part every frame; AutoRotate must be off during the cut-scene or shift lock spins the camera. A reviewer of the move asked for "more acceleration coming into" the punch. On R6: a camera part on its own Motor6D is one more Poser joint, keyed like a prop; following it with the client camera is game code, and SKILL.md asks before any camera takeover. Bring the fist into the contact at rising speed, never easing into it (SKILL.md, "Spacing, slow in and slow out" row).
- **Classic-style R6 games ask for simple, readable, responsive motion.** A developer hiring for a city game modelled on 2013 to 2014 Roblox asked for R6 work "that feels simple, readable, and responsive rather than overly realistic". On R6: for a classic style use fewer key poses, short anticipation and quick recoveries, readable from the player's camera, and keep the motion layers (spline, key offsets, `life`) that make simple poses feel alive.

## Locomotion, idle and emote sets

- **A locomotion set has seven states and hard limits.** Roblox's Marketplace animation packs (R15) cover seven locomotion states: Idle, Walk, Run, Jump, Fall, Climb and Swim (with a Swim Idle), up to two weighted idle clips, 8 to 9 clips in all, and every clip loops except Jump. Upload validation rejects a clip that moves the root joint too far from its start, exceeds a maximum frame-to-frame speed, translates a body part more than 1.5 studs from its starting position in any frame, or runs longer than 10 seconds. On R6: build an R6 set with the same states and the same limits. The slides in this file (0.2 to 0.6 stud pistons, leg slides under 1 stud) fit inside 1.5 studs; loops end on their first pose; Jump is a one-shot.
- **A combat set pairs every attack with its own reaction; a movement set adds transitions.** BIue_k's "[R6] High-Tier Combat System Animation Pack" on the Creator Store holds 14 clips: attacks (Light Punch 1, 2 and 3, Heavy Punch, Light and Heavy Throw), reactions (a Light Punch Reaction for each of the three light punches, a Heavy Punch Reaction, Knocked Floor, Flying and Flying Recovery) and a Block. The same creator's "[R6] High-Tier Movement Animation Pack" holds 9: Idle, Walk, Run, Jump, Fall, Crouch Idle and Crouch Walk, plus the one-shots Land and Crouch Transition. On R6: plan a combat set as attack and reaction pairs, the reaction keyed from the attack's contact frame (combat.md, "The victim on contact"), with the knockdown states (on the floor, flying, recovering) as their own clips; plan a movement set with the transitions between its loops (land, crouch in and out) as one-shots that start and end on the loops' frames.
- **Idle variations play by weight.** In the default Animate script Idle has two variations, and each plays with probability weight / total weight (weights 5 and 10 play one third and two thirds of the time). On R6: author a main idle and a rarer variation that starts and ends on the main idle's frame, and set the weights so the variation shows about a third of the time or less (macro idle variants, keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing").
- **R6 emotes are 5 to 10 second copies of dance videos.** The team behind the game Noob Mayhem, hiring an R6 emote animator, supplied YouTube dance references and asked for 5 to 10 second emotes "in the form of an R6 roblox animation". On R6: copy the dance from the reference video (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference"; video-rotoscope.md for an exact copy), place the beats on the music, and loop on the first pose.

## Tools and workflow

- **Moon Animator 2: mirror, looper, time stretch and onion skins.** The unofficial Moon Animator 2 guide lists Mirror (for walks and left or right-handed actions), Looper (walk cycles), Time Stretch, Wiggler (two keyframes vibrating on waves) and grouped keyframes for pauses, and suggests spamming linear keys at 3 to 10 frames per second to imitate a graph editor for arcs, fake physics and ragdolls. Its CFrame and scale tracks do not export as Roblox animations; they need scripts. Its onion skin shows neighbouring poses but does not move the rig; travel comes from keys on the root. On R6 in Poser: `warp` is the time stretch, `life` the nearest thing to the Wiggler (slow noise, not a vibration), `Poser.bake` the dense linear export (pipeline.md, "Baking and export"). Poser has no mirror helper: mirror a key by swapping Left and Right joints and writing `{lift, -twist, -side}` with `p.x` negated, the convention `Rig.solveLeft` uses in WeaponRig.lua; mirroring a whole clip needs new runtime code.
- **Animation Editor traps on R6.** Moving an R6 torso down for a crouch in the Animation Editor moved the whole grid; the poster's fix came from a reply pointing to anchoring the HumanoidRootPart. In 2026 the Adaptive Animations beta stopped the R6 Torso from rotating or moving in the editor until the beta was turned off, and R6 animations imported from Blender failed with "Torso has animation data but isn't moving" or played stiff with limbs not moving (reported May to July 2026, unresolved in those threads). On R6: after `Poser.bake`, play the KeyframeSequence on a clean R6 rig in Studio and check that the Torso pose changes and every limb moves before delivery.
- **Blender R6 rigs add IK, torso influence and onion skins.** Under a free R6 run made in Blender, its animator found Studio easier but Blender "way higher" in quality, and another developer recommended Moon Animator for higher quality inside Studio. Aeresei's R6 IK + FK Blender rig (363 likes on its DevForum post) blends IK and FK per limb, has a torso-influence setting so the limbs can ignore torso motion, a head that can follow a target, grab points, and three torso controls (UpperTorso, Spine, LowerTorso) that drive the single torso, whose pivot sits slightly lower than Roblox's. It needs Cautioned's Blender Animations plugin, and its IK and FK exist only in Blender; the Studio rig is a normal R6. Another R6 IK rig toggles leg IK and ships an onion-skin add-on. A 2026 Studio plugin, Apex Animator, plants feet and hands while the body moves, lets an arm stretch to reach, and bakes everything into ordinary joint keys. On R6: torso influence 0 on the legs is what `Feet.flat` does after the torso is final; an arm that stretches to reach is an arm slide, which must keep torso contact; the lower torso pivot is `waist` (r6-mechanics.md, "Lean from the waist").

## Does not transfer to R6

- Bendy limbs (the Minecraft style) and middle joints: an R6 player character has six rigid parts; extra joints make a custom rig.
- Squash and stretch by scaling: parts cannot scale in a clip, and Moon Animator scale tracks do not export (SKILL.md, "Squash and stretch" row).
- Brick blur (extra pieces added for one frame): a clip cannot add body parts; the substitute is a Trail (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Smears and impact frames").
- Rising onto the toes or rolling through the foot: R6 has no toes or ankles; the substitutes are a small hop and a leg that tips onto its sole's front edge.
- Facial acting: the default R6 head has no face rig; eye-lines become head turns.
- A wrist flick or a finger pose: the R6 hand is the end of the arm block; only the arm's twist turns the hand face.
- Video capture to animation (Animation Capture - Body): R15 rigs only.
- A detached limb on a player character: other players see it from every side (see "Limbs on the body: when a gap is allowed").
