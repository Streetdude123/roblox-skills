# Game animation direction

How lead game animators direct a character's whole animation set rather than one clip: when response wins over weight and for which actions, which clips a set needs and how they join, how one weight and one style hold across the set, what reads from the gameplay camera, how animation, design, effects and sound share one clock, and where the time goes under a deadline. Each finding is translated to R6 with Poser. Every finding was checked against its source (sources.md, "Game animation direction research"). Frame counts are at 60 fps unless a source states another rate; the conversions are ours.

## Responsiveness against weight

- **Reaching the new action fast matters more than natural motion.** Normoyle and Jörg (Motion in Games 2014) tested player controllers in a platform game. "Switch" changed motion in about 0.03 s; "Blend" crossfaded over 10 frames at 30 fps (0.31 to 0.37 s per transition); "MoveTree", built from captured transitions, took 1.3 s from stand to jog and 1.41 s to turn 90 degrees. The least responsive controller hurt players' performance and their sense of control: Switch players reached the goal in significantly less time and lost fewer lives than MoveTree players. Outside viewers rated MoveTree the most natural, yet players did not report better motion quality with it. The authors conclude that responsiveness "should always be prioritized over naturalness", while the animation still changes enjoyment, and note that scaling the turn clips' playback speed or allowing transitions out of transitions would have made MoveTree faster. On R6: the body must show the new action within the Switch to Blend range, about 2 to 22 frames after the press. Poser's blend sizes (`fadeIn` 0.08 to 0.2 s, pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract") sit inside it. Never make the player wait for a start, turn or stop clip to end: the root moves from the first frame, and a new `Rig:play` may replace a clip mid-transition because the inertial blend starts from the pose on screen. When a start or turn clip feels slow, raise its `speed` in `play` before you cut its frames.

- **Rank every action by how fast it must answer.** Stellar Blade's director Kim Hyung-tae told 4Gamer (reported by VGC, April 2024) that its parry and dodge appear about 0.5 s after the press. The skill activates on the press, but the movement follows, because instant movement "would not fit the game's style of animation". The day-one patch made "the input time for basic attacks, as well as the timing of blocking and parrying" more responsive. Jonathan Cooper (gameanim.com, "Basics: Fast vs Slow", 2005) puts the player character's feel before the look of its animations, cites Devil May Cry's instant reactions and Resident Evil's deliberate unresponsiveness as both right for their games, and says control "should be tempered by an appropriate dose of unresponsiveness within the context of the project". Richard Lico (a Bungie animation lead on Destiny, gameanim.com interview, 2017) says reactive gameplay needs an anticipation that "is mostly just a pose with a short blend duration", and that a game punch is more about its follow-through, because "'What happened' tells the story of your previous button press". The Aztez developers count 4 to 6 frames of anticipation on average for Bayonetta's opening attacks, before 3 to 30 frames of attack (frame rate not stated). On R6: sort the set into tiers before keying. Block, parry, dodge, dash and M1 answer first: a first pose equal to the pose on screen, `fadeIn` 0 to 0.05, and a readable pose within 1 to 4 frames (our starting values). Heavies and skills may load longer (combat.md, "Frame budgets: startup, active, recovery"). Only ultimates, emotes and finishers may hold the player. The weight goes after the press (principles.md, "Game feel: anticipation, contact, hitstop, recovery, cancel").

- **Cancel once the action has done its job, and layer weight over the response instead of in its way.** Peyman Massoudi (Game Developer blog, on Shadow Blade: Reload) writes that "when you want to have responsiveness, you have to kill some naturalness". A grappling move "can do its job in its 50% of length", so every cancellable clip gets a safe blend interval from which run, jump or dash may cancel it; players cancel attacks once the enemy is dead. A landing never interrupts: the fall blends straight into the run or an attack while an additive land clip shows the momentum in the upper body (the standing idle has its own land clip), and an additive "windmill" on the spine and hands plays when the character stops and starts in quick succession. On R6: put the `cancel` event where the move's job is done (the contact, the release of a throw), often near half the clip, and keep authoring the settle after it (combat.md, "Cancels and the first actionable frame"). Poser plays one clip per rig, so an additive landing over the run needs a composite clip (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays": torso offset at the waist, arms from the land, legs from the run) or new runtime code that adds an offset clip to the playing one.

## Designing the animation set

- **Plan the set from the states that overlap, not from a move list.** Lico on Destiny: the third-person player animations had to look good "while simultaneously aiming up 90 degrees, crouch-running diagonally back & left at a speed in between run & walk, blending out of a grenade throw, while firing, and starting a melee. All while remaining instantly responsive, yet fluid in motion". It took years and many small solutions. On R6: list every pair of states the place lets overlap (M1 while walking, block while strafing, a skill during a dash, an emote cancelled by a hit) and decide for each pair which clip owns which joints. Build each pair as a composite (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays") and test the pairs in Play, not the clips alone. A pair that cannot be composed on R6 (an attack twist over walking legs, stylized-and-roblox.md, "Level of detail and masks") gets its own moving variant.

- **Many rough clips blending together beat one beautiful clip.** Cooper ("The 12 Principles of Animation (In Video Games)", gameanim.com, 2019): keeping work "in a pose-to-pose or unfinished state as long as possible" wastes less and lets the animator rough out more animations in the same time; "many animations blending together creates a better and more fluid game character than a single beautifully animated animation". On R6: block every clip of the set in whole poses on `e = "step"` keys first and play the whole set in the place (keyframe-workflow.md, "Block in whole poses"). Polish in screen-time order only after the set plays end to end (polish-and-critique.md, "Game polish: priorities, blends and loops").

- **Inertia decides the turn, stop and lean clips.** Cooper ("Basics: Making Character-Driving Fun", 2005) separates desired speed from actual speed. Inertia delays the run after a full stick push and the stop after a release. It creates room for a "turn 180", a "stop" and a "run-lean" animation. Deceleration "will typically be required to be faster than acceleration", because "players do not want to die running off cliffs", and a turn-rate limit in degrees per second gives the feel of steering. Twitch games such as Ninja Gaiden and Devil May Cry would suffer greatly from too much inertia, while the Mario series "thrives on it". His Mario 64 case study credits that game with the first 90 and 180 degree turns and turn-leaning, and in his opinion Mario Sunshine, with its immediate 180 degree turn and no loss of speed, "suffered all the more for it". On R6: read the place's movement first (WalkSpeed, any acceleration or turn-rate code). A battlegrounds place keeps near-instant control, so its turns and stops are short overlays that never hold the root (locomotion.md, "Starts, stops and turns", for the numbers). A slower adventure or heavy-character place can afford a 180 degree turn clip, a braking stop and a lean blended by turn rate; make the stop shorter than the start.

- **The camera's facing mode decides the locomotion clips.** In Roblox's player scripts (BaseCamera and MouseLockController, Roblox-Client-Tracker), mouse lock in third person (shift lock) sets `Enum.RotationType.CameraRelative`, so the body "continuously aligns with the camera's look direction". It also offsets the camera by `Vector3.new(1.75, 0, 0)`. The default `MovementRelative` turns the body to face its movement (creator-docs, RotationType). On R6: a place that allows shift lock or forces camera-relative facing shows strafes and backpedals constantly, while the R6 Animate script plays its one walk clip at every speed above 0.01 and has no strafe clips (r6-craft.md, "Locomotion, idle and emote sets"), so a sideways move plays the forward walk sideways (our inference). Build left and right strafe cycles and a backpedal there (locomotion.md, "Keeping feet planted across speeds and directions"). A place with only movement-relative facing spends that effort on turn and stop clips instead.

- **The neutral idle shows the character; the battle idle shows what it can do next.** David Gibson (Blizzard, GDC 2016 talk on Overwatch's Mei, in an Uppsala University summary): "the idle of the characters needs to convey their personality on top of their movement speed and function in-game". Lico (80.lv, 2017): "making the idle calm and subtle helps to balance the rhythm" of the run. On R6: author two idles when the place has a combat state. The neutral idle (hubs, lobbies, menus) carries the personality: posture, breathing rhythm, a signature fidget. The battle idle (weapon out, or R6's toolnone state, r6-craft.md, "Locomotion, idle and emote sets") stays calm and low in amplitude and never looks aggressive (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Rhythm, idle and readability in action games"). Overwatch differs: Blizzard's Overwatch 2 team writes that most heroes "are poised to attack in their idle pose" (Cassidy, Reaper), in a shooter where the idle is always a combat idle; Wuyang's fix, a staff held to the side that moves to the attack inside the primary fire, is in polish-and-critique.md, "Line of action, silhouette and reversals". In a melee set where every move starts from the idle, keep the battle idle neutral. Keep its largest joint difference to every move's first key under about 80 degrees, so the pose-distance blend in pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract" (distance / 1000 deg/s), stays at its 0.08 s floor; measure it with `Poser.posesAt` and pull any outlier toward the idle.

## Weight class and character identity

- **Give each character an archetype and fix its timing rules early.** Cooper ("Basics: Fast vs Slow"): games build on three archetypes, "Slow but strong, fast but weak, and an average of the two", and speed attributes are "directly affected by the animation". Split each move into anticipation, apex and recoil, and "create a set of timing 'rules' early in the prototyping phase, ie. average PC punch or kick anticipation, around which additional animations can be balanced as fast or slow". Sakurai (GDC 2008, in Cooper's notes) found that turning each move's four stages into numbers "greatly aided the designers". On R6: write the class table before the first clip and keep it in the clip plan. Our starting values sit inside the battlegrounds M1 range (r6-craft.md, "How battlegrounds sets are built and timed"). Light class M1 contact on frame 10, cancel 8 frames after contact, landing settle 6 to 8 frames; medium contact 12, cancel 10 to 12 after, settle 10; heavy contact 14, cancel 14 to 16 after, settle 14 to 16. Store each clip's contact and cancel as `events` and let the game config read them (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays", one set of event times).

- **Weight is part of the character and must agree everywhere.** Sakurai: parameters, motions and "character essence" must all be consistent, for example the weights of Mario and Samus make them fall differently. On R6: a class's stats and its clips must tell the same story. If the place gives a heavy class a lower jump or a faster fall, its jump and land clips use the heavier landing tier for the same drop (principles.md, "Locomotion controller"), its hit reactions move less, and its knockback curves travel less. For mass in the motion itself (slow starts, heavy limbs, slowed detail), use the body types research (body-types-and-scale.md, drafted in this run, "Giants and small bodies: mass, momentum and detail").

- **Contrast the player's speed with the enemies'.** Massoudi: the player character Kuro is very agile, but the enemies' "animations have much more frames", and slower enemy animations help "to get the focus out from the player" when the eye settles on one enemy. On R6: give player clips the light or medium class and NPC grunts the heavy class with longer telegraphs (combat.md, "Telegraphs and reaction time"). Rank the clips by peak joint speed across player and NPC sets together (anime-craft.md, "Contrast across a scene"); the player's attacks should sit at the top.

- **A move's shape follows the character's role, not the move's type.** Blizzard's Overwatch 2 animation team ("Weekly Recall: Animation Station") first decides with narrative designers "what kind of movements would fit that Hero's history and personality": "if we put Soldier: 76's well-trained, stiff gestures on a Hero like Mercy, it wouldn't make sense". For Wuyang's slam, "the dramatic over-the-head pose Reinhardt takes also didn't fit Wuyang's agile style", and the team "added a spin into the third-person animation to reflect Wuyang's swift battle style and his support Hero mindset": "Reinhardt wouldn't care about quickly repositioning to avoid another hit", while Wuyang "would likely focus on casting an ability quickly and dodging a retaliatory attack". If the team "isn't feeling it, we go back to the drawing board". On R6: give each class its own version of a shared move type. A heavy class's slam rises overhead and stays down on contact (polish-and-critique.md, "Contact, weight and impact"). A light or support class's slam arcs over the shoulder and turns its follow-through into a step away: a RootJoint yaw of 180 to 360 degrees over 18 to 24 frames that ends facing the target, ready to dodge (our starting values). In an upright pose the COM sits on the torso's vertical axis, 0.64 studs below its centre, so a pure yaw leaves it in place (our derivation from SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", centre of mass).

- **The walk is part of the character's identity.** Matthew Boehm (Blizzard, GDC 2017, reported by Inven Global): "each character walks differently", "to better convey the character's personality and physical traits"; Genji "walks at a faster pace than other heroes, but his upper body remains calm and unshaken", and Junkrat "constantly limps" on his artificial leg. Riot's clarity article (2021): Lee Sin's generic shape means "a lot of his silhouette is carried by his animations", and Ivern's "goofy walk also adds to his recognizability", although animation is ideally "more of a bonus differentiator rather than a main one". On R6: every R6 body shares one shape, so animation becomes the main differentiator and the walk carries the identity. Change at least one measurable channel per character: cadence, torso bob, torso roll, arm swing range, lean (locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk"). A Genji-like walk takes a quick cadence under a quiet torso (low `life`, small arm swing, head held level). A limp gives one leg a shorter stance (body-types-and-scale.md, drafted in this run, "Fatigue and limps").

## One style across the set

- **Write a style sheet for each character.** Cooper's notes on a Pixar talk (Andy Schmidt, Ratatouille, 2007): each character got "a signature movement style to differentiate themselves from one another". "Pose character sheets were devised to illustrate various correct-vs-incorrect methods for animating the characters – essentially style sheets setting a brand bible for each character". Cooper ("The Game Animation Test", 2016): new hires "are required to be consistent with the rest of the team's animation": how far a studio exaggerates mocap or keeps it ultra-realistic, and whether keyed poses "are popping and jumping between them or keeping everything fluid with lots of overlap"; "Much of the style comes from time-constraints". On R6: the sheet is numbers plus captures. Record the golden poses as joint values, the push level (keyframe-workflow.md, "Plan before keying", the style dial), the hold style (moving-hold drift), the `lag` and `springs` presets per joint, the `life` level, the peak speed of ordinary moves against signature moves, and two `rear34` captures per pose: one right, one wrong. Check every new clip against the sheet before review.

- **Compare poses across the set from one fixed side view.** Sakurai showed each attack's four stages to his animators and designers with side-on photographs of poseable "Microman" figures, dictated every pose, and left the final quality to the animator; enforcing the poses this way meant fewer animation changes for balancing. On R6: lay out the stage poses of every attack in the set with `_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "side"})` at the same spacing and camera, one strip per clip, and compare them side by side. Two attacks whose strips look alike read as one move (principles.md, "Game feel: anticipation, contact, hitstop, recovery, cancel", one clip per state).

- **Check the whole set by script, not clip by clip.** JC Delannoy (EA, 2016) advises fixing animation data problems "proactively and thoroughly" with verification and fixup scripts that can re-check every asset, since a fix-them-as-they-come approach becomes a long game of "whack-a-mole". On R6: keep one Studio script that loops over the set and prints, per clip, `Poser.check(clip).text`, the `_G.feet` numbers, the largest joint difference between its first and last pose and the idle (from `Poser.posesAt`), and its peak joint speed. Re-run it after every batch of changes. The script is new code; the helpers exist.

## Readability from the gameplay camera

- **Know what the default camera shows.** Roblox's BaseCamera starts 12.5 studs from the subject, aims 1.5 studs above an R6 HumanoidRootPart and limits pitch to 80 degrees up or down; `Camera.FieldOfView` is vertical and defaults to 70. Shift lock moves the camera 1.75 studs to the right (MouseLockController). Our derivation: at 12.5 studs the view is 17.5 studs tall, so the 5-stud R6 body fills about 29% of the screen height, about 62 pixels per stud on a 1080-pixel screen; shift lock looks at the back from about 8 degrees to its right. A target 40 studs ahead is about 52.5 studs from the camera: about 73 pixels tall, 15 pixels per stud. On R6: at combat range only whole-body shapes read: torso lean and twist, an arm clear of the torso outline, the leg split. A 10-degree wrist or head move does not. A thrust along the facing reads from behind only as the arm shortening, so give strikes a sideways or vertical arc in the screen plane, and give the load a shoulder-line change of 30 degrees or more (our starting value). Capture at 12.5 studs in Play and at a 40-stud distance before calling a move readable.

- **A pose needs 10 to 16 frames to be seen.** Lico (80.lv, 2017): "The way I approach readability is to balance the amount of motion with how extreme my posing needs to be"; it takes "the average person 5 to 8 frames at 30fps to really 'see' a pose". But people "can easily feel a subtle motion consisting of as little as 2 frames". For overt moves such as Destiny's supers he keeps the actions quick and smooth "while 2 or 3 strong key poses leverage a moving hold with a jitter for emphasis". On R6: at 60 fps, a pose the player must recognise (a telegraph, a finisher's golden pose, a taunt) holds 10 to 16 frames as a moving hold; a motion only felt (the strike itself, a flinch) may last 4 frames. A signature move gets 2 or 3 such held poses, each with a small shake on its hold. This is longer than the 6 to 12 frame recovery hold in SKILL.md (method step 3, "Recoveries"): keep 6 to 12 on chainable moves, where response matters more than recognition, and 10 to 16 where the pose must be read.

- **The pose shows identity and direction at a glance.** Riot (clarity article, 2021): "Silhouettes are the single most important thing for champion recognition in League"; silhouette size hints at playstyle ("larger champions with bulky armor are probably tanks or fighters"); "it should be clear which direction a champion is facing from their silhouette alone. You should know whether someone is charging towards you or away at a glance". On R6: the parts' sizes never change, so the pose carries what a silhouette would. Give each class a stance it returns to (a heavy class wide and low, legs slid out to the feet-rule limit and the torso dropped; a light class narrow and high). Make a charge read from the front: torso pitched forward 15 to 20 degrees with the head held toward the target (our starting values). Check opponents' views with `_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "front34"})` as well as `rear34`.

- **Attention follows importance, and the hit area follows the visual.** Riot: "the amount of attention an ability grabs should match its level of importance" (spells that do more damage draw more attention). Visual noise "adds up quickly and can make it difficult to react properly in teamfights". Hit areas that do not line up with their effects frustrate players. Taric's stun reads through "an audio wind-up" and "a well-defined hitbox", and "Projectiles should convey the direction they're traveling, even without motion". Cosmetic animations must not give an advantage: Pool Party Renekton's recall moves his model upward while "his hitbox never changes". On R6: rank the set's telegraphs so the anticipation size follows the threat: M1 smallest, skills larger, ultimates largest (Cooper, 2019, also ties longer anticipation to bigger damage as risk against reward). At contact, put the striking fist or blade tip at the hitbox's edge (print it from `Poser.posesAt` against the server's hitbox size). A skin or emote variant keeps the original's event times and contact positions, and never carries the body away from the HumanoidRootPart.

- **An enemy attack has five phases with minimum lengths.** Jerry Zhang (Game Developer blog, 2022, on Dark Souls 3) splits an enemy attack into Opening Pose, Attack Signal, Attack, End Pose and Return. The opening pose's "silhouette should be discernable and naturally hints the incoming attack". Attack Signal and Attack together "need to have a guaranteed duration of at least 340ms", longer for a move the player has not met yet. An End Pose must last at least 170 ms "for the player to confirm the end of this attack", or 240 ms when it baits. The hitbox is active "only 2-4 frames". He measured the game's input response at about 60 to 100 ms. On R6: in an NPC attack, hold the opening pose as a moving hold. Give at least 21 frames from the start of the signal to the end of the active window. Hold the end pose at least 11 frames (15 for a feint) before the return, and keep the hit active 2 to 4 frames. This sits inside the 26 frames from clip start to contact in combat.md, "Telegraphs and reaction time".

- **A wind-up has a ceiling, and the hit pose is the one players see longest.** Sakurai (GDC 2008, Super Smash Bros. Brawl, in Cooper's notes) breaks every attack into four parts. StandBy is "essentially the first frame" (the idle, or the fall in the air). WindUp "should never take longer than 0.75 seconds". Strike is "the strongest pose, seen often by players as the action pauses here when a hit lands". FollowThrough "must not look like the StandBy, and typically is the longest part". Moves were exaggerated to give the player feedback and to warn the opponent. On R6: cap a gameplay wind-up at 45 frames. Because hitstop holds the contact pose for 8 to 16 frames (combat.md, "Hitstop"), the contact must be a complete, readable pose in silhouette. This differs from keyframe-workflow.md, "Plan before keying" (the golden pose), which names the load or the follow-through extreme, not the contact, as the golden pose: keep the longest moving hold on that pose, and make the contact strong enough to survive the hitstop. The 45-frame cap also differs from anime-craft.md, "Contrast across a scene", which gives a showcase move 60 to 120 frames of preparation: the cap holds for attacks an opponent must answer; the long preparation suits moves that take control away (an ultimate cut-in, an emote).

- **Design fixes the cast time; the animation fits inside it.** Rory Alderton (Riot, 2014): Yasuo's ultimate had a hard limit of "one second - that's 30 animation frames - cast time", and "it was absolutely vital the animation did not exceed this time"; ultimates "need to be larger than life and highly readable". On R6: take each skill's lock time from the design, set the clip so `Poser.realTime(clip, t)` of its release event equals it (use `warp` before the release, pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"), and put the extra spectacle in the follow-through after control returns. Poses that vanish behind effects are covered in acting-and-performance.md (drafted in this run), "Silhouette, distance and the player camera".

## First and last frames

- **No vital pose before the blend has built up.** Delannoy: a clip that starts with its anticipation loses it under a long crossfade, because the screen still shows mostly the old clip. Author assets "so that there aren't any vital poses in the animation until the current animation has reached a large enough weight in the blend", for example, do not start the bat swing too early in a swing clip entered by a long blend. On R6: Poser's inertial blend removes the offset on a smoothstep over `fadeIn`, so key a clip's first vital pose (the anticipation extreme, the first step's push) at or after `fadeIn` (frame 5 for 0.08 s, frame 12 for 0.2 s). When a player move must load in 1 to 3 frames, make its first key equal the idle or the previous clip's end pose and play it with `fadeIn` near 0 (stylized-and-roblox.md, "The Animator: fades, priorities and weights").

- **A blend needs the outgoing motion to keep moving.** Delannoy: for a smooth transition "it is essential for the outgoing/slave animation to still update and move according to its momentum for the duration of the blend". If the outgoing clip runs out first, the blend uses "a static pose with no defined velocity", read as zero speed, and the blended speed jumps. On R6: Poser's `inertial` and `cross` blends both start from a frozen pose and neither carries the old clip's velocity (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). Keep blends between moving clips short (0.08 to 0.12 s) and make the incoming clip's first 4 to 6 frames continue the outgoing motion's direction and speed. A long blend that keeps sampling the outgoing clip needs new runtime code.

- **Start a moving attack from the run's own frames.** Massoudi: for run attacks "the animators have concatenated one loop of run before it and created the run attack just after that", so the speed blend works and the run's speed and responsiveness carry into the attack. On R6: author a run attack with one run cycle copied at its head and the attack keyed after it. Play it with `startAt` set to the running clip's current phase time and `fadeIn = 0`, so the first frame equals the frame on screen, and keep the root moving at run speed until the strike's braced plant.

- **Add travel by scaling the speed curve, never by an even offset.** Delannoy: when a clip must cover 20% more distance, spreading the extra motion evenly over every frame keeps the character moving in its idle. Scaling the speed curve by 20% speeds only the moving parts and keeps "zero velocity" where the clip rests, which avoids foot sliding. On R6: when a dash or lunge must cover more studs, scale the root travel curve that the place's mover reads (pipeline.md, "Networked actions, hits and knockback", root travel from the clip) by the ratio of distances; never add a constant per frame. The end hold keeps 0 studs/s, and `_G.feet` must still show slide 0.05 or less there.

## One clock for animation, design, effects and sound

- **Animation sets the timing in the game; sound and effects lay over it.** Jon Mummery (animation lead, Perfect Dark Zero, Rare, 2006 interview): the animator "has to be able to communicate with everyone from programmers, artists, audio, particles, and the concept guys on a daily basis". Designers ask for animations of "a certain length" or that "move a certain distance", and this "goes back and forward until we settle on something mutual". For a weapon he animated the shooting and reloads first and put them in the game; "Once happy with how a weapon feels in-game and its position on screen", he sent a movie of the animation to the audio department to add sound on top. As lead he also edited the game's data files to tweak animation speeds. On R6: settle each clip's length, travel and `events` with design in the place first. Once it feels right, freeze the event table (contact, cancel, step, effect cues) and hand sound and effects a capture plus the real event times from `Poser.realTime`. Any later retime re-sends both.

- **Key poses on the beat when the game has a tempo.** Tango Gameworks (Hi-Fi Rush, CEDEC 2023, in a gameanim.com summary): the game "was animated for 120BPM (beats-per-minute) at 60 frames per second and dynamically modified thereafter. Key poses hit on the beat", and all elements were matched to the BPM so they "could be sped up and down in sync". On R6: at 120 BPM a beat is 30 frames: put key poses on frames 0, 30 and 60 (15-frame steps on eighth notes) and play at `speed = bpm / 120` to follow another tempo. Keys, lag and `events` stay in clip time, so effects tied to the events move with the speed. Use this for emotes and rhythm moves; pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays", has keying to loudness peaks for songs without a fixed tempo.

- **Each client fires a move's effects from its own copy of the clip.** A DevForum developer of the Roblox game Combat Legends (2026) describes the client to server to all clients pattern. The server validates the input; then every client plays the animation on the attacker and "listen[s] to the animation events on said animation to display visuals and sfx", which keeps a laggy attacker's move smooth for viewers. In a 2020 thread, effects fired by a remote at the attacker's marker appeared on other clients before the animation reached that marker; marker signals did fire on observers' copies of another player's track. In a 2024 thread a reply recalls that the events do not fire if the animation fails to load. Reviewers of a 2022 combat demo noticed particles appearing before the animation played. On R6: Motor6D.Transform does not replicate, so each observer already plays the clip locally (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"). Drive each client's effects and sounds from that client's own clip clock crossing the clip's `events` times, never from the arrival of the attacker's remote. Poser clips are code, so they cannot fail to load, but the event scheduler is the place's code (pipeline.md: Poser has no marker dispatcher).

## Blocking in the place and reviewing in play

- **Hand designers gray-box animation from the first days.** Jay Hosfelt (Epic Games, GDC 2014, in a gameanim.com summary) covered tools and techniques for quick "gray box" animation assets that give designers fast iteration and let animators take part in early prototyping. Mummery: animators sometimes animated temporary models they built themselves before the final model existed. On R6: give design stepped blocking clips (whole poses on `e = "step"`, real contact and cancel `events`) in the place before any polish, and keep the clip names and event names fixed while the keys change, so gameplay code never has to change with the animation.

- **Review from the angles other players see.** Boehm's GDC 2017 talk (Inven Global): viewing Overwatch's first-person animations from a third-person view showed cheats made for the player's camera. Reinhardt's right hand passed through his left hand "as if it were a ghost", and Winston's left arm never touches the ground: he "actually walks only with his two legs". On R6: the owner sees the back from 12.5 studs, but opponents see the front and sides at any distance, and a cheat toward the owner's camera (a blade turned flat to it) shows from there. Capture `front` and `front34` strips at the contact and the recovery of every combat clip, and look for limbs through each other or through the torso.

- **Expect the first clips of a new character to be wrong.** Lico (80.lv, 2017): "my first animations with a new character rarely feel right", and lots of feedback helps develop the character's identity. Mikhail Vershinin (Game Developer blog, 2024): in many studios with streamlined production, a piece of work that gets feedback more than four times passes to another animator. On R6: block the character's idle, walk and M1 first, review them in Play, and rework them once the rest of the set exists. Track the review rounds per clip in the clip plan; a clip on its fifth round needs a new reference or a new golden pose, not a sixth tweak.

## Polish under a deadline

- **Spend the time on poses; beautiful motion is not the goal.** Vershinin: create the poses on stepped interpolation with hair and clothing off, add breakdowns "Still no interpolation!", and spline only after the art manager or lead has approved every pose; "good preparation accounts for 60-70 percent of the work"; overlaps on hair and clothing come last; "Too beautiful animation is not a guarantee of gameplay success or a game feature". On R6: under a deadline cut the polish layers before the poses. Keep the stepped poses, the timing and the `events`; then spline with `curve = "spline"` and `Feet.flat`. `lag`, `springs` and `life` come last (keyframe-workflow.md, "Layer by layer").

- **Secondary motion is cheap, but test that it helps.** Cooper (2019): drag on loose parts done procedurally in the engine is "a fantastic way to increase the quality of a character's animation with little extra work", not least because "their motion will continue whatever the following animation the player performs". Massoudi: Kuro's scarf helps show more acceptable motion. Normoyle and Jörg found the opposite in one test: a controller identical to Blend except for a physically simulated cape was enjoyed significantly less, and the authors do not know why. On R6: put springs on loose parts (capes, hair, tails, chains, principles.md, "Follow-through and springs") but play-test the character with and without them. Poser's spring state resets on `play`, so loose parts do not carry their swing from one clip into the next; carrying it needs new runtime code.

## Mistakes leads see most

- **The mistakes leads name most.** Vershinin: animators "immediately working with interpolation, merging the blocking and spline stages", and scaling the root until the character turns "into jelly". Lico: "It's way too easy to craft poses/actions that defy physics and anatomy". Gibson: "total symmetry looks stiff". Delannoy: extra motion spread over still frames, and key poses placed so early that the blend hides them. Cooper (2019): "A common mistake most junior animators make is to have all elements of a character start or arrive at the same time". A DevForum reviewer of a combat clip (2022): the character "recovers from punching as if it was using as little force as possible". On R6: these map to existing checks. Block in steps first (keyframe-workflow.md, "From stepped to spline"); keep the COM over the support (SKILL.md, method step 4); break twins (principles.md, "Poses first: the posing checklist"); spread stops (`Poser.check` unison); show the force in an over-extended recovery (principles.md, "Game feel: anticipation, contact, hitstop, recovery, cancel"); and the two blend rules in "First and last frames" above.

## Stylised and realistic direction

- **A realistic project dials the exaggeration down, not off.** Bruno Velazquez (animation director, Santa Monica Studio, GDC 2019, reported by GameRevolution): the earlier God of War games were about 90% hand-animated and 10% motion captured in cinematics. Grounding Kratos pushed the team toward motion capture. The old "comic book, over the top" proportions "worked for the gameplay and the far away camera angles" but not once those were no longer the focus. The new Kratos "is still over the top", but "a little bit more grounded than it used to be before". His walk is "dynamic, strong, and grounded" without overexaggerating, kills "still had to be brutal, but not overly so", and dual swords "had to be reined in and Kratos didn't jump around as much with them". On R6: a grounded R6 game (horror, military, a slow melee duel) pushes poses at the low end of SKILL.md's 10 to 20% (about 10%), keeps attacks on the ground, takes timings from human-motion-data.md and keeps one flourish per signature move. A battlegrounds set pushes to 20% and lets signature moves break physics (anime-craft.md, "Contrast across a scene").

- **Stylised work keeps real body mechanics under the exaggeration.** Lico on Polyarc's Moss: he aims for "all that texture and accuracy of proper body mechanics" mixed with the style of classic Disney. Disney's animators pushed exaggeration "while also showing exquisite detail like how a hip reacts when the foot plants". He merges realism and exaggeration instead of choosing one. On R6: in a stylised set, push the poses and the timing contrast, but keep the mechanics checks unchanged: COM over the support, planted soles flat, a torso drop when a foot takes the weight (body-mechanics.md). The exaggeration lives in the poses and spacing, never in a body that floats.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Stretched limbs and blades (Boehm: Genji's Dragonblade stretching "like rubber", Zenyatta's leg at "an abnormal length", McCree's stretched arm): R6 parts cannot scale, and sliding a limb out past torso contact breaks the feet and arm rule; the nearest substitute is a piston along the limb within the contact limit (r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons").
- Root squash under 20% (Vershinin): R6 parts cannot scale; a compact pose stands in for squash (SKILL.md, the principles table, "Squash and stretch").
- Eyelid animation (Vershinin's "crazy eyes"): R6 has no face rig by default.
- First-person reload rules (Boehm: keep reloads clear of the reticle, Soldier: 76's reload moved to the left hand): they govern first-person view arms, which an R6 body does not have; they apply only to a viewmodel the place builds.
- Motion matching (Naughty Dog's The Last of Us Part II, GDC 2021): needs a large captured motion database and a matching runtime; Poser plays authored clips.
- Joystick input stabilisation (Delannoy): input code, not animation; it belongs to the place's control scripts.
