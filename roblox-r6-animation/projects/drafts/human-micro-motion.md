# Life in still poses: sway, breath, gaze and fidgets

The measured small motion that keeps a standing person from ever being still (postural sway, weight shifts, breathing, gaze shifts, fidgets), how game animators build idles that never look looped, and what each number becomes on six rigid R6 parts through Poser. Real data is the target for timing, weight and balance; the translation decides what reads on blocks seen from the player camera. Every finding was checked against its source ([sources.md](sources.md), "Life in still poses: sway, breath, gaze and fidgets research"). Human-to-R6 scaling uses 5 studs for a 1.75 m body (2.86 studs per metre), as [human-motion-data.md](human-motion-data.md) does; numbers marked "our derivation" are computed here from the sourced ones. Torso `p` x, `p` y and `p` z are the side, height and front-back components of the RootJoint offset, keyed on the `Torso` joint ([pipeline.md](pipeline.md), "Poser clip contract"). The idle recipe, the breath rate and the `life` range this file builds on are in principles.md, "Idle or held stance", and locomotion.md, "Idles and breathing".

## Quiet standing sway

- **Quiet standing sways about 2 cm front to back over two minutes.** Foulger, Reiter, Kuo et al. (2025; 16 adults, three 2-minute trials per stance, PLOS ONE) measured the whole-body centre of mass (COM) on a force platform: a front-back RMS of 4.1 to 4.9 mm and a maximum front-back range of 21.3 to 25.2 mm, nearly the same at every stance width. On R6 that is an RMS of 0.012 to 0.014 studs and a range of 0.06 to 0.07 studs of torso `p` z (our derivation). Key the sway as a slow drift of torso `p` z with `Feet.flat` keeping the soles: `auto` keys 10 to 30 s apart, 0.04 to 0.08 studs between neighbours (our choice, about twice the real range, so the drift adds to the breath without rocking the body). At its real size the drift is about one pixel (What reads from the player camera).
- **Side sway shrinks as the feet widen; front-back sway does not.** Foulger et al. measured a side-to-side COM range of 21.7 mm with the feet narrow, 10.8 mm at hip width and 8.1 mm at shoulder width (RMS 4.2, 2.0 and 1.7 mm), while the front-back range stayed at 21 to 25 mm. Błaszczyk and Beck (2023; 100 young adults, 60 s with eyes open, Journal of Human Kinetics) found the centre of pressure (COP) moving faster front to back (6.4 to 6.5 mm/s) than side to side (5.2 mm/s). Duarte and Freitas's posturography review (2010) states that a wider base raises stability, through less sway or wider limits. One study of COP extent (Yoon et al., 2012; 100 adults) found the largest side extent at shoulder width, which its authors put down to effort in the lower leg muscles; the COM data above describe the body. On R6 the default legs stand at about shoulder width (sole centres 1 stud apart, about 0.35 m; our derivation). There, keep the torso `p` x drift at about 0.4 of the `p` z drift. When the legs slide together under the torso (a formal or nervous stance), make the side drift as large as the front-back drift (our derivation from the ratios).
- **A person trying to stand still moves each joint a fraction of a degree.** Wang, Ko, Challis and Newell (2014; PLoS ONE), feet side by side at shoulder width, standing as still as possible: joint-angle SDs of 0.21 degrees at the ankle, 0.18 at the knee, 0.30 at the hip and 0.31 at the neck. On one foot they rose to 0.57 to 0.92 degrees, and with deliberate rocking to 0.82 to 1.78. On R6 real stillness is under a third of a degree, well below `life = 1` (about 1 degree on the torso, 1.4 on the head and 1.2 on the arms, pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). The `life` range in SKILL.md is therefore already several times as restless as a still person, which is what six blocks need to read alive. For a one-legged or balancing hold, raise `life` to about three times the two-footed value, as the measured SDs rise (our derivation).
- **Sway is slow: half of its power lies under about 0.33 Hz.** Yamamoto, Smith, Suzuki et al. (2015; 15 young adults, 60 trials of 70 s each, Physiological Reports) measured median sway frequencies of 0.32 Hz side to side and 0.33 Hz front to back, with 95% of the front-back power under about 1.1 Hz. They separate a slow component (0.1 to 0.5 Hz), a fast one (0.5 to 1.0 Hz) and a very fast one (1.0 to 2.5 Hz) whose COP motion stays under 3 mm, and cite Zatsiorsky and Duarte's "rambling" and "trembling" at about 0.16 and 0.67 Hz. On R6 Poser's default `lifeRate` of 0.35 Hz sits on the measured median. Keep it between 0.15 and 0.5 Hz for a calm stance (our choice). The fast components move under 0.01 studs (our derivation), below what reads, so a calm idle gains nothing from a faster `lifeRate`; a visible tremble is effort, not stillness (principles.md, "Moving holds and settles").
- **The body holds a spot, then migrates, at every time scale.** Yamamoto et al. found the COP staying inside a 3 mm circle for about 2.3 s on average (with large differences between people) before it moved to another spot. Duarte and Sternad (2008; Experimental Brain Research; 30-minute standing, young and older adults) found the fluctuations not different from 1/f noise over time scales of 10 to 600 s. On R6 one breath wave plus `life` covers only the scale of seconds. Add the longer scales as separate layers: the torso drift keys 10 to 30 s apart (above) and the weight shifts of the next section over minutes (our translation of the 1/f result).
- **Closing the eyes speeds the sway by a fifth to a third.** Błaszczyk and Beck (2023) measured a mean COP speed of 9.2 mm/s with eyes open in both sexes and 11.2 (women) and 12.4 mm/s (men) with eyes closed; the front-back speed rose from 6.4 to 8.0 and from 6.5 to 9.1 mm/s, the side-to-side speed from 5.2 to 6.2 and 6.4 mm/s. On R6, for a character with closed eyes (dozing, meditating, savouring a moment), enlarge the torso drift by a fifth to a third and keep its slow rate (our scaling of the measured speeds). Closed eyes show only on a Dynamic Head driven by runtime code (Head and gaze, blinks) or a face that is already closed.
- **Unpleasant feelings make the body sway less; arousal makes the breath bigger.** Takahashi, Kaneko, Nakazawa et al. (2025; 22 young men viewing pictures for 72 s while standing, Physiological Reports): unpleasant pictures reduced the variability of the COM and of the ankle angle against neutral and pleasant ones; high-arousal pictures raised tidal volume, and pleasant ones raised the breath rate. On R6, for a frightened or disgusted character, shrink the torso drift and `life` (by about a third, our choice) and keep the breath. For an excited character, enlarge the breath's torso pitch and sink rather than its rate.

## Weight shifts in long standing

- **Natural standing mixes three patterns: shifting, fidgeting and drifting.** Duarte and Freitas (2010) summarise Duarte and Zatsiorsky's study of prolonged unconstrained standing, the posture of someone talking or waiting in a line: shifting is a step (a quick move of the mean COP from one region to another), fidgeting is a pulse (a fast, large displacement and a return to about the same position), and drifting is a ramp (a slow, continuous displacement). On R6 give each its own curve on torso `p` x and `p` z, with `Feet.flat` keeping the soles. Key a shift as a 20 to 40 frame move into a new held offset that keeps drifting, a fidget as an out-and-back of 30 to 60 frames, and a drift as one ramp over 10 s or more (frame counts are our choice). A deliberate, acted balance change from one leg to the other took 6.6 to 7.7 s per clip in StayStill (Fidgets and idle breaks).
- **Small shifts come about every 25 s, full ones every few minutes.** Bates, McGregor and Alexander (2021; BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders) had 22 people of normal flexibility stand 15 minutes with one foot on each of two force plates. Median fidgets per minute: 2.39 that moved 10 to 25% of body weight between the feet, 0.70 that moved 25 to 50%, and 0.26 that moved more than 50%. Leeuwis et al. (2026; Journal of Applied Physiology) chose a 30 s interval between prompted shifts to match how often people shift spontaneously in prolonged standing (citing Prado-Rico and Duarte). On R6, in an idle watched for minutes, schedule a small shift about every 25 s, a medium one about every 1.5 minutes and a full shift onto the other leg about every 4 minutes (the inverse of the medians), with the intervals varied. Moving a fraction f of the weight moves the COM f studs between soles 1 stud apart. Under `Feet.flat` the legs stay vertical while the torso slides over them, so only the head, torso and arms (0.68 of the mass in body-mechanics.md, "Centre of mass on R6") carry the COM, and the torso `p` x must be about 1.5 f: 0.15 to 0.37 studs for a small shift (our derivation). Medium and full shifts need more slide than the feet rule allows (next bullet).
- **People rest either evenly or with about 87% of the weight on one leg.** Prado-Rico and Duarte (2019; 41 young adults, Motor Control): in relaxed standing the asymmetry was small on average but large instant by instant, because people alternated between postures; women were more asymmetric than men, and two preferred modes of weight distribution appeared. Leeuwis et al. (2026) report the modes from that work's 16-minute trials: about 50/50 and an asymmetry averaging 74%, that is 87% of the weight on one leg; women spent 37% of the time in highly asymmetric postures and men 14%. On R6 an 87/13 rest puts the COM 0.37 studs off the stance centre, beyond the 0.33 sideways limit in SKILL.md, "Measure before you look", and it needs about 0.55 studs of torso slide, past the 0.5 at which the far leg keeps half its top face under the torso (SKILL.md, "Feet rule"; our derivation). Park the torso at 0.3 to 0.45 studs (about 70/30 to 80/20) and sell the rest with the cues in body-mechanics.md, "Balance and weight shift": the stance-side shoulder dropped by a small roll and the free leg unweighted with a slight lift or splay. Give a female character's idle more time in the asymmetric mode than a male one's, as measured.
- **Long standing brings more shifts, and shifts cost energy rather than save it.** Leeuwis et al. (2026; 20 adults) note that standing longer than about 10 minutes raises the incidence of weight shifts (citing earlier studies). Prompted shifts every 30 s raised energy use by 5.3%, and standing asymmetrically between shifts raised it by a further 7.0%, so the authors conclude that both behaviours may serve other goals, such as relieving discomfort or fatigue. Freitas, Wieczorek, Marchetti and Duarte (2005; 14 adults and 14 elderly, Gait & Posture) found quiet-stance COP RMS and speed higher after 30 minutes of standing than before, and the elderly made postural changes of lesser amplitude. On R6 an NPC who has waited for a long time shifts more often and sways more: after about 10 minutes, shorten the shift intervals and enlarge the drift (by how much is our choice). An old or stiff character makes smaller shifts. Show each shift as relief: the newly free leg relaxes with a small unweighting slide after the torso arrives (our reading).

## Breathing

- **At rest the inhale is the shorter part of the breath.** Davis and Stagg (1975; 15 resting adults, 228 to 365 breaths each, Journal of Physiology) found mean inspiratory times mostly between 1 and 2.5 s and expiratory times between 2.0 and 3.5 s, with tidal volumes of 0.4 to 0.9 litres. Bigger breaths had longer inhales (tidal volume against inspiratory time, mean r = +0.70), so the mean inspiratory flow stayed nearly constant from breath to breath. On R6, split a calm 4 s breath about 40/60 (our choice inside those ranges): the inhale returns the torso to its rest height and pitches it back over about 96 frames, and the exhale sinks and pitches it forward over about 144. The inhale must not lift the torso above rest, or the legs lose contact (r6-mechanics.md, "Planted feet on rigid legs", a rising torso). Give a bigger breath a longer inhale, not a faster one. This contradicts keyframe-workflow.md, "Breath as a chain" ("give the inhale more of the cycle"), and the "inhale slower than the exhale" of SKILL.md's life rule. Use the measured split for a realistic, calm or tired idle; keep the long inhale for a performed breath the viewer must watch, such as a deliberate deep breath or the intake of a sigh.
- **Concentration speeds the breath, shrinks it and stills the body.** Hagio, Obata, Nakazawa et al. (2018; PLoS ONE): standing during mental arithmetic raised the breath rate from 16.0 to 19.8 per minute, shrank the breathing movement from 2.39 to 1.58 cm SD and cut the front-back COP RMS from 3.47 to 2.49 mm (about 28% less). The change in breath size correlated with the change in sway (r = 0.75). They cite earlier work: a voluntarily larger tidal volume gave more COP displacement and joint motion, and holding the breath gave less. On R6, for a character listening, reading or aiming, shorten the breath to about 3 s (180 frames), cut its pitch and sink by a third and cut the drift with it. Wherever the breath grows (after exertion, a sigh), let the drift and `life` grow for those breaths too (our scaling of the measured ratios).
- **After light exertion the breath takes minutes to settle.** Spicer and Dennis (2024; 100 healthy young women, JSAMS Plus): 14.3 breaths per minute at rest and 19 one minute after a six-minute walk test; the heart rate came back within four beats of resting after 4.0 to 4.5 minutes on average. On R6, after a walk or a light task, start the idle at about 3.2 s per breath (19 per minute) and lengthen it to about 4.2 s (14.3 per minute) over about 4 minutes. After a sprint, start from locomotion.md's 2 to 2.5 s ("Idles and breathing"). A breath whose period changes needs a procedural joint (a joint given as a function of `(t, ctx)`, pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"), and a clip that never ends in play carries `length = 100000` (pipeline.md, "Baking and export"; bake only an excerpt of 60 s or less). This clip ramps the period over 240 s, splits each breath 40/60 and lets `life` run unlooped, so nothing repeats. Its 2 degree torso breath, 0.04 stud sink and half-size head with lags of 0.25 and 0.45 s follow principles.md, "Idle or held stance"; replace the rest keys with the idle's own pose.

```lua
local Feet = require(script.Parent.Feet)

local T0, T1, RAMP = 3.2, 4.2, 240
local B = (T1 - T0) / RAMP

local function cycles(t)
	if t < RAMP then
		return math.log(1 + B * t / T0) / B
	end
	return math.log(T1 / T0) / B + (t - RAMP) / T1
end

local function breath(t)
	local u = cycles(t) % 1
	if u < 0.4 then
		return (1 - math.cos(math.pi * u / 0.4)) / 2
	end
	return (1 + math.cos(math.pi * (u - 0.4) / 0.6)) / 2
end

return {
	name = "IdleRecover",
	length = 100000,
	loop = false,
	life = 1,
	lag = {Head = 0.25, ["Right Arm"] = 0.45, ["Left Arm"] = 0.45},
	joints = {
		Torso = function(t)
			local s = breath(t)
			return {r = {-4 + 2 * s, 0, 0}, p = Vector3.new(0, -0.04 + 0.04 * s, 0)}
		end,
		Head = function(t)
			return {r = {-0.5 + breath(t), 0, 0}}
		end,
		["Right Arm"] = {{t = 0, r = {0, 0, 0}}},
		["Left Arm"] = {{t = 0, r = {0, 0, 0}}},
		["Right Leg"] = {{t = 0, r = {0, 0, 0}}},
		["Left Leg"] = {{t = 0, r = {0, 0, 0}}},
	},
	post = Feet.flat({r = {0.5, 0, 0}, l = {-0.5, 0, 0}}),
}
```

- **After hard exercise the extra sway fades in three phases, the fast one halving each minute.** Zemková's review (2022; Frontiers in Physiology) describes the recovery of COP speed after exercise: first a plateau or a slight rise while ventilation is repaid, then a fast exponential decline with a half-time of about 1 minute that follows the fall in ventilation, then a slow component. It may take a few minutes for sway to return to its pre-exercise level after prolonged or intense exercise. On R6, after a sprint or a fight, hold the enlarged drift and breath for the first two or three breaths, then halve their excess over the calm idle about every minute, and let the last part fade over a few minutes (our translation). In the procedural clip above, multiply the breath's pitch and sink by `1 + k * 0.5 ^ (t / 60)` after the plateau, with k the starting excess.
- **A sigh is a double breath about every two minutes.** Andrews, Melnychuk, Dockree et al. (2026; Psychophysiology) counted as sighs the breaths of at least twice the mean inspiratory volume: 0.63 per minute during an attention task of eight 8-minute blocks and 0.45 per minute with spontaneous breathing in a 21-minute task, at mean rates of 14.8 and 16.3 breaths per minute. Vlemincx, Severs and Ramirez's review (2022; Biological Psychology) reports more sighs during fear, depression and desire imagery than during relaxation or a neutral state, more under mental stress, and sighs as a sign of relief. On R6, in an idle longer than a minute, make one breath every 1.5 to 2 minutes a sigh: twice the torso pitch and sink, a longer inhale (as Davis and Stagg found for bigger breaths) and a long exhale with the arms settling 2 to 4 frames after the torso (our numbers). Double the rate for an anxious or sad character, and give a relief sigh after a fight ends.
- **The head floats with the breath by millimetres.** MoCap Online's idle design guide (2026; a seller of captured idles) describes the head bobbing "a few millimeters" with the breathing cycle and the shoulders rising on the inhale. On R6 a few millimetres is under 0.01 studs and, as a slide, breaks SKILL.md's "Turn the head; do not slide it". Keep the head's `p` at zero and give it about 1 degree of `lift` in phase with the torso breath, lagged, as in the clip above. A DevForum reviewer asked for the same thing on an R6 idle: the head down on the exhale and back up on the inhale, "as the shoulders go up so should the head" (What reads from the player camera).

## Head and gaze

- **Small gaze shifts are mostly the eyes; on R6 the head must make them.** Stahl (1999; Experimental Brain Research) found a central eye-only range in which gaze shifts came with no head movement, 35.8 ± 31.9 degrees wide (mean ± SD across subjects); beyond it the head moved about 0.77 degrees per degree of extra eye eccentricity. People differed widely but each was consistent across sessions. Fang, Nakashima, Matsumiya et al. (2015; free visual search in a 360 degree display, PLoS ONE): 90% of the gaze shifts made without the head stayed within ±30 degrees horizontally and ±12 vertically. On R6 the painted eyes do not move, so every look is a head turn (r6-craft.md, "The head and acting without a face"). Turn the head the whole way to a target the player must see the character notice. Keep idle glances that only show life small (5 to 10 degrees of `twist`, our choice), because a person would make them with the eyes. Treat the eye-only range as character: a lively character turns the head even for small glances, a reserved one glances seldom and keeps the head still (our reading of the individual differences).
- **The gaze lands well before the head stops.** Hooge, Niehorster, Nyström et al. (2024; Behavior Research Methods): in a 150 degree gaze shift the gaze arrived in about 250 ms, as fast as an 80 degree saccade, while the 90 degree head movement took about 400 ms; the eyes reach about 40 degrees each way. In a single-trial example in Freedman's review (2008; Experimental Brain Research), the head kept turning for 250 ms and 15 degrees after the eyes reached the target, the eyes counter-rotating, so its 17 degree movement added only about 2 degrees to the gaze. On R6 the head stands for both, so shape a look as a fast arrival and a slow finish. Put a breakdown at about 60% of the turn's frames with 85 to 90% of the angle, on `auto` tangents (our translation: in Hooge et al.'s example the gaze arrived at about 60% of the head's time): a 90 degree look arrives in about 15 frames and settles by frame 24. The settle is the head catching up behind the eyes and reads as a moving hold, not a stop.
- **Surprise decides whether the eyes or the head start first.** Hooge et al. (citing Zangemeister and Stark, and Moschner and Zangemeister): when the target is unpredictable, the saccade starts 25 to 40 ms before the head; when it is predictable, the head starts first. Freedman notes that the head can begin well before the saccade in large or predictable gaze shifts. On R6 a 25 to 40 ms eye lead is 1.5 to 2.4 frames and has no eyes to show it. Start a startled look at once, fast, with no wind-up. Start a planned look (a guard checking a door he knows) with the head early and smooth; the small anticipation dip of principles.md, "Idle or held stance", suits this planned look, not the startled one (our reading).
- **A scan is one head movement carrying several fixations.** Fang et al. (2015): about 43% of head movements carried one saccade and about 36% two, fewer for more, so the eyes jumped several times inside one continuous head movement; the head's share of the gaze shift grew with the number of saccades. On R6, key a look-around as one continuous head turn with two or three speed dips where the eyes would fixate: keys 8 to 15 frames apart on `auto`, slowing but staying above a tenth of the peak speed so `Poser.check` counts no stop (our translation). Neither one even sweep nor separate stop-and-go turns shows the fixations.
- **A fast look back takes about half a second; at free speed it comes in steps.** Anastasopoulos, Naushahi, Sklavos et al. (2015; standing adults turning to remembered targets as fast as possible, Experimental Brain Research): single-step gaze shifts took 315, 449 and 599 ms to targets at 90, 135 and 180 degrees, with peak head speeds of 279, 363 and 428 deg/s. Multiple-step shifts took 506, 611 and 791 ms with peaks of 172 to 266 deg/s. Single steps made up about 70% of these fast trials but about 30% in earlier studies at freely chosen speed, and the feet started on average 350 ms after the eyes (citing their 2009 study). On R6 a startled look back over 135 degrees takes about 27 frames with the head peaking near 6 degrees per frame. A casual one over 135 to 180 degrees takes 37 to 47 frames in two or three steps, the head slowing between them (our conversion). Start the `Feet.flat` yaw pivot about 21 frames after the head starts, so the body turns only when the head and torso run out of range. human-motion-data.md, "Starts, stops and turns", measured the leading foot only about 130 to 190 ms after the head in timed 180 degree turns: use that order for a turn toward a visible goal and the late feet for a look back.
- **People blink about every 3.5 s at rest, more in talk, less in reading.** Bentivoglio et al. (1997; 150 people, Movement Disorders): 17 blinks per minute at rest, 26 in conversation and 4.5 while reading, with cognition mattering more than age. A classic R6 head shows a decal face with no eyelids. Roblox has since moved every classic head and face to a Dynamic Head (DevForum, "Completing the Dynamic Head Migration", rolled out on 23 March 2026), and a Dynamic Head's `FaceControls` include `LeftEyeClosed` and `RightEyeClosed`, each set from 0 to 1 (creator docs). Poser drives Motor6Ds only, so a blink needs new runtime code that sets those two properties, and a player can lock the face (`HumanoidDescription.StaticFacialAnimation`, per the announcement). keyframe-workflow.md, "One thought, one action", puts acted blinks on thought shifts and never on a timer. Keep that for performances; the measured rate applies only as a background rate in a long idle.

## Fidgets and idle breaks

- **People touch their face one to two times a minute.** A webcam study of 490 pedestrians in public places (2023; Interactive Journal of Medical Research) measured an average of 1.8 face touches per minute and cites 0.8 per minute indoors in an earlier study of 10 people; a review by Spencer, Francis and Coroneo (2021; The Ocular Surface) gives 9 to 162 touches per hour across the literature. On R6 a hand at the face lifts the whole arm and becomes the main action of the moment, so treat it as an idle break, not as background: once every 30 to 60 s at most (our choice). The fist reaches only forehead height over the head centre (SKILL.md, "Overhead reach"), so aim at the chin, the cheek or the side of the head, and check the hand path by forward kinematics (`Poser.posesAt`).
- **One idle action lasts about four to ten seconds.** StayStill (Atxa Landa, Rodriguez, Lazkano and Kucherenko; Symposium on Computer Animation 2026) recorded 50 non-actors aged 22 to 65 performing each idle action twice, cleaned into 1534 clips at 30 fps. Dividing each action's frames by its clip count (our derivation) gives mean clip lengths of 4.1 s to check a watch, 4.8 s to scratch the head or yawn, 4.7 to 5.0 s to look back, 5.3 s to touch the face or rub the eyes, 6.0 s to stretch the arms, 7.0 s to look at the floor, 7.4 s to look around, 6.6 to 7.7 s for a balance change from one leg to the other and 9.5 s to look up at the sky. On R6 author each idle break as one clip of 250 to 600 frames that starts and ends on the main idle's frame (r6-craft.md, "Idle variations play by weight"). Looking (up, around, down, back), checking a watch (the arm lifts across, the head drops to it), stretching the arms or the back, and a weight change read well on six parts. A head scratch works with the arm up beside the head. A yawn is only a torso stretch with the head tipped back, unless runtime code opens a Dynamic Head's jaw.
- **Acted idles look as real as idles caught unawares.** Atxa Landa, Lazkano, Rodriguez et al. (2025; arXiv; 123 viewers, 10 s clips on one neutral humanoid) recorded idles from people who did not know they were filmed and from people asked to act them. Viewers labelled both kinds "real" equally often (0.537 and 0.539; chi-square p = 0.89), and the average joint speeds matched. On R6, film yourself acting the wait (StayStill's subjects were told they were waiting in the street for someone or for a bus) and copy it as the reference (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference"); hidden-camera footage is not needed.

## Building idles that never loop visibly

- **Viewers can tell hand-keyed idles from captured ones, and their speeds differ.** In Atxa Landa et al.'s second experiment (2025; 114 viewers), Mixamo's handmade idles against recorded idles were told apart about 62% of the time (0.627 and 0.618 correct; p = 1.8 × 10^-44). The handmade idles turned their joints more slowly and more evenly across the body, varied less, and moved the arm from the upper arm. In the recordings the arms turned fastest, the forearm more than the upper arm, and the neck faster than in the handmade clips. MoCap Online, which sells captured idles, writes that hand-keyed idles tend to be "either too still (wooden) or too exaggerated (floaty)". On R6 make the speeds uneven: the head and the arms carry the fastest small motions and the torso the slowest. The R6 arm stands for the forearm (r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons"), so give it small swings and twists of its own instead of riding the torso. Check per-joint peak speeds from `Poser.posesAt`: in a calm idle the head and arms should peak above the torso (our translation).
- **Noise reads as balance and a wandering gaze when its statistics match.** Perlin and Goldberg (Improv, SIGGRAPH 1996) used coherent noise for the small motions of a character keeping its balance, the controlled randomness of blinking and a gaze wandering around a room: "viewers do not perceive the mechanism itself. Instead they perceive some statistics of the motion it produces." The authors attribute visitors' belief in their character partly to motions that "never repeated themselves precisely". Perlin's face demo lets the viewer switch off "the little controlled random movements that bring the character to life". On R6 `life` is this noise (two octaves at `lifeRate`); give it the measured statistics (Quiet standing sway). In a looped clip Poser samples `life` on a circle of the loop length, so it repeats every loop (pipeline.md, "Baking and export"). For an idle that must never repeat, play a non-looping clip with `length = 100000` (Breathing, the example clip) or rotate several breaks.
- **Layer by scope: whole-body actions at the back, fleeting ones in front.** Perlin and Goldberg place global actions such as walking in the rear groups, local ones such as head scratching in front of them and very fleeting ones such as scratching or coughing further in front still; a front action obscures the ones behind it on the joints they share. On R6 Poser plays one clip per rig (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). A fidget is either its own idle break that starts and ends on the idle's frame, or part of an explicit composite clip that takes the fidget's joints over the idle with a weight envelope (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"). Laying a fidget over a running idle on only its own joints needs new runtime code.
- **One loop shows after about 90 s; break it with timed variations.** MoCap Online's guide (2026; vendor advice, not a study) says a single idle loop becomes noticeable after about 90 s and recommends a base loop plus 2 to 4 secondary idles at random intervals. Its design guide gives loops of 2 to 4 s for a simple breathing idle and 8 to 12 s with a weight shift, a foot-to-foot weight shift over a 4 to 8 s cycle, fidgets on a timer of 30 to 60 s, and breath rates of 15 to 20 per minute relaxed, 20 to 25 alert and 25 to 30 in a combat idle. On R6 use a fidget timer of 30 to 60 s, randomised. The vendor's 4 to 8 s weight shift is much faster than the measured small shifts (about every 25 s) and full shifts (minutes). principles.md, "Idle or held stance", likewise asks for a weight shift to the other leg every three to six loops. Use those compressed cadences for a player idle the player sees for seconds at a time, and the measured cadence for an NPC watched for minutes. No measured breath rate for a combat stance was found; the vendor's 25 to 30 per minute is a starting value.
- **Idle breaks wait for stillness and reset on movement.** Opsive's Ultimate Character Controller starts its idle variations only after the character has been grounded and still for a start delay of 15 s by default; moving or leaving the ground resets the timer, a random pick may repeat the last variation, and a sequential option rotates them. An "AFK Animation System" posted for feedback on the DevForum plays a random idle after 3 s without input, one at a time, waiting for each to finish (a reply notes that its clips came from another game). On R6 start the first break 10 to 15 s after the character stops (our choice between the two), reset on any input or move, never pick the same break twice in a row, and hand back to the main idle through `onDone` (pipeline.md). The native Animate script only picks idles by weight (r6-craft.md, "Idle variations play by weight"), so this scheduling is controller code.

## What reads from the player camera

- **Real sway is about one pixel; readable life starts near a degree.** Roblox's `Camera.FieldOfView` defaults to 70 degrees vertical (creator docs). On a 1080-pixel-tall view a stud at distance d then spans about 771 / d pixels: 77 at 10 studs and 39 at 20 (our derivation). The measured COM RMS sway (0.012 to 0.014 studs) is about one pixel at 10 studs, and the measured joint SDs (0.2 to 0.3 degrees) move the end of a 2-stud part about 0.01 studs, under a pixel. A 1 degree torso pitch moves the head top, 2 studs above the torso centre, about 0.035 studs: 2.7 pixels at 10 studs and 1.4 at 20. On R6 give anything meant to be seen at least 1 degree of rotation on the torso or head or 0.03 studs of translation (about 2 pixels at 10 studs), and carry the read with the breath and the weight shifts, the largest slow layers. The traced commissioned idle in principles.md, "Idle or held stance" (torso about 1.3 degrees, head up to 4.8, arms 4 to 6, torso height 0.04 studs), sits above these floors.
- **Roblox animators reject idles that look like a still image.** On the DevForum thread "Idle Animation feedback!" (2025; a shopkeeper idle for a battlegrounds-style game), the first replies were "I thought it was a still image for the first few seconds" and "Even with the animation playing it is hard to tell if your character is moving". Reviewers asked for a more frequent and slightly more exaggerated bob, the head down on the exhale and up on the inhale, the torso bobbing slowly with the arms and head following, and both legs moved forward so their tops stay on the body while the feet stay in place. They also asked for constant motion instead of an idle that "moves then pauses for a moment then moves again". The update was called "way, way better". Other threads praise idles that "move in arcs" ("R6 Animations Feedback"), fault one that looks "quite linear and not really smooth" and tell its maker to take "inspiration from how you stand idle" ("[R6] Floating Idle Animations"), and advise moving the torso up and down "to simulate the chest heaving" while the legs stay still ("Voting Poll: Sword Idle Animations"). On R6 meet the floors above, keep `Poser.check` at frozen 0 with no pause between beats, move the head with the breath, and let `Feet.flat` slide the legs as the torso sinks so their tops follow the body.

## Does not transfer to R6

- The fast (0.5 to 1.0 Hz) and very fast (1.0 to 2.5 Hz) sway components, under 3 mm of COP (Yamamoto et al.): under 0.01 studs, below a pixel from the player camera.
- Joint-angle SDs of 0.2 to 0.3 degrees in still standing (Wang et al.) as amplitudes: below a pixel; R6 uses the larger `life` instead.
- Ankle, knee and hip sway strategies and the ankle-angle changes with emotion (Wang et al., Takahashi et al.): R6 has no ankle or knee and one torso for pelvis and chest; sway shows only as the torso moving over planted feet.
- Chest and abdominal expansion in breathing (abdominal SD of 1.6 to 2.4 cm, Hagio et al.): parts cannot scale; the breath lives in torso pitch and sink.
- The 25 to 40 ms eye lead and the eyes counter-rotating while the head finishes (Hooge et al., Freedman): the default R6 head has no moving eyes, and the lead is under 3 frames.
- Pupil changes around sighs (Andrews et al.): no eyes.
- Perlin and Goldberg's arm noise with the forearm at twice the upper arm's frequency: there is no elbow; the R6 arm is one part.
- Blinks and a yawning mouth on a classic decal face: no eyelids or jaw; on a Dynamic Head they need new runtime code (Head and gaze).

## Not covered

- Chest wall and shoulder displacement in millimetres during quiet breathing: no open source with usable numbers was found.
- Head movement durations and peak speeds for small natural glances of 5 to 40 degrees: the open sources gave numbers only for large or as-fast-as-possible gaze shifts.
- The size and duration of single shifts, fidgets and drifts in Duarte and Zatsiorsky (1999): the paper is not open access and the lab's own copies were unreachable (the host closed the connection).
- Wiltman and Chambers (2017) on weight shifting and discomfort during prolonged standing: not open access.
- Breathing recovery after a sprint or a fight: only a six-minute walk test was found.
- The length of the pause at the end of a resting exhale.
- Head sway in quiet standing in millimetres.
- GDC talks, film animators' write-ups or studio blogs on idle systems with readable text: none found in this run. Polycount idle threads refused the fetch (403), so their advice was not used; AnimSchool's idle post repeats principles.md's macro variation over three to six loops and adds no number.
