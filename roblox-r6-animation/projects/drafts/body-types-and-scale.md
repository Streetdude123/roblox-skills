# Body types, weight and scale on six parts

How size, mass, age, strength, load and fatigue change motion, and how to give each R6 character its own timing and posture when every R6 body is the same six blocks. Every finding was checked against its source (sources.md, "Body types, weight and scale on six parts research"). S is a character's scale against the stock R6 rig (`Torso.Size.Y / 2`, r6-mechanics.md, "Scaled rigs"); a stock R6 has S = 1, a 2 stud leg and its soles 1 stud apart. Frame counts are at 60 fps. The square-root timing rule itself is in body-mechanics.md, "Gravity, jumps and falls on Roblox" ("Timing tells size"); this file holds the evidence behind it and what else changes with size and build.

## Size and timing: dynamic similarity

- **Equal Froude numbers make different sizes move alike.** R. McNeill Alexander (2004, Journal of Anatomy, "Bipedal animals, and their differences from humans"): where gravity matters, two systems move in a dynamically similar way only at equal Froude numbers, speed squared over (length times gravity). Alexander (1976) and Alexander and Jayes (1983) proposed that similar animals of different sizes at equal Froude numbers move alike, with equal relative stride lengths and equal duty factors; the length they used is the hip height. On R6: the hip height is the leg, 2 S studs. To build a character at scale S from a stock cycle, keep the stock Froude number: multiply the speed by sqrt(S), the stride per cycle by S and the cycle time by sqrt(S), and keep the duty. The stock walk of pipeline.md, "Custom r6-style rigs" (4.8 studs/s, a 0.84 s cycle, duty 0.59), becomes 9.6 studs/s on a 1.68 s cycle at S = 4, still at duty 0.59 (our calculation).
- **The walk-run switch moves with the square root of leg length.** Kram, Domingo and Ferris (1997, Journal of Experimental Biology): humans and other bipeds with different leg lengths switch from a walk to a run at different absolute speeds but at about the same Froude number, 0.5; under simulated low gravity the switch came at slower speeds but at about the same Froude number. Usherwood et al. (2018, Journal of Biomechanics; 487 participants, mostly 11 to 18 years old) put the adult switch at a dimensionless speed `v / sqrt(g L)` of 0.75, a Froude number of 0.56. On R6: locomotion.md, "Speeds, cadence and the walk-run boundary", gives 5.9 studs/s for the stock leg at 35 studs/s^2. At scale S the switch is 5.9 sqrt(S) studs/s: about 4.6 at S = 0.6, 8.4 at S = 2 and 11.8 at S = 4 (our calculation). Give each character its walk or run clip from its own switch speed, never from the stock 5.9.
- **Froude scaling fixes the duty factor better than the stride.** Donelan and Kram (1997, Journal of Experimental Biology): at equal Froude numbers under 0.25 g to 1.0 g, subjects walked with nearly the same duty factor, but their relative stride lengths differed by as much as 67%, so the authors rejected full dynamic similarity. They note that a small child must run to keep up with an adult who walks at a comfortable pace. On R6: trust the scaling for the gait choice and the planted share (a scaled walk keeps 55 to 60%, SKILL.md, "Walks need double support"). Set the stride from the scaled leg's reach instead: a stance of about 2.4 S studs (SKILL.md, "Locomotion speed"), measured with `_G.feet` on the scaled rig. When a child-sized character keeps pace with an adult, play its run while the adult walks.
- **Games and film slow big things by the square root of their scale.** Tore Knabe (game developer, 2022): pose and time the creature at human scale, scale it up, then divide the animation speed by the square root of the scale factor, because a creature x times as big needs sqrt(x) times as long for its centre of mass to fall x times as far; multiply its movement speed by sqrt(x). Film miniatures use the same law: frame rate = 24 x sqrt(D/d), so a 1/48 model is shot at about 166 fps and a 1/36 model at 144 fps (Cinematography Mailing List, Geoff Boyle and others). On R6: play the stock-scale clip on the scaled rig with `rig:play(clip, {speed = 1 / math.sqrt(S)})` (0.71 at S = 2, 0.58 at S = 3, 0.5 at S = 4, 1.41 at S = 0.5) and set its WalkSpeed to the clip's built speed times sqrt(S). Poser steps springs with the clip-time advance and samples `life` and `lag` in clip time (`Poser.lua`, `step`), so the same `speed` slows them by the same factor with no retuning. locomotion.md, "Frames per step", says a giant takes about one step per second; from a stock walk stepping every 0.42 s that is S of about 5.7 (our calculation). The speed factor is only the start: Tanaka found a stretched clip still looked light ("Giants and small bodies: mass, momentum and detail").
- **Viewers read size from stride frequency.** Jokisch and Troje (2003, Journal of Vision): point-light dogs with high stride frequencies were judged smaller than ones with low frequencies, and the judged size followed the inverse quadratic relation between stride frequency and size that pendulum mechanics predicts. On R6: where every avatar is the same size, cadence alone can make one character read bigger. A step rate 0.85 times another character's reads about 1.4 times as big; 0.8 times reads about 1.6 times (our use of the inverse square). A rigid leg plants only about 2.4 studs per stance, so a slower cadence at the same stride means a slower game speed for that character: set its WalkSpeed from the clip.
- **Stride frequency falls with mass by a small power.** Heglund and Taylor (1988, Journal of Experimental Biology; 16 quadruped species from 30 g mice to 200 kg horses): the minimum, preferred and maximum sustained speeds within a trot and a gallop scale with about the 0.2 power of body mass, nine-fold apart from mice to horses; the stride frequencies at those equivalent speeds scale with about the -0.15 power of mass. On R6: at a fixed shape, mass grows with S^3, so M^-0.15 is S^-0.45, close to the 1/sqrt(S) of the speed rule above (our derivation). Use one rate factor, 1/sqrt(S), for the whole character.
- **Strength grows with area, weight with volume, so giants jump low.** J. B. S. Haldane (essay "On Being the Right Size"): a giant ten times as high, wide and thick as a man weighs a thousand times as much, but its bones have only a hundred times the cross-section, so each square inch of bone carries ten times the load; the height an animal can jump is "more nearly independent of its size than proportional to it" (a flea about two feet, a man about five). Masanobu Tanaka (GenDesign CTO, animator on Shadow of the Colossus and animation director on The Last Guardian, CEDEC 2021 talk "5 Rules for Animating Giant Characters"): at three times the height a body has 27 times the mass but 9 times the bone and muscle strength, so the bigger it is, the harder it is to hold up and move; a stretched human run on his giant model jumped too high for a giant. On R6: let a giant leap about the same height in studs as a stock R6, not S times higher: keep the Humanoid's JumpHeight (7.2 studs by default, body-mechanics.md, "Gravity, jumps and falls on Roblox") or lower it, and key a giant's hops at stock heights. A small character jumping the same studs reads as springy, which suits it.

## Building a character at another size

- **Every offset scales by S; rotations carry over.** DevForum (2019, "R6 walking animation doesn't scale down correctly"): on a scaled-down R6 NPC the walk put the limbs farther from the body than at normal size; a reply said the scaled joints' C0 differ, so each scale needs its own animation. A 2024 thread on miniature R6 characters reports a walk whose right leg did not look attached, even with a walk authored on the miniature rig. In a 2021 thread on scaled characters, translations "(not rotation)" went off: a big boss was flung further than intended and sank into the ground, and a small character moved less. On R6: Poser writes `Motor6D.Transform` itself, so nothing rescales its keys (our reading of pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). Multiply every `p` in the clip, every `Feet.flat` target's x, z and lift, and every root travel curve by S; keep every `r`. An imported stock clip scales on load with `Poser.fromSequence(kfs, Poser.wrapsOf(rig), {pScale = S})`. `Feet.lua` has the stock leg geometry built in (r6-mechanics.md, "Scaled rigs", lists what scales), so planting a scaled rig needs a scale input in `Feet.lua`: new runtime code. Scale the limb-gap limit too (0.12 studs at S = 1, SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference"; our reading).
- **The Animator scales baked clips by the model's scale, with known faults.** Creator docs (`Model:GetScale`): a model's scale factor "will be applied to joint offsets of Animations played on an AnimationController under that model". For Humanoid characters, a developer replying in the 2021 thread (EmilyBendsSpace) wrote that the animation system assumes clips authored on a standard R15 with HipHeight 2.0 and scales their translations by HipHeight / 2.0, because a published animation stores no rig scale. In 2023 a developer fixed an animation on a scaled-down character with `Model:ScaleTo`. A 2026 bug report says animations glitch on characters scaled above 1 (giant NPCs that once walked well now walk glitchy); the workaround posted is `Workspace.Retargeting` set to Disabled (the docs: retargeting "adapts animations to character rigs of different proportions"). On R6: size characters with `Model:ScaleTo`, bake clips on a stock rig (`Poser.bake`) and let the Animator scale them. Then play the baked clip on the scaled character and compare the torso's offset at one key with S times its authored `p`; test with `Workspace.Retargeting` at Default and at Disabled before shipping a giant.

## Giants and small bodies: mass, momentum and detail

- **Heavy is slow to change speed, not slow.** Tanaka: mass is "the degree to which a body keeps its current speed". A truck cannot start, stop or turn suddenly; a small insect can. When Trico brakes hard it keeps moving forward for a while; a small bird stops and starts at once. Mass does not set top speed (a 350 t jumbo jet flies at 890 km/h), so top speed must not differ by mass. (gameanim.com's English summary of the talk says a large mass can stop suddenly; the slides say it cannot, and this file follows the slides.) David Clayton (animation supervisor, Weta Digital, on Rampage): big creatures "can move quickly, but they need some time to generate that speed, and they need some time to wash off that speed". A DevForum answer on a heavy, grounded brawler asks for momentum: a skid and a turning circle instead of instant direction changes, and a slow stop instead of Roblox's instant one. On R6: keep a heavy or giant character's top speed and lengthen its starts and stops: reach full speed over 2 to 3 steps with the lean growing each step, and brake through a skid step with the braced leg planted ahead (SKILL.md, "Push before speed, stopper before stop") while the torso travels on 0.3 to 0.6 S studs past the plant (our starting values). Root acceleration belongs to the movement system: offer a momentum controller as a question (SKILL.md, opening paragraph).
- **Smooth arcs read heavy, broken arcs read light.** Tanaka: a large mass makes a point's path smooth; a small mass lets it bend sharply. Sudden starts and stops express small things (small birds, insects, fingers, faces); smoothed paths express heavy weapons, trucks and giants. On R6: on a giant, print the hand, foot and head-top paths (SKILL.md, "Arcs and paths") and remove every corner: no `flat` key inside a path and no change of direction within 6 x sqrt(S) frames (our starting value). On a small or light character, allow corners: a `flat` key and a restart 2 to 3 frames later on a head turn, a hop or a dart reads quick and light.
- **A heavy part speeds up and slows down later.** Tanaka: living things move with optimal efficiency; the head is heavy, so it accelerates and decelerates more slowly than an arm, and each part speeds up and slows down to suit its mass. On R6: ease each part by its share of the body (r6-mechanics.md, "Centre of mass and balance": torso 0.497, head 0.081, each arm 0.050): into a held pose, give the torso the longest settle, the head the next, the arms the shortest, for example 12, 9 and 6 frames at S = 1 (our starting values; the `speed` rule above stretches them on a giant). The head still starts a turn first (SKILL.md, "Turn the head; do not slide it"); on a giant it starts first and arrives late.
- **Gravity does not scale: keep the bob's acceleration.** Tanaka: gravity is the same for every object whatever its mass, so animation time cannot simply be stretched; a jog's airborne hips roughly follow the gravity parabola; a sprint with a big up-and-down motion makes gravity look stronger and the runner look like a miniature, so the vertical range must shrink at a sprint. Clayton: Weta puts a falling "gravity cube" with a calculated parabola in a shot to check that a jumping or falling character drops at the right rate; once George grows, "when he jumps and lands and moves through frame, it's going to be slower". On R6: on a scaled character, scale the torso bob by S and the cycle by sqrt(S) so the bob's acceleration (amplitude over period squared) stays the stock value; a bigger bob at the slower timing reads as strong gravity and a toy (our derivation). Give a sprint a smaller bob than a jog. Fit a giant's airborne COM to the viewer's gravity as r6-mechanics.md, "Centre of mass and balance" (flight), describes.
- **A giant lifts its heavy leg briefly and low.** Tanaka: his giant run, even with its paths smoothed, still looked light because it moved its own heavy limbs easily; a leg is about 15% of body weight (6 t for a giant he estimates at 40 t), so lifting it takes the whole body's muscles, and the time the leg is lifted should be short. On R6: raise a giant walk's planted share to about 0.65 and keep the swing foot's lift at about half the stock lift times S (our starting values). Roll the torso 2 to 3 degrees toward the stance leg as the swing foot leaves the floor, so the body is seen working to lift the leg (our reading).
- **A slowed clip loses detail: add contact detail back.** Tanaka: a slowed motion goes blurry like an enlarged low-resolution photo, so add detail back: the shake when a running foot hits the ground, the instant of the push in a jump, the shock travelling through the body. Where 1/30 or 1/60 s frames cannot show it, stretch the time a little and cheat. A shake must slow down as it dies out, not stay fast; a little stage trickery (keren-mi) helps but is physically a lie, so use it sparingly. On R6: on each giant foot contact, key a torso `p.y` dip of 0.04 to 0.08 S studs and two rebounds, each smaller and longer than the last (about 3, 5 and 8 frames), with the arms and head 1 to 2 frames behind (our starting values). Key it at stock timing and put a `warp` of about sqrt(S) on those frames, so the shake plays close to stock speed inside the slowed clip; `Poser.realTime` then gives the event times.
- **Planted feet hold; airborne limbs hang loose.** Tanaka: show the difference between a loaded and an unloaded limb. A foot in the air gets an exaggerated rotation to show that no force acts on it; a planted foot is fixed firmly and sinks a little. On R6: a sole cannot sink into the floor (SKILL.md, "Feet rule"), so sink the body: drop the torso 0.03 to 0.06 S studs over the 3 to 4 frames after each contact while `Feet.flat` keeps both soles flat. Let the swing leg overshoot its forward angle 5 to 10 degrees past the stock walk before it plants (our starting values).
- **A giant cannot snap between poses: layer instead.** Tanaka: a giant cannot change pose suddenly, and even a small change shows from the player's view. Damage can start at any time from any pose, so The Last Guardian layered additive animations: small pose changes, the same first and last pose, as short as possible. The layer moved planted feet, so the feet were fixed by IK while planted and the centre-of-mass shift that caused was handled. On R6: Poser plays one clip per rig; build a giant's flinch while walking as the explicit composite of pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays": the walk's legs and `Feet.flat` stay, the flinch rides on the torso, arms and head as offsets under a weight envelope, short, starting and ending at zero offset. A general additive layer in Poser is new runtime code.
- **A big body's ripples settle slowly.** Clayton: if a big creature stops suddenly or hits something, "the ripple effect caused by that sudden stop needs to take place slowly. That's what gives you the cue that it's big." In a crane swing, momentum "slowly built up speed" until the crane hit, then the energy passed to the target "almost like those little Newton's cradle swinging balls". On R6: after a giant's stop or slam, keep the arms, head and props moving and settling over shrinking bounces. With `speed = 1 / math.sqrt(S)`, a `follow` spring (6 Hz at S = 1) already acts at 6 / sqrt(S) Hz. Build a heavy swing's speed over the whole wind-up on `auto` keys with no snap, and hold the contact by strength (combat.md, "Hitstop").
- **Small bodies start, stop and turn at once.** Tanaka: a small mass can stop, start and turn suddenly, and its paths may break. Knabe's rule runs the other way for small bodies: at S = 0.5 the clip plays 1.41 times faster. On R6: on a small character (a pet, a sprite, a shrunken player), play at `1 / math.sqrt(S)`, keep holds short, allow `flat` stops on the head and hands, and put the hop of r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons", at stock height so it reads big against the small body (our reading).

## Heavy bodies

- **A heavy body walks slower on a wider base.** Ko, Stenholm and Ferrucci (2010, Journal of Biomechanics; 164 adults aged 50 to 84 in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging): preferred speed 1.20 m/s at normal weight, 1.13 overweight, 1.06 obese; stride width 10.0, 10.6 and 12.4 cm; stance 63.3, 63.7 and 64.5% of the cycle. The obese absorbed more mechanical work at the hip in the side-to-side plane, and earlier studies also found slower speed and wider steps. On R6: for a heavy build, widen the foot targets in the same ratio (x from 0.5 to about 0.62 on each side, our scaling of the 1.24 ratio), walk about 12% slower, and move the torso further over each stance foot: torso `p.x` sway up to about 1.5 times the 0.17 stud walk range of locomotion.md, "Walk body motion", plus a roll of 2 to 4 degrees toward the planted foot (our starting values). The 1.2% longer stance is under a frame per step; leave the duty.
- **A boss walk reads heavy when it slows, sways and drops.** DevForum feedback on a Roblox boss walk ("Bricklord", 2024): "more of a delicate sashay than a menacing boss saunter. Try slowing down the movement overall by at least 50% and add more dramatic swings from side to side to feel like Bricklord is stomping instead of tiptoeing"; others asked for a torso bounce, a forward tilt (it leaned back), a bigger torso twist, and pointed out that a still torso under moving limbs looks wrong. On R6: for a heavy character at stock size, build the walk for 0.5 to 0.7 of the brisk walk's speed with a matching WalkSpeed (no slide), roll the torso 4 to 6 degrees toward each stance foot, drop torso `p.y` into each contact, pitch forward 4 to 8 degrees from the waist and widen the twist to about twice locomotion.md's plus or minus 3.5 degrees (our starting values). locomotion.md, "Walk body motion", warns that a big torso twist reads as a plod: that is the read a heavy boss wants, so keep the stock twist for an ordinary walk. SKILL.md's exaggeration row agrees: push the torso's twist, lean and drop, not the leg lift.
- **Stomps need a cue on the contact frame.** DevForum ("Haha Mech go stomp", 2020): a small sine and cosine sway and up-and-down bounce gave a procedural mech "that big stompy feel"; reviewers asked for footstep sounds, particles on the ground material and camera shake when the foot touches the ground. On R6: put an event on each contact key (`events = {stepR = 0.42, stepL = 0.84}`, read through `Poser.realTime` on a warped clip) so the sound and the dust fire on the frame the sole lands. Particles and camera shake are VFX and camera work: offer them as a question (SKILL.md, opening paragraph).
- **Added mass lengthens contact, shrinks the back kick and adds lean.** Xu et al. (2017, BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders; four women running with a weight vest): with 30% of body weight added, stance rose from 37.8 to 43.2% of the cycle, peak vertical ground force from 2.7 to 3.1 body weights, peak hip extension fell from 23.2 to 10.5 degrees and peak hip flexion before terminal swing rose from 33.8 to 45.8; all subjects leaned forward to make up for the lost hip extension, and the knee showed no systematic change. On R6: in a heavy run, keep each foot down about 29% of the cycle instead of 25% (about 1.5 more frames per contact on a 0.68 s cycle), kick the back leg to about half the stock -50 degrees (the source's ratio is 0.45), and lean the torso 4 to 8 degrees further (our starting value); keep the front swing at the stock limit (SKILL.md, "Runs are judged by the legs").

## Load carriage

- **A pack bends the trunk and shortens the steps.** Liew, Morris and Netto (2016, Journal of Applied Biomechanics; systematic review of 54 studies): walking with a backpack increased the trunk flexion angle, the hip and ankle ranges of motion, the vertical and horizontal ground forces and the cadence, and reduced stride length. Knapik, Harman and Reynolds (1996, Applied Ergonomics review): heavier pack loads raise ground forces, knee flexion and forward trunk inclination; a load close to the body's centre of gravity costs least, and a double pack (half in front, half behind) changes walking less than a backpack. On R6: lean from the combined COM as body-mechanics.md, "Carrying, pushing and lifting", describes; then shorten the step about 10% and raise the cadence to keep the speed, and deepen the torso dip of each stance by 0.05 to 0.1 studs for the extra knee bend (our starting values). A load split front and back gets less lean than a backpack of the same mass.
- **A loaded child stiffens and slows.** Singh and Koh (two 2009 studies of 15 and 17 children aged about 10, packs of 10, 15 and 20% of body weight): above 15%, pelvic and trunk rotation fell and upper-body stiffness rose (Journal of Biomechanics); at 20%, walking speed and cadence fell and double support time rose, more with the load low on the back (Gait and Posture). On R6: under a heavy pack, halve the torso twist and the arm swing, and add about 0.05 to the planted share (our starting values). The adult review found a higher cadence and these children a lower one: give a loaded adult the quicker step, a loaded child the slower one (both kept).

## Strength and effort

- **The lift's duration sells the weight.** Hamilton, Joyce, Flanagan, Frith and Wolpert (2007, Psychological Research): observers judged a box's weight mainly from the duration of the lift, and less from the grasp or the place; the early lift (lift-off to peak speed) counted more than the late lift, and even the first 125 ms of the lift moved the judgement, while the grasp did not. Their standard lift lasted 876 ms and was resampled from 75 to 125%. In real lifting, the grasp duration was the best predictor of the true weight. On R6: to make a carried object read heavy, lengthen the hands' acceleration off the surface: keep the first 8 frames (125 ms) of the lift slow and put the speed peak late, or stretch that segment with a `warp` of 0.8 (1.25 times as long) and leave the slowing into the hold. A light object leaves fast. R6 has no fingers to grip, so a 4 to 6 frame moving hold of the hands on the object stands in for the grasp (our reading).
- **Force breaks the curve; too much drag reads weak.** Tanaka: muscles can only pull and stiffen when they work. A path changes only for a reason, and muscle is the main one: a sudden change in the curve shows force, a smooth curve shows a relaxed body. The drag that makes motion supple (nokoshi, a part left behind) makes a character look powerless when overused. On R6: on a strong character's effort beat, break the curve on purpose with a `flat` key or a short named ease into the effort pose, and no `lag` on the working parts (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain", already keeps it off the acting limb). Hold carried-part `lag` at 1 frame (0.017 s) on a strong, tense character and give the 2 to 3 frames and `drag` springs to a relaxed or weak one (our mapping onto SKILL.md's 1 to 3 frame range).

## Children

- **Young children have no clean walk-run switch.** Usherwood et al. (2018): children aged 1.1 to 4.7 years (leg length 0.31 to 0.525 m), free to choose their gait, showed no discrete walk-run transition; their duty factor and the skew of their ground force changed steadily with speed. Taller and older people switch at the dimensionless speed 0.75. On R6: for a toddler-sized character, do not swap a walk clip for a run clip at one speed. Drive one procedural cycle whose planted share falls steadily from about 0.6 to 0.35 as speed rises, reading the speed from `ctx` as the locomotion starter does (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"; our starting values). Keep the swap at the switch speed for adults.
- **New walkers hold the arms high and the feet wide.** Gimunová et al. (2021, International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health; 26 toddlers): after 1 to 5 weeks of independent walking the arm's peak angle from the body (hip, shoulder and wrist points) was 78 to 87 degrees; after 11 to 15 weeks it was 47 to 49 (the "high guard" lowers). Step width fell from 0.19 to 0.12 m while step length stayed 0.18 m, the left hip's range fell from about 42 to 29 degrees, and the trunk's forward-back range from 8.5 to 5.9. Kubo and Ulrich (2006): the arms came down as the toddlers walked faster. On R6: for a first-steps toddler, raise both arms 70 to 85 degrees from hanging, out to the sides and a little forward, and hold them there with small motion; set the soles as far apart as a step is long; pitch the torso through about 8 degrees per stride (our mapping). Lower the arms to 45 to 50 degrees for a child who has walked for months. Keep each arm on its shoulder (SKILL.md, "Feet rule", the arm contact).
- **Children take more steps for their size.** Voss et al. (2020, Gait and Posture; 164 participants aged 5 to 30): absolute stride length and speed rose with age, while cadence (absolute and scaled by size) and speed scaled by size fell; at a fast walk, children aged 5 to 8 had longer stance and double support and shorter swing than 11 to 13 year olds (and, at 5 to 6, than young adults). Thevenon et al. (2015, Annals of Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine; 382 children aged 6 to 12): cadence fell with height, the timing values rose with height from 110 to 130 cm and then levelled off, and overweight children had a longer stance and more double support. On R6: a child on a stock-size R6 reads younger with shorter, quicker steps at the same speed, about 15 to 25% more steps per second, and a slightly larger planted share at speed (our starting values).

## Older adults

- **An older walk: slower cadence, longer double support, wider steps.** Letournel et al. (2025, Bioengineering; 36 community-dwelling adults, mean age 74, Xsens suits): cadence 88 and 97 steps per minute (left and right) against an adult mean of 113; double support 53% of the cycle against about 20% for healthy adults (the authors' manual heel and toe marking raises their values); wider steps, lower speed and shorter strides than adult norms. The suits slowed the walk itself (1.2 m/s in the six-minute walk test, 0.5 with the suits on). On R6: step an older NPC every 37 to 41 frames instead of about 32 (60 / 97 to 60 / 88 s per step; our conversion), plant each foot 65 to 70% of the cycle and widen the soles a little (our starting values). locomotion.md, "Frames per step", gives 50 frames for "an elderly or tired walk" from Williams' chart: use the measured 37 to 41 for a healthy older person and Williams' 50 for a frail or tired read (both kept).
- **Older walkers lose hip extension, push-off and arm swing.** Kerrigan et al. (1998, Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation; 31 healthy adults aged 65 to 84 against 31 young adults): the differences that remained at both comfortable and fast speeds were reduced peak hip extension, increased forward pelvic tilt, and reduced ankle plantarflexion and push-off power. Khiyara, Sidaway and Hejrati (2026): reduced arm swing is frequent in older adults, and restricting arm swing shortens the stride and slows the walk. On R6: cut the back leg's swing behind the hip to 60 to 70% of a young walk's, pitch the torso 3 to 6 degrees forward from the waist through the whole cycle (the single torso part shows the pelvic tilt as a whole-body pitch), flatten the torso bob a little for the weaker push-off, and halve the arm swing (our starting values). For a frail character add the hunch of r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons".

## Fatigue and limps

- **Tired runners lean, shorten their steps and sway.** Winter, Gordon and Watt (2017, Journal of Sports Medicine and Physical Fitness; systematic review of 12 studies): during fatiguing runs, speed, step frequency and step length fell, trunk flexion rose, side-to-side acceleration and the hip and knee ranges changed, and stride regularity fell. Rubio et al. (2026): after 5 km of running, or 5 km of walking with 22.7 kg at 1.5 m/s, peak trunk flexion rose in 18 of 20 women, with an effect size of 0.80 or more (the authors' mark of a meaningful change, not a significance test). On R6: a tired run lowers the cadence and the stride 5 to 10%, leans 4 to 6 degrees further from the waist, lifts the swing leg into the hip 0.5 to 0.6 studs instead of 0.7 to 0.95, and doubles the torso's side sway (our starting values). Blend toward it over a long chase with a procedural Torso joint that reads a stamina value from `ctx` (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract").
- **Fatigue widens the steps and breaks the repeat.** Helbostad et al. (2007, Journals of Gerontology; older people aged about 78 to 80, tired by repeated sit-to-stands): preferred speed and step length did not change, but step width, side-to-side trunk acceleration and step-length variability rose. On R6: a fatigued walk keeps its speed, widens each foot target about 0.05 studs and adds side sway (our starting values). A one-stride Poser loop repeats exactly, so author a two-stride loop whose four steps differ by 5 to 10% in length and by 1 to 2 frames in timing (our starting values).
- **A limp shortens the stance on the painful leg.** Auerbach and Tadi (StatPearls, "Antalgic Gait in Adults"): antalgic gait is walking altered by pain into a limp "whereby the stance phase is shortened relative to the swing phase"; healthy gait is cyclical and symmetric. On R6: break the mirror of locomotion.md, "Walk landmarks and mirrors", on purpose: plant the hurt leg for about 0.45 of the cycle and the good leg for about 0.65, overlap the two windows so one foot is always down, hurry the body off the hurt leg, and drop the torso deeper on the good leg's contact (our starting values). Each `Feet.flat` target is its own function of time, so the asymmetry needs no new code.
- **Blend a limp in by health.** DevForum ("Low HP limping effect", 2022): a developer blended the normal walk with an edited limping walk, setting the limp's animation weight to match health below 50 HP; the blend worked only once the limp's priority was above Movement, the default Animate script's priority. On R6: Poser plays one clip per rig, so drive the limp from one procedural walk whose leg timing and torso offsets read a hurt value from 0 to 1 in `ctx`, or bake both walks and blend the tracks with native Animator weights at a priority above Movement (pipeline.md, "Native Animator playback").

## One weight class across a clip set

- **Fix the weight class first, then derive every clip from it.** Tanaka: estimate a giant's weight from creatures of similar size (a 27 m character at about 40 t from sauropod sizes, a leg at 15% or 6 t) and picture it before animating. Clayton: early motion studies of George at his different sizes, leaping off cars beside a gravity indicator, got the client to accept that the bigger he grew, the slower his jumps and landings would be. On R6: before keying the set, write the class into the clip plan (templates/clip-plan.md): S, the build (light, average, heavy), the rate factor 1/sqrt(S), the planted share, the foot lift, the contact sink, the lean, the arm swing and the spring presets. Build one test clip (a start, a stop, a jump and a landing), get it accepted, then take every clip's numbers (idle, locomotion, attacks, hits) from that table so no clip plays lighter than its neighbours (our method). anime-craft.md, "Contrast across a scene", covers breaking the scale on purpose once it is set.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Ankle push-off and plantarflexion changes with age, weight and load (Kerrigan et al., Ko et al., Xu et al.): there is no ankle; they show only as a shorter back swing and a flatter bob.
- Forward pelvic tilt apart from the chest (Kerrigan et al.): one torso part; only a whole-torso pitch shows it.
- The grasp of the fingers (Hamilton et al.): the R6 hand is the end of the arm block; a short hold of the hands stands in.
- Extra knee bend under a load (Knapik et al.): a stance-knee slide that small does not read on a rigid leg (human-motion-data.md, "Does not transfer to R6"); a deeper torso dip stands in.
- Body shape by type: a child's big head, a heavy body's girth, a giant's thick limbs. Parts cannot scale or change shape in a clip; a different build needs different meshes, and the motion must carry the read.
- Muscle that hardens under effort (Tanaka): R6 parts do not deform; only the break in the curve shows force.
- Loose clothes and long hair to show scale (Knabe): R6 has no cloth; only a rig with extra motored parts can swing them on springs.
- Falling dust, particles, fog and camera shake to sell size (Knabe, the mech thread, Clayton): VFX, lighting and camera work, not animation; offer them as a question.

## Not covered

- Children's cadence by age in steps per minute: only the directions in the Voss et al. and Thevenon et al. abstracts were read.
- Trunk flexion angles in degrees for older adults, for pack loads and for fatigue: the open sources read gave directions or effect sizes, not angles.
- Arm carriage and arm swing of heavy people: no source found.
- Turning radius or stopping distance by body mass: no source found.
- Posts or portfolios by R6 animators on giant, child, old or heavy R6 characters: DevForum searches found a boss walk critique, scaling bug threads and a limp blend thread, but no R6 animator's method for these body types.
- GDC talks on small creatures or heavy humans: only Tanaka's CEDEC talk on giants was found and read.
- Animation Mentor and AnimSchool articles on weight: the fetches were blocked (Cloudflare and 403). iAnimate, Animator Island and Keith Lango pages on weight were not found.
- Jokisch and Troje's full text: the journal page needs JavaScript; the abstract and BioMotionLab's demo page were used.
- Landing sink and recovery by body mass: no measured source; anime-craft.md, "Action beats" ("The length of the impact sets the weight"), keeps its light and heavy starting numbers.
- Strain trembles and fatigue tremor in numbers: no source found.
