# Polish and critique

This file holds the polish techniques and review habits that professional animators and leads use to lift a clip from correct to good, the notes students and Roblox animators receive most, and how each one reads on six rigid R6 parts in Poser. Every finding was checked against its source (sources.md, "Polish and critique: what separates professional motion research"). Film sources count at 24 fps; their 60 fps values multiply by 2.5 and are marked "our conversion". Values marked "starting values" are this file's suggestions for R6, not numbers from the source.

## Spacing and favouring

- **Favour one end of a move, not both.** Victor Navone (Pixar), "Splinophilia": he makes an ease by moving the inner key of a hold to 85 to 95% of the change between the two poses and smoothing its tangent. An identical ease-in and ease-out makes the spacing symmetrical, "which again is not very interesting. It's better to pick one of the poses to favor." A curve that passes beyond the second pose gives an overshoot, not an ease. On R6: two `flat` extremes with `auto` between them give exactly that symmetrical ease. Decide per move which end it favours. A settle into a guard favours the arrival: one `auto` key 3 to 5 frames before the end at 85 to 95% of the change on each moving channel. A slam favours the departure: one key 3 to 5 frames after the start at 5 to 15% of the change, and no ease into the contact (starting values). The two-sided favour pair in lesson-videos.md, "The same tricks in Poser (our translation, 60 fps)", is for a flick that snaps at both ends.
- **The breakdown sets the timing, not the default curve.** Shawn Kelly (Animation Mentor, Tips and Tricks Volume 1, "Key poses, breakdowns and in-betweens"): with only key poses, the computer's smooth interpolation gives a bell curve "with perfectly smooth ease-ins and ease-outs in all the wrong places". His ball lands on frame 1, peaks on frame 5 and lands again on frame 10; he drags frame 3 up toward the peak, so the ball covers more distance between frames 1 and 2 than between 3 and 4 and eases into the top. A breakdown either makes an ease or carries body mechanics (an arc, overlap). On R6: give every segment between two extremes at least one breakdown whose value is off the midpoint. A rise (a jump's torso `py`, an arm lifting) puts its breakdown at 70 to 80% of the travel at half the time, so it slows into the top; a drop (an arm falling, a slam) puts it at 20 to 30%, so it speeds into the bottom (starting values). Print the per-frame values: equal steps mean the segment is still even.
- **Subdivide a cycle by halves, side view first.** Bavelly, sole animator on Warrior Cats: Ultimate Edition (Roblox Creator Spotlight on the DevForum): she gathers reference in side, front and top views and blocks where the poses start and end. She breaks the movement into quarters (step, passing, next step, mirrored), then eighths, then sixteenths. She works the side view first, then the front view, where the body moves side to side and the weight shifts between the feet, then the hips, head and body curve. She checks the Graph Editor for smooth curves, adds the details last (ear bounce, tail sway, eye focus), and sends work-in-progress clips to her team throughout. On R6: block a cycle as 4 whole poses on `step` keys rounded to the 60 fps grid, add the 4 eighths, and add sixteenths only on joints whose path still kinks. Finish the side strip (`_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "side"})`) before the front strip, where the torso roll and `px` sway live; add `lag`, `springs` and `life` last.

## Drag down the chain

- **Drag lives in the breakdown; end offsets give only end overlap.** Keith Lango, "Offset keys vs. changing shapes": offsetting the arrival keys down a chain (hips on frame 10, mid spine 11, upper spine 12, head 13) gives "some overlap at the end of the move, but you get absolutely no drag in the middle of the move. Drag during the move is where the weight comes from." The mixed curves also tend to leave "a funky hitch". He builds drag with breakdowns whose shapes change, and offsets keys a lot, sometimes per channel, only at the final polish. On R6 this bites harder than on a full rig. Poser's `lag` and `springs` act on each joint's own channels (Poser.lua samples and springs every joint in its own pose space), and the arms and head hang from the torso, so a torso turn carries them rigidly: a lagged or sprung arm with no keys of its own does not trail a torso turn at all. Key the drag. In the breakdown where the torso has made 60 to 70% of its turn, key each carried arm's twist or side back against the turn by about a third of the torso's change, then let it catch up and pass on the extreme (starting values). Add `lag` and key offsets afterwards for the end overlap. This refines SKILL.md, "Offset the chain": `lag` delays a joint's own move, not the move its parent gives it.
- **Favour the breakdown down the chain like a wave.** The Animator's Checklist, "Spacing or how to prevent your animations are floaty": in breakdowns, "As the principle of the wave, we favor the last pose sequentially (segment by segment)", which shows which parts lead; a change from an S shape to a C shape makes the transition more dynamic. On R6: in each breakdown key, put the driver nearest its next pose and each carried part further back: the torso at about 70% of its change, the head at about 50%, the free arm at 30 to 40% (starting values). Extremes stay whole poses (keyframe-workflow.md, "Block in whole poses").
- **Copy and offset for quick overlap, then fix the ends.** Navone, "Splinophilia" Part 2, "Tips and tricks": copy a driving curve's keys onto each joint down a tail and offset them in time; "Start with an offset of 1 frame per joint and increase as needed", depending on the speed of the action, and scale the values to each joint's range. The result "will require more custom keys at the beginning and ending of the action". Copying the head's side-to-side tilt keys onto its turn channel, offset 5 frames, adds overlap inside one joint. On R6: for a loose chain on a custom rig (a cape, a tail, a ponytail), copy the driver's curve with 2 to 3 frames per link (1 film frame, our conversion). For a head in an idle or a dance, copy the head's `side` keys into its twist at about half the size and about 12 frames apart (5 film frames, our conversion). Never on a head that holds a target (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain").

## Overshoots and settles

- **The sharper the stop, the bigger the overshoot.** Scott McWhinnie (AnimSchool, "Breaking down breakdowns"): great and intense emotions do not come to a full stop, "there simply isn't enough time", so they overshoot and settle back; "The sharper and more abrupt an action, the greater the overshoot"; anticipations and overshoots are not always necessary. Spacers then set the ease out of rest, the ease into rest and the slow-down before a change of direction. On R6: size each keyed overshoot by how fast the part arrives. A part arriving at 25 degrees a frame or faster takes the top of the strike range in principles.md, "Timing and spacing in frames" (5 to 10%); a 15 to 20 frame reach takes 2 to 4%; a slow weight shift takes none and eases in (starting values for the last two).
- **Two kinds of moving hold: the overshoot and the long ease.** Animation Mentor newsletter, June 2009, "What is a moving hold?": a moving hold either zips past its destination and quickly recovers into the key pose, or eases slowly into it. The overshoot pose is the key pose "taken up a notch", placed "maybe even just a couple frames after your 'real' pose", with overlap on the wrist, the prop and the head, then an arc back. A character that "just hit an invisible wall" is "far too common on the demo reels I see". Subtle ambient motion (tiny chest rotations like breaths, small head readjustments, a slight weight shift in the hips) keeps the held pose alive. On R6: for an overshoot hold, key the joints that carry the line about 5 frames past the arrival (2 film frames, our conversion), each pushed further (the torso lean 3 to 5 degrees more, the arm 5 to 8 more), then arc back to the true pose over 8 to 12 frames with the head and the free arm 2 frames behind (starting values). For a long ease, arrive at 85 to 90% and travel the rest during the hold (SKILL.md, "Moving holds"). A snap into a pose takes the overshoot; a calm arrival takes the long ease.
- **Overshoot the extreme, then hold a little longer.** David Gibson's GDC 2016 Animation Bootcamp talk on Overwatch, in an attendee's notes: the snappy timing came from always overshooting the extreme pose and then holding the key poses a little longer than usual, with slightly different timing on the two arms, because total symmetry looks stiff. On R6: in a snappy hero style, give every big pose change an overshoot key (the finding above), then a moving hold of 8 to 15 frames before the next action, and land the two arms 2 to 3 frames apart (starting values).
- **A struggle vibrates on an envelope.** Louaye Moulayess (AnimSchool, "Adding vibration to animation"): find the point of maximum vibration, where the character struggles most to lift; set zero vibration at a start and an end point so the poses around it do not change; key every frame between with alternating offsets that build to the peak and fall away; then copy the vibration to another control and scale its amplitude down. On R6: alternate a torso `side` (or `px`) offset every 2 to 3 frames, the 12 to 15 Hz of principles.md, "Moving holds and settles", rising from 0 to its peak on the hardest frame of the effort and back to 0. Copy it to the head and the arms at about half the amplitude, 1 frame later (starting values).

## Fixing floaty motion

- **"Too swimmy" means too many in-betweens.** 11 Second Club, Helpful Hints, "The top 5": "Your animation feels too swimmy / too computer-y / too weightless" is one of the five comments posted most on contest entries. It comes from erring slow, with too much ease-in and ease-out. The fix an experienced 2D animator gave was "Take out some in-betweens": key every frame, delete the frames that should go, and slide the rest together, without changing a drawing. A bouncing ball that took 158 frames to settle became 77 frames with the same drawings. On R6: put `warp` above 1 (1.5 to 2, starting values) over only the swimmy ease segments, which skips in-betweens there without moving a pose, then bake. `warp` runs the whole clip's clock, so for one joint move its breakdown keys nearer the extremes instead. Deleting single frames needs new code: Poser has no helper that turns a clip into per-frame keys.
- **Heads and hands float first.** Same source: swimminess shows most in head and hand movements. Hands and heads move fast and are heavy under gravity: relaxed arms "drop pretty quickly to their resting position as soon as your muscles stop holding them up in the air", and a head drop or turn "will probably take fewer frames than you'd guess". On R6: end a gesture with the arm falling to the side in 6 to 10 frames, speeding up, with only a 2 to 3 degree settle at the bottom (starting values). Time a relaxed head drop at the short end of the head-turn range in principles.md, "Timing and spacing in frames".
- **A long gap without keys floats.** Kelly (Tips and Tricks Volume 1): with 20 or more frames between breakdowns, check that the motion does not feel "floaty" or robotic, because the computer's smoothing "often just results in things looking drunk or wobbly". He rarely lets more than 4 or 5 frames pass without a key on at least one controller. On R6: that is 50 frames (0.83 s) and 10 to 12 frames (our conversion). In a one-shot, keep a key on some joint at least every 10 to 12 frames, and look again at any joint whose curve runs 50 frames between keys. A breath or a `life` drift in a long hold is the exception.
- **Clean arcs everywhere read light.** Jonathan Cooper, Game Anim, chapter 3 (on Game Developer): animating every element on clean arcs "can look light or floaty when nothing catches the eye". Because of its weight, "the head will often snap after the rest of the body comes to a stop following a punch", breaking from the body's arc. On R6: after a punch's contact, when the torso stops, key a short head nod 2 to 4 frames later (4 to 6 degrees of lift over 2 frames, back over 4 to 6) instead of letting a `follow` spring smooth it away (starting values). The head's twist stays on the target (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain"); the nod rides on lift only.

## Contact, weight and impact

- **A light weapon bounces off; a heavy one stays down.** Overwatch 2 animation team (Blizzard, "Weekly Recall: Animation Station"): Wuyang's ground slam first borrowed Reinhardt's hammer slam, but a thin, light staff "wouldn't linger on the ground like Reinhardt's bulky hammer: it would bounce against the ground due to its weight". Changing the slam to a light ricochet felt more realistic. His staff swings arc over the shoulder "to maximize velocity". On R6: for a light weapon's ground strike, key a rebound 3 to 4 frames after the contact that lifts the tip 8 to 15 degrees through the weapon arm's lift, then the recovery. For a heavy weapon, keep the contact 6 to 10 frames with the torso sinking 0.1 to 0.2 studs (`Feet.flat` keeps the soles), then a slow lift (starting values; principles.md, "Weight and support": a heavy weapon rises slower than it falls).
- **Recoil sells power without costing response.** Cooper: a game animator "will often exaggerate the kickback of a pistol to show its relative power", "all the while maintaining the instant response"; a sword that swings immediately can look light, so the weight goes into the follow-through, "a fast in but a slow out". An R6 gun clip on the DevForum drew "Seems like two keyframes", "add some more bounce" and "add momentum to the arm movement", and one reply suggested rotating the torso a bit and moving the arm back "a stud or two then back to get the better recoil effect". On R6: fire on the first frame with no wind-up. Over the next 2 to 3 frames twist the torso 5 to 8 degrees so the gun-side shoulder goes back, piston the gun arm back 0.2 to 0.4 studs along its length, then return over 8 to 12 frames with the head 2 frames behind (starting values). A full stud breaks the arm contact rule (SKILL.md, "Feet rule"; "No elbows or knees" gives 0.3 to 0.6 studs on strike frames).
- **Never cut out of a strike.** In an R6 combat thread on the DevForum ("How could I improve on the impact of these anims?"), replies said the moves "start and end quite abruptly instead of easing gradually to and from full speed", that "The way they just cut out of the move lessens the impact", and that a punch needs a slight pull-back first and a follow-through "before slightly recoiling", with the upper body lurching forward "a frame or two behind the arm". On R6: the fist and the torso still meet on the contact key (principles.md, "Overlap: successive breaking of joints"); after it, key the torso's forward overshoot 1 to 2 frames after the arm's, and end every attack on a follow-through and a recovery, never on the contact pose.

## Curves: tangents, cleaning, economy

- **Pick the tangent by the cause of the motion.** Navone, "Splinophilia" Part 1: a fast-out or fast-in (linear or broken tangents, no ease at that key) suits motion "acted on by an external force". A ball hit by a golf club fast-outs of its hold and only decelerates; a ball that rolls into a wall eases out and fast-ins to the impact. An ease-in gives the ball "a more natural deceleration and a sense of weight". On R6: a joint leaving a hold gets a zero tangent under `auto`, so a jolt from outside needs a named ease. `e = "quad"` (or `quart` or `expo`; the direction defaults to `out`) on the next key starts that segment at full speed and decelerates into the key: the first segment of a struck victim. `e = "quad", d = "in"` on the impact key accelerates into it: a body or limb stopped by the floor or a wall. Keep `auto` everywhere else (SKILL.md, "Spline through breakdowns").
- **Let the keys hold the extremes.** Navone, "Splinophilia" Part 2, "Spline hygiene": do not let splines pass beyond the values of neighbouring keys ("let the keys describe your extremes, not your curves"), or the extremes "may change or disappear once you start offsetting keys in time". The exception is a quick polish overshoot on fingers and faces, or pushing an extreme onto a half frame. Keep tangent handles from overshooting other keys in time, "as this will give you hitches in the motion". On R6: `auto` is clamped, so every extreme stays on its key through `lag` and key offsets. Use `smooth` only for a small overshoot on a free hand or a loose part with no contact (keyframe-workflow.md, "From stepped to spline", bans it near holds and planted parts). A film half frame is about one frame at 60 fps (our conversion): move the key one frame instead.
- **Few keys, dense only at contacts, never repeated.** Navone: delete extraneous keys and let the computer smooth; once blocking is approved, clean out redundant keys (flat tangents, same value); keep more keys where they must be accurate, "such as showing contact between two body parts"; "If something looks repetitive in your splines, there's a good chance it looks repetitive in your animation as well." Martin Scotto (AnimSchool, "Avoiding spline depression"): do not be afraid to delete keys and reduce a curve to its essential poses; "the default spline and auto tangent settings don't take into account timing, weight, or intent". On R6: delete a key only when it shapes neither a path nor a timing. Navone's redundant same-value keys are the copied hold pairs that Poser turns into dead stops (keyframe-workflow.md, "From stepped to spline"): move the second key 5 to 15% along the move instead of deleting it. Keep keys dense around foot targets and a fist on a target. Print each joint's key times and break up any joints that share one pattern; `Poser.check` shows them as unison.
- **Dense stepped keys, then spline one beat at a time, the body first.** Ricky Renna (AnimSchool, "Cleaning up curves: splining 101"): before splining, have stepped keys at least every three or four frames (sometimes five); switch everything to auto tangents; start cleaning with the biggest controllers (body); spline one section at a time, 30 to 40 frames or from one action to the next, rather than one controller across the whole shot. Jason Schleifer (republished by the 11 Second Club, "The fear of moving past blocking"): split the shot into beats (his example: sitting on frames 1 to 20, the reach 21 to 28, the glass to the lips 29 to 35, the sip 36 to 45), shorten the timeline to one beat, hide the arms and legs and clean the torso first. On R6: before `curve = "spline"`, give the stepped blocking a key on some joint every 8 to 10 frames (our conversion). Then clean beat by beat (75 to 100 frames, or one action; our conversion) in the layer order of SKILL.md, "The motion pass, in layers", and capture each beat's strip before the next.
- **The Animation Editor's curve editor starts Linear.** Roblox creator docs, "Curve Editor": curve animations have three interpolation modes: Linear (the default, constant speed), Constant (snaps from key to key) and Cubic (eases, and the only mode with tangents); an unset tangent is computed from the set ones, and "Set Tangent To Zero" resets one. A DevForum reply found that Cubic interpolation in the curve editor makes an animation look "really good". On R6: when a clip is finished or edited in the curve editor, set Cubic on every moving key, because Linear plays at constant speed, which SKILL.md rules out for a living part ("The principles, applied directly", spacing row). Keep Constant only where Poser had `step`.

## Arcs and the breakdown

- **A reach overshoots up, then comes down into the grab.** Kelly (Tips and Tricks Volume 1, "Track your arcs"): a hand lifting to grab a rope traces "a skinny, interrupted figure 8 path, with the hand moving up slightly higher and further forward than it needs to go, and then arcing over and back down a bit as it grabs". Decide in the thumbnails whether the head moves in an over-arc or an under-arc; one of the breakdown's main jobs is to define the arcing path of the hips and limbs. On R6: key the reaching arm's breakdown 3 to 5 frames before the grab, about 5 to 10 degrees higher and a little further forward than the grab pose, then arc down into it (starting values). For a head turn, pick the arc in the plan and key it with one mid-turn head lift, up for an over-arc and down for an under-arc; a turn keyed only at its ends travels flat. Print the fist path in root space to confirm it (SKILL.md, "Arcs and paths").

## Line of action, silhouette and reversals

- **A reversal shows power.** Kelly (Tips and Tricks Volume 1, "Reversals"): in a heavy box lift the spine's curve reverses from a ")" shape to a "(" shape as the shoulders heave up and back and the hips come forward under the box, then reverses again as the weight settles. Reversals appear in pitches, javelin throws, tennis serves, jumps, pushes and pulls. Start from the body mechanics, then accentuate the reversal. On R6: there is no spine; the line runs from the planted feet through the torso to the head. Reverse it with the torso's pitch against the legs and the head following the torso's curve: a throw's load leans the torso back 10 to 15 degrees with the head tipped back, and the release pitches it 15 to 25 degrees forward with the head forward, within 4 to 8 frames (starting values). Shift torso `p` with the lean so the COM stays over the soles (SKILL.md, "Centre of mass"; `Feet.flat` keeps them planted).
- **Hold a weapon off the body's line.** Overwatch 2 animation team: Wuyang's early idle aimed his staff forward, so he was "running around with a stick aimed at other Heroes", and his silhouette "didn't read well when he was viewed head on since his staff was facing the same direction as his body". The fix held the staff to the side and moved it to the attack position inside the primary-fire animation. On R6: in an idle or a walk, angle a held weapon 30 to 45 degrees off the torso's facing so it reads from the front and from `rear34`, and let the attack's anticipation bring it into line (starting values; grip limits in weapons.md).
- **The idle states the personality.** Gibson's GDC 2016 talk, in an attendee's notes: an Overwatch idle conveys the character on top of movement speed and function. Tracer stands wide, faces the camera head on and leans back, "peppy and ready to go"; Mei's first concept art did not match her shy archetype, which called for feet together, slightly off-balance, the body angled a little away from the camera and weapon. For a run or a walk, full extension keys and a passing pose that match the character leave the rest "pretty much just adjustments". On R6: a confident idle has the `Feet.flat` targets wide within the feet rule, the torso pitched back 3 to 5 degrees and the head level and square; a shy one keeps the legs at their default positions, where R6 legs already touch, with the torso twisted 10 to 15 degrees away and the head down 5 to 10 (starting values). Polish a cycle's contact and passing poses before anything else in it.
- **Exaggeration is intensity, not size.** 11 Second Club, "The top 5": "Exaggeration doesn't simply mean 'make things bigger.' It is more often about increasing the visual intensity of a pose, even if it's a smaller performance." The same article notes that viewers judge an entry from its first pose, the poster frame, before they press play. On R6: for a small or inward emote (protective, shy, cold), push the line rather than the reach: torso hunched forward 10 to 15 degrees, head down, both arms drawn in with a gap to the torso (starting values). Make the first frame of every emote a readable pose, not the middle of a blend.

## Anticipation and holds

- **Build energy in the hold before a big move.** Jean-Luc Delhougne (AnimSchool, "Blocking plus workflow: breakdowns"): when the key poses are right but the movement "starts or stops abruptly", shape the moving hold before the big motion: nudge the hips down and forward and add slight opposition in the spine and head. "It's not about big moves—it's about involving the whole body in the motion". On R6: in the 6 to 10 frames before a big action, sink torso `py` 0.03 to 0.08 studs and shift it slightly toward the move, with the head tilting 2 to 4 degrees against it (starting values); `Feet.flat` keeps the soles flat.
- **Leave the anticipation out only for a surprise.** Gwilym Morris (AnimSchool, "Acting through anticipation"): a "surprise gag" leaves the anticipation pose out to surprise the audience, and anticipation can also mislead, as when a character's reaction makes a light object look heavy. On R6: drop the anticipation only on a move whose point is surprise (a gag emote, a stand appearing), never on an NPC attack the player must read (combat.md, "Telegraphs and reaction time").
- **Hold an apex longer than reality, except on attacks the player must dodge.** Cooper: "Real life never looks real enough"; a real action copied exactly looks slow and imperfect, so poses are "accentuated and held a little longer than in reality", and where a physical bounce peaks on a smooth parabola, an animator "may choose to hold longer at the apex" before "zooming back to the ground, much like a martial-arts character thundering down with a flying kick". On R6: in a root-motion jump attack or a cinematic, slow the apex with `warp` (0.4 to 0.6 over 6 to 10 frames) and play the drop at 1.2 to 1.5 (starting values). A Humanoid physics jump cannot be slowed by the clip (body-mechanics.md, "Gravity, jumps and falls on Roblox"). An enemy's jump attack keeps its rise and fall similar (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Rhythm, idle and readability in action games").

## Watching your own animation

- **Flip back and forth between consecutive keys.** 11 Second Club, Helpful Hints, "Back and forth": step between two consecutive key poses with the next-key and previous-key buttons, over and over, the way a 2D animator rolls drawings. Every body part should change, even when the main change is one arm. In the example from The Incredibles, Bob's raised arm also brings the shoulder up, shifts the hips a little ("it sells the idea of the body's inertia"), lifts the other shoulder, pulls the other arm in, tightens the fist and turns and tilts the head. On R6: capture each key alone with `_G.editStrip(clip, {t}, {facing = "rear34"})` from the same camera and flip between the images; print each joint's change between the two keys. A joint at 0 while another moves 30 degrees or more is a dead part: give the torso a few degrees of roll toward the raised arm and the head a small tilt (starting values).
- **Every frame is a drawing: test random frames.** 11 Second Club, "Every frame is a drawing": pick frames at random, most of them in-betweens, and ask whether each one could hang on a wall like an animation cel. CG "fools our eyes into taking a lot longer to see" that a frame is bad, and "You can set keys on every body part on every single frame if you want to". On R6: capture 5 random times that are not key times with `_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "rear34"})`, and fix any frame whose line of action or silhouette breaks (an arm inside the torso, a twinned pair, a limb off its socket) with a breakdown key (the count is a starting value).
- **Review frame by frame, in a file that steps.** Animator Island, "Want an animation critique? Use Quicktime!": animation is an illusion made of single frames, so a critique has to break it back into frames. YouTube and animated GIFs cannot be stepped; a QuickTime file lets a reviewer go "one frame at a time and roll/flip the images", which yields specific notes instead of "it needs more easing". On R6: give reviewers the recorded video file (pipeline.md, "Review videos (scripts/video)") or the per-frame captures, not only a streamed upload, and step through every contact and hand-over frame by frame yourself before calling a clip done.
- **Check the side view before polishing to the camera.** Holiday Liu (lead animator, Steamroller Studios, on 80.lv): when a polish-stage shot feels mechanically off, she checks the side view and often finds the action was cheated to the camera view, leaving an unrealistic side view that is hard to fix at polish. Get the motion right in perspective first, then polish spacing, arcs and silhouettes to the camera. Cooper: game actions must look great from all angles, so "cheating is out of the question". On R6: the player orbits the camera, so there is no camera to cheat to. Pass the side strip (balance, push, brace; SKILL.md, steps 4 and 5) before any arc or spacing polish from `rear34`.
- **Fresh eyes come from time away and from non-animators.** Brian Lemay, "Reviewing and analyzing your pencil test": without a teacher, set the assignment aside "for a week or so" and look at it again; write the critique out, and keep a note of the error you keep making ("Don't forget the overlapping action!!"). Liu: work on several shots and switch between them for fresh eyes, and practise giving yourself notes. Tim Borrelli (GDC Animation Bootcamp roundtable on Game Developer): non-animators "won't try to find an animator solution for you, they'll just tell you what feels wrong to them". On R6: keep a running list of the notes this project keeps getting (a lagged guard hand, a dead hold, a sliding brace) in the review record of the clip plan ([clip-plan.md](../templates/clip-plan.md)) and check every new clip against it before review. Re-review a clip in a later session before hand-off, and ask a non-animator what feels wrong, not how to fix it (keyframe-workflow.md, "Review with fresh eyes", covers the fresh reviewer and the flipped capture).
- **Take the note's intent, not its letter.** Liu: understand the reason behind a note. Her example: "reduce head drag and make it sharper and direct" may mean the character is reacting to a sudden sound, so reduce the movement and drag and judge the fix against that reason. On R6: when a reviewer calls a head floaty, find out what the head is doing. Reacting to a sound, it leads (about 5 frames for a reflex, keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting"); holding a target, it counters the torso with no lag; being carried, it gets a shorter `lag` or a stiffer spring. process.md covers how to ask.

## Common notes and their fixes

- **The five comments posted most on contest entries.** 11 Second Club, "The top 5": "You should return to the basics" (bounce a ball, match a pose from a magazine photo, shift the weight from one leg to the other), "You need stronger poses", "too swimmy / too computer-y / too weightless", "Your character's energy doesn't match the dialogue", and "Your camera moves are much too distracting". On R6: the weight shift is the first test for a new rig or controller (`Feet.flat` with the COM moving over one sole; body-mechanics.md, "Balance and weight shift"); the pose and swimmy notes are answered in this file ("Exaggeration is intensity, not size", "Fixing floaty motion"); keep the review camera still in emote and cutscene captures.
- **"The other arm looks glued to the torso."** On an R6 combo posted to the DevForum, a reviewer asked for the non-punching arm to move up and down or rotate "to make it not look like completely glued to torso"; the animator answered that it guards the head and that the torso's fast turn hides its motion. On R6 both are right. A guard arm must not wear `lag` (principles.md, "Overlap: successive breaking of joints"), but it needs a keyed counter-motion of its own: as the other arm strikes, pull the guard fist 0.05 to 0.1 studs back toward the face and change its lift 3 to 5 degrees, landing 2 frames after the strike's contact (starting values).
- **"The torso doesn't read as turning."** A DevForum reply to a solo developer's animations said that although the torsos move, "it still feels like they are facing the front still", perhaps because the rig is all one colour; another reply advised making the torso's rotation speed go "slow → fast → slow". On R6: a box torso's turn reads from the shoulder line and the arms. Push the twist until the shoulder line visibly changes from `rear34` (a guard-to-punch turn of 56 degrees, SKILL.md, "Rigid legs"), spline it with the fast part in the middle (no `linear`), and carry the arms with it.
- **"Like hitting the replay button."** On a looping animation posted to the DevForum, a reply said it "looks a bit static, like someone is just hitting the reverse/replay button over and over", and advised easing in and out at the turnarounds and making "a few slight variations of the loop". On R6: let `auto` flatten each turnaround, but spread the joints' reversal frames 2 to 4 frames apart so the body never reverses on one frame (`Poser.check` unison), and build two or three variants (next section).

## Game polish: priorities, blends and loops

- **Polish what the player sees most, first.** Overwatch 2 animation team: they start with the common movements, because a player spends far more time standing in the lobby, using the primary fire and running around the map, and perfect those for the hero's personality before complex animations like Ultimates. A rough draft borrowed from other heroes gets a new hero into playtests early, and playtesting runs throughout. On R6: order the work by screen time: idle, walk and run, the basic attack chain, then specials and emotes. Put every move in the game first as a placeholder (an existing clip or the stepped blocking) and playtest before polishing any one of them.
- **One main idle, rare variants, one shared end pose.** Roblox's default Animate script, as quoted in a DevForum thread, lists three idle animations with weights 1, 1 and 9; an R6 copy quoted in another thread lists two idle entries weighted 9 and 1. Replies in the first thread explain that the variants add variety, that one main idle should play most of the time, that every variant should end in about the same pose, and that too many variants make that shared ending itself repetitive. On R6: author one main idle (weight 9 of 11, about 82% of picks) and one or two variants (weight 1 each, about 9%), each starting and ending on the main idle's first pose so any pick loops cleanly. principles.md, "Idle or held stance", gives the variation cadence.
- **Ease the cross-fade between clips.** DevForum, "How to make a smoother transition between two animations": the native fade looked linear to the poster. Developers drove `AdjustWeight` every frame from a tweened value (Exponential InOut over 1 s; Exponential Out over 0.5 s) for a smoother hand-off; one reported that a weight of exactly 0 broke replication and used 0.05 or 0.001 instead; another got a similar result from `Stop(0.25)`. Roblox's AnimationTrack reference says the weight moves from WeightCurrent to WeightTarget over the fade time but does not name the curve. On R6: Poser's `blend = "inertial"` already eases its offset on a smoothstep over `fadeIn` (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract", which also sizes the fade from the pose distance). For native tracks an eased weight ramp is runtime code in the game's controller, not a clip field (stylized-and-roblox.md, "The Animator: fades, priorities and weights", covers near-zero weights).
- **A walk and a jog cancel each other's bounce.** Cooper (gameanim.com essay on blending): blended cycles must sync and start on the same pose (he uses the right-foot-up passing pose). A walk blended with a jog "planes along without a bounce midway between the blend due to the passing pose of a walk being the highest point whereas the equivalent pose is the lowest or weight-taking frame on a jog". On R6: before blending two cycles by speed, compare their torso `py` at the same normalised time; where one peaks while the other dips, the blend flattens the bob, so add an in-between cycle with its own bob or switch with a short fade instead of a long blend (locomotion.md, "Keeping feet planted across speeds and directions", covers the foot markers). Poser plays one clip per rig, so a weight blend of two cycles needs runtime code; the locomotion starter's procedural clip is one way (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays").

## Does not transfer to R6

- Detaching the arm from the shoulder to place it where a real forearm would be (an R6 combat thread on the DevForum): it breaks the arm contact rule (SKILL.md, "Feet rule"); suggest the bend with a 0.3 to 0.6 stud piston instead (SKILL.md, "No elbows or knees").
- Rotating and scaling limbs past the rig so elbows rest on knees (11 Second Club, "The top 5"): R6 parts cannot scale and have no elbows or knees.
- Breaking the rig on fast frames for smears (Overwatch's McCree arm and D.Va leg in Gibson's talk notes; "breaking bodies" in Mariel Cartwright's Skullgirls talk on 80.lv): R6 parts cannot stretch; combat.md, "Smears and trails", gives the Trail substitute.
- Eye darts, eye poses and blinks as the life of a moving hold (Animation Mentor newsletter, June 2009): R6 has no eyes by default; the head leads and a face decal swap stands in (keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting").
- Finger curls and finger overshoots (11 Second Club, "The top 5"; Navone's spline-hygiene exception): R6 has no fingers.
- Drag through the mid and upper spine (Lango's offset example): the R6 torso is one part, so the drag moves to the arms and head and is keyed there (see "Drag down the chain").
