# Polish and critique

This file holds the polish techniques and review habits that professional animators and leads use to lift a clip from correct to good, the notes that students and Roblox animators receive most, and how each one reads on six rigid R6 parts in Poser. Every finding was checked against its source with an independent re-read ([sources.md](sources.md), "Polish and critique: what separates professional motion research"). Film sources count at 24 fps; their 60 fps values multiply by 2.5 and are marked "our conversion". Values marked "starting values" are this file's suggestions for R6, not numbers from the source.

## Spacing and favouring

- **Favour one end of a move, not both.** Victor Navone, "Splinophilia" Part 1: he makes an ease by moving the inner key of a held pose to 85 to 95% of the change between the two poses and smoothing its tangent. An identical ease-in and ease-out makes the spacing symmetrical, "which again is not very interesting. It's better to pick one of the poses to favor." A curve that passes beyond the second pose gives an overshoot, not an ease. On R6: under `auto`, a move keyed only at its two held poses gets a zero tangent at both ends, so it plays as that symmetrical ease. Pick the end each move favours. A settle into a guard favours the arrival: the arrival key at 85 to 95% of the change, and the hold travels the rest (principles.md, "Moving holds and settles"). A slam favours the departure: keys packed near the raised pose and no ease into the contact (lesson-videos.md, "2. NobleFrugal Studio: timing and spacing"). lesson-videos.md also favours both ends of a move; that suits a flick with only a few frames, not a longer move.
- **The breakdown sets the timing, not the default curve.** Shawn Kelly, Animation Tips and Tricks Volume 1 (Animation Mentor): with only key poses, the computer's smooth interpolation gives "essentially a bell-curve, with perfectly smooth ease-ins and ease-outs in all the wrong places". His ball is on the ground on frame 1, at the top on frame 5 and on the ground again on frame 10. He drags frame 3 up toward the top, so the ball covers more distance between frames 1 and 2 than between frames 3 and 4 and eases into the top. A breakdown either makes an ease or carries body mechanics (an arc, overlap). On R6: `auto` gives the same bell between two extremes. Give each such segment at least one breakdown off the midpoint. A rise (a jump's torso `py`, an arm lifting) puts it at 70 to 80% of the travel at half the time, so the part slows into the top. A drop puts it at 20 to 30%, so the part speeds up toward the bottom (starting values). A drop that ends on a contact also needs a contact that keeps its speed. With this breakdown, keep `auto` and make the contact a pass-through with a key one frame past it (the compression), as SKILL.md does for strikes ("Follow-through and through motion"). The named ease into an impact ("Pick the tangent by the cause of the motion", below) cannot follow a breakdown: it starts at zero speed, so the part would stop dead on the breakdown. Print the per-frame values: steps that grow and shrink symmetrically mean the segment is still the bell.
- **Subdivide a cycle by halves, side view first.** Bavelly, the sole animator on Warrior Cats: Ultimate Edition (Roblox Creator Spotlight on the DevForum), gathers reference of a cat walking in side, front and top views and blocks where the poses start and end. She breaks the movement into quarters (a step, the passing frame, the next step, then the mirror with the other legs), then eighths, then sixteenths. She starts with the side view, then the front view, where the body moves side to side and the weight shifts between the feet, then adjusts the hips, the head and the body's curve. She checks the Graph Editor for smooth curves, adds the details last (ear bounce, tail sway, eye focus), and sends work-in-progress clips to her team throughout. On R6: block a walk as 4 whole poses per cycle (contact, passing, the other contact, the other passing) on `step` keys on the 60 fps grid, add the 4 eighths, and add sixteenths only on joints whose path still kinks. Finish the side strip (`_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "side"})`) before the front strip, where the torso roll and `px` sway live; add `lag`, `springs` and `life` last. principles.md, "Walk", gives the poses and amounts.

## Drag down the chain

- **Drag lives in the breakdown; end offsets give only end overlap.** Keith Lango, "Offset Keys vs. Changing Shapes" (2009): offsetting the arrival keys down a chain (hips on frame 10, mid spine 11, upper spine 12, head 13) gives "some overlap at the end of the move, but you get absolutely no drag in the middle of the move. Drag during the move is where the weight comes from." The offsets also often leave "a funky hitch in the motion". He builds drag with breakdowns whose shapes change, and offsets keys a lot, sometimes per channel, only at the final polish. On R6 this bites harder. Poser's `lag` and `springs` act on each joint's own channels in its parent's space (Poser.lua), and the arms and head hang from the torso, so a torso turn carries them rigidly. An arm with `lag` or a spring but no keys of its own does not trail a torso turn at all. Key the drag. In the breakdown where the torso has made 60 to 70% of its turn, swing each hanging arm against the turn by about a third of the torso's change (starting values): the arm whose shoulder moves forward swings back (negative `lift`), the other swings forward. Solve a raised arm from the direction you want instead (r6-mechanics.md, "Direction and rotation checks"). Let the arms catch up and pass on the extreme, then add `lag` and key offsets for the end overlap. This refines SKILL.md, "Offset the chain": `lag` delays a joint's own move, not the move its parent gives it.
- **Favour the breakdown down the chain like a wave.** The Animator's Checklist, "Spacing": in breakdowns, "As the principle of the wave, we favor the last pose sequentially (segment by segment)", which defines which parts lead; going from an S shape to a C shape makes the transition more dynamic. On R6: in each breakdown key, put the driver nearest its next pose and each carried part further back: the torso at about 70% of its change, the head at about 50%, a free arm at 30 to 40% (starting values). A head that holds a target is not a carried part; it counters the torso on the torso's frames (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain"). Extremes stay whole poses (keyframe-workflow.md, "Block in whole poses").
- **Copy and offset for quick overlap, then fix the ends.** Navone, "Splinophilia" Part 2, "Tips and Tricks": copy a driving curve's keys onto each joint down a tail and offset them in time. "Start with an offset of 1 frame per joint and increase as needed", depending on the speed of the animation, and scale the values to each joint's range. The result "will require more custom keys at the beginning and ending of the action". Copying the head's side-to-side tilt keys onto its turn channel, offset 5 frames, adds overlap inside one joint. On R6: for a loose chain on a custom rig (a cape, a tail, a ponytail; custom-rigs.md), copy the driver's curve with about 2.5 frames per link (1 film frame, our conversion). For a head in an idle or a dance, copy the head's `side` keys into its `twist` about 12 frames apart (5 film frames, our conversion) at about half the size (a starting value). Never do this on a head that holds a target (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain").

## Overshoots and settles

- **The sharper the stop, the bigger the overshoot.** Scott McWhinnie (AnimSchool, "Breaking down breakdowns"): great and intense emotions do not come to a full stop "because there simply isn't enough time", so they overshoot and settle back. "The sharper and more abrupt an action, the greater the overshoot." Anticipations and overshoots are not always necessary. Spacers set the ease out of rest, the ease into rest and the slow-down before a change of direction. On R6: size each keyed overshoot by how fast the part arrives. A part arriving at 25 degrees a frame or faster takes the top of the follow-through range in principles.md, "Timing and spacing in frames" (5 to 10%). A 15 to 20 frame reach takes 2 to 4%; a slow weight shift takes none and eases in (starting values for the last two).
- **Two kinds of moving hold: the overshoot and the long ease.** Shawn Kelly, "What Is a Moving Hold?" (Animation Mentor newsletter, June 2009): a moving hold either zips past its destination and quickly recovers into the key pose, or eases slowly into it. The overshoot pose is the key pose "taken up a notch", placed "maybe even just a couple frames after your 'real' pose", with overlap on the wrist, the held prop and the head, then an arc back; it helps if everything does not stop on the same frame. A character that "just hit an invisible wall" is "far too common on the demo reels I see". On R6: for an overshoot hold, key the joints that carry the line about 5 frames past the arrival (2 film frames, our conversion), each pushed further (the torso lean 3 to 5 degrees more, the arm 5 to 8 more). Then arc back to the true pose over 8 to 12 frames with the head and the free arm 2 frames behind (starting values). For a long ease, arrive at 85 to 90% and travel the rest during the hold (SKILL.md, "Moving holds"). Following McWhinnie above, an abrupt arrival takes the overshoot and a calm one the long ease. The ambient motion Kelly adds to a hold (tiny chest rotations like breaths, small head readjustments, a slight weight shift in the hips) is the breath and `life` (SKILL.md, "Life").
- **Overshoot the extreme, then hold a little longer.** David Gibson's GDC Animation Bootcamp talk on Overwatch, in Eva Sokolova's notes (Babel, April 2016): the snappy timing came from always overshooting the extreme pose, then holding the key poses a little longer than usual, with slightly different timing on the arms, because total symmetry looks stiff. On R6: in a snappy hero style, give every big pose change the overshoot hold above, then a moving hold of 8 to 15 frames before the next action (starting values). Land the two arms 2 to 3 frames apart (keyframe-workflow.md, "Polish: arcs, offsets, retiming").
- **A struggle vibrates on an envelope.** Louaye Moulayess (AnimSchool, "Adding vibration to animation"): find the point of maximum vibration, where the character struggles most to lift the box. Set the vibration to zero at a start point and an end point so the poses around it do not change. Key every frame between, alternating in direction, building to the peak and falling away. Then copy it to another control and scale its amplitude down. On R6: alternate a torso `side` (or `px`) offset every 2 to 3 frames, the 12 to 15 Hz of principles.md, "Moving holds and settles", rising from 0 to its peak on the hardest frame and back to 0. Take the size from the same section: 0.6 to 1.3 degrees, and under a weight held overhead 1.2 on the arms against 0.6 on the torso, so the arms that bear the load shake more. Copy it to the head at about half the size, 1 frame later (starting values).

## Fixing floaty motion

- **"Too swimmy" means too many in-betweens.** Eric Scheur, 11 Second Club, Helpful Hints, "The Top 5": "Your animation feels too swimmy / too computer-y / too weightless" is the third of the five comments posted most on contest entries. It comes from erring on the side of moving too slowly, with too much ease-in and ease-out. The fix an experienced 2D animator gave him was "Take out some in-betweens": set a key on every frame, delete the frames that should go, and slide the rest together, so the remaining drawings are unchanged. A bouncing ball that took 158 frames to settle became 77 frames. On R6: to do exactly this, bake at 60 fps (`Poser.bake` writes a key on every frame), delete the frames, close the gaps and play the result with `Poser.fromSequence` (pipeline.md, "Importing sequences"). For a quicker try, raise `warp` above 1 (1.5 to 2, starting values) over only the swimmy segments; it plays them faster without moving a pose. Recheck the speed cap afterwards (principles.md, "Strike speed and retiming").
- **Heads and hands float first.** Same article: swimminess shows most in head and hand movements. Hands and heads move pretty fast and are heavy when gravity takes over: relaxed arms "drop pretty quickly to their resting position as soon as your muscles stop holding them up in the air", and a head drop or turn "will probably take fewer frames than you'd guess". On R6: end a gesture with the arm falling to the side in 6 to 10 frames, speeding up into the bottom (`e = "quad", d = "in"` on the bottom key), with only a 2 to 3 degree settle (starting values; body-mechanics.md, "Loose parts: falling, swinging, drag", gives the swing periods). Time a relaxed head drop at the short end of the head-turn range in principles.md, "Timing and spacing in frames".
- **A long gap without keys floats.** Kelly (Tips and Tricks Volume 1): with 20 or more frames between breakdowns, check that the motion does not feel "floaty" or robotic, because the computer's smoothing "often just results in things looking drunk or wobbly". He rarely lets more than 4 or 5 frames pass without a key on at least one controller. On R6: those are 50 frames and 10 to 12 frames (our conversion). In a one-shot, keep a key on some joint at least every 10 to 12 frames, and look again at any joint whose curve runs 50 frames between keys. A breath or a `life` drift in a long hold is the exception.
- **Clean arcs everywhere read light.** Jonathan Cooper, Game Anim, chapter 3 (excerpt on Game Developer): animating every element of a character on clean arcs "can look light or floaty when nothing catches the eye". Due to its weight, "the head will often snap after the rest of the body comes to a stop following a punch", breaking from the body's arc. On R6: after a punch's contact, when the torso stops, key a quick head snap 2 to 4 frames later in the direction the body was travelling. For a forward punch that is 4 to 6 degrees of negative `lift` over 2 frames, back over 4 to 6 (starting values), keyed instead of left to a `follow` spring. The head's `twist` stays on the target (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain").

## Contact, weight and impact

- **A light weapon bounces off; a heavy one stays down.** Overwatch 2 animation team (Blizzard, "Weekly Recall: Animation Station"): Wuyang's Guardian Wave first used Reinhardt's Earthshatter as a placeholder, but his thin, light staff "wouldn't linger on the ground like Reinhardt's bulky hammer: it would bounce against the ground due to its weight". Changing the slam to a light ricochet made it feel more realistic. His swings arc over the shoulder "to maximize velocity while maintaining speed". On R6: for a light weapon's ground strike, key a rebound 3 to 4 frames after the contact that lifts the tip 8 to 15 degrees through the weapon arm's `lift`, then the recovery. For a heavy weapon, keep the contact 6 to 10 frames with the torso sinking 0.1 to 0.2 studs (`Feet.flat` keeps the soles), then a slow lift (starting values; principles.md, "Weight and support": a heavy weapon rises slower than it falls).
- **Recoil sells power without costing response.** Cooper (chapter 3): a game animator "will often exaggerate the kickback of a pistol to show its relative power and damage as a weapon in gameplay, all the while maintaining the instant response and feedback of firing". A sword that swings immediately can look light, so the weight goes into the follow-through, "a fast in but a slow out". An R6 firing clip on the DevForum drew "Seems like two keyframes", "add some more bounce" and "add momentum to the arm movement". One reply suggested rotating the torso a bit and moving the arm back "a stud or two then back to get the better recoil effect". On R6: fire on the first frame with no wind-up. Over the next 2 to 3 frames twist the torso 4 to 8 degrees so the gun-side shoulder goes back and piston the gun arm back 0.2 to 0.4 studs along its length. Then return over 6 to 10 frames on `auto` keys (starting values, as in r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons"). The head keeps the eyes on the target: it counters the torso twist on the same frames with no `lag` (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain"). A stud or two is far past the 0.3 to 0.6 stud pistons measured on reference punches (SKILL.md, "No elbows or knees"). Ease the recoil, not the shot (lesson-videos.md, "1. Alan Becker: the 12 principles, translated to R6", slow in and slow out).
- **Never cut out of a strike.** In an R6 combat thread on the DevForum ("How could I improve on the impact of these anims?"), replies said the moves "start and end quite abruptly instead of easing gradually to and from full speed" and that "The way they just cut out of the move lessens the impact". Another said a punch needs a slight pull-back first and "some follow-through before slightly recoiling", with the upper body lurching forward "a frame or two behind the arm". On R6: SKILL.md already ends every attack on a keyed follow-through and a recovery ("Follow-through and through motion", "Recoveries"). The new point is the order after the contact. Up to the contact the torso drives and meets the fist on the contact key (principles.md, "Overlap: successive breaking of joints"); after it, the torso's forward overshoot peaks 1 to 2 frames after the arm's.

## Curves: tangents, cleaning, economy

- **Pick the tangent by the cause of the motion.** Navone, "Splinophilia" Part 1: a fast-out or fast-in (linear keys, broken or shortened tangents) gives an abrupt start or stop and suits an object "being acted on by an external force". A ball hit by a golf club fast-outs of its hold and only decelerates; a ball rolling into a wall eases out and fast-ins to the impact. An ease-in gives the ball "a more natural deceleration and a sense of weight". On R6: a joint leaving a hold gets a zero tangent under `auto`. Where an outside force starts the motion (the first segment of a struck victim), put `e = "quad"` (or `quart` or `expo`; the direction defaults to `out`) on the next key: the segment starts at full speed and decelerates into it. Where a wall or the floor stops the part, put `e = "quad", d = "in"` on the impact key: it accelerates into the stop. This `in` ease ignores the previous key's tangent and starts at zero speed (the ease branch of `segChan` in Poser.lua), so key that segment from a rest key at the top of the move with no breakdown in between; after a breakdown that arrives with speed, the part would stop dead on the breakdown. A drop that needs a breakdown for its arc keeps `auto` and gets a key one frame past the contact (the compression) instead. principles.md, "Motion is the curve, not the keys", calls an `out` ease that leaves a stop a jolt and the cause of the dead look; that holds for a move the body makes itself, not for one a hit forces on it. Keep `auto` everywhere else (SKILL.md, "Spline through breakdowns").
- **Let the keys hold the extremes.** Navone, "Splinophilia" Part 2, "Spline Hygiene": do not let splines pass beyond the values of neighbouring keys ("let the keys describe your extremes, not your curves"), or the extremes "may change or disappear once you start offsetting keys in time". The exceptions are a quick polish overshoot on fingers and faces, or pushing an extreme onto a half frame. Keep tangent handles from overshooting other keys in time, "as this will give you hitches in the motion". On R6: `auto` is clamped, so every extreme stays on its key through `lag` and key offsets. Use `smooth` only for a small overshoot on a free arm or a loose part with no contact; keyframe-workflow.md, "From stepped to spline", bans it on torso `py` and on a planted part next to a held value. A film half frame is about one frame at 60 fps (our conversion): move the key one frame instead.
- **Few keys, dense only at contacts, never repeated.** Navone ("Spline Hygiene"): delete extraneous keys and let the computer smooth; once blocking is approved, clean out redundant keys (flat tangents, same value); keep more keys where they must be accurate, "such as showing contact between two body parts". "If something looks repetitive in your splines, there's a good chance it looks repetitive in your animation as well." Martin Scotto (AnimSchool, "Avoiding spline depression"): do not be afraid to delete keys and reduce a curve to its essential poses; "the default spline and auto tangent settings don't take into account timing, weight, or intent." On R6: delete a key only when it shapes neither a path nor a timing. A copied hold pair is not a redundant key in Poser; it is a dead stop (keyframe-workflow.md, "From stepped to spline", gives the fix). Keep keys dense around foot targets and a fist on a target. Vary the values and timing of repeated beats; joints that share every key time show as unison in `Poser.check` (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain").
- **Dense stepped keys, then spline one beat at a time, the body first.** Ricky Renna (AnimSchool, "Cleaning up curves: splining 101"): before splining, have stepped keys at least every three or four frames (sometimes five). Switch everything to auto tangents and start cleaning with the biggest controllers (the body). Spline one section at a time, 30 to 40 frames or from one action to the next, rather than one controller across the whole shot. Jason Schleifer ("The fear of moving past blocking", republished by the 11 Second Club) splits the shot into beats (his example: sitting on frames 1 to 20, the reach 21 to 28, the glass to the lips 29 to 35, the sip 36 to 45). He shortens the timeline to one beat, hides the arms and legs and cleans the torso first. On R6: before `curve = "spline"`, give the stepped blocking a key on some joint every 8 to 10 frames (our conversion). Then clean beat by beat (75 to 100 frames, or one action; our conversion) in the layer order of SKILL.md, "3. The motion pass, in layers", and capture each beat's strip before the next.
- **The Animation Editor's curve editor starts Linear.** Roblox creator docs, "Curve Editor": curve animations have three interpolation modes: Linear (the default, constant speed), Constant (snaps from key to key) and Cubic (eases, and the only mode with tangents). Studio computes an unset tangent from the set ones, and "Set Tangent To Zero" resets one. One DevForum reply found that setting the curve editor's interpolation mode to Cubic made an animation look "really good". On R6: when a sparse clip is finished or edited in the curve editor, set Cubic on every moving key, because Linear plays at constant speed, which SKILL.md rules out for a living part ("The principles, applied directly", the spacing row). Keep Constant only where Poser had `step`. This interpolation mode is not `Pose.EasingStyle`: for KeyframeSequence poses, stylized-and-roblox.md, "Markers and easing", says to use CubicV2, not the legacy Cubic. A Poser bake has a Linear key on every frame and needs neither.

## Arcs and the breakdown

- **A reach overshoots up, then comes down into the grab.** Kelly (Tips and Tricks Volume 1, on tracking arcs): a hand lifting to grab a rope traces "a skinny, interrupted figure 8 path, with the hand moving up slightly higher and further forward than it needs to go, and then arcing over and back down a bit as it grabs". Decide in the thumbnails whether the head moves in an over-arc or an under-arc; one of the breakdown's main jobs is "to define the arcing path of the hips and limbs". On R6: below the overhead reach limit (SKILL.md, "Overhead reach"), key the reaching arm's breakdown 3 to 5 frames before the grab at 5 to 10 degrees more `lift` than the grab pose, then arc over and down into it (starting values). For a head turn, pick the arc in the plan and key one mid-turn head `lift`, up for an over-arc and down for an under-arc; a turn keyed only at its ends travels flat (lesson-videos.md arcs a head turn with a small dip). Print the fist path in root space to confirm it (SKILL.md, "Arcs and paths").

## Line of action, silhouette and reversals

- **A reversal shows power.** Kelly (Tips and Tricks Volume 1, "Reversals"): in a heavy box lift the spine's curve reverses from a ")" shape to a "(" shape as the shoulders heave up and back and the butt comes forward under the box. It reverses again as the weight settles. Reversals help create powerful pitches, javelin throws, tennis serves, jumps, pushes and pulls. Think about the body mechanics first, then accentuate the reversals. On R6: there is no spine; the line runs from the planted feet through the legs and the torso to the head. Reverse it with the torso's pitch against the legs, the head continuing the torso's curve. A throw's load arches the torso back with the head tipped back; the release pitches it forward with the head forward. For sizes that read, use the measured ranges in principles.md, "Weight and support" (a cast arching 26 degrees back, then throwing 36 to 38 forward). Lean from the waist (SKILL.md, "Waist rule") and check the COM over the soles on the load and on the release (SKILL.md, step 4).
- **Hold a weapon off the body's line.** Overwatch 2 animation team: Wuyang's early idle aimed his staff forward, so he was "essentially running around with a stick aimed at other Heroes", and his silhouette "didn't read well when he was viewed head on since his staff was facing the same direction as his body". The fix held the staff to the side and moved it to the attack position inside the primary-fire animation. On R6: this matches stylized-and-roblox.md, "Rhythm, idle and readability in action games" (raise the weapon in the attack's anticipation, not in the idle). In an idle or a walk, angle a held weapon 30 to 45 degrees off the torso's facing so it reads from the front and from `rear34` (starting values; grip limits in weapons.md).
- **The idle states the personality.** Gibson's talk, in Sokolova's notes: an Overwatch idle conveys the character's personality on top of movement speed and function. Tracer stands wide, faces the camera head on and leans back, "peppy and ready to go". Mei's initial concept art did not match her shy archetype, which called for feet together, slightly off-balance, the body angled a little away from the camera and weapon. For a run or walk cycle, full extension keys and a passing pose that match the character leave the rest "pretty much just adjustments". On R6: a confident idle sets the `Feet.flat` targets wide within the feet rule, pitches the torso back 3 to 5 degrees and keeps the head level and square. A shy one keeps the legs at their default positions, where R6 legs already touch, twists the torso 10 to 15 degrees away and drops the head 5 to 10 (starting values). keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", gives the same status read (a level, still head against a dropped head). Polish a cycle's contact and passing poses before anything else in it.
- **Exaggeration is intensity, not size.** 11 Second Club, "The Top 5": "Exaggeration doesn't simply mean 'make things bigger.' It is more often about increasing the visual intensity of a pose, even if it's a smaller performance." The same article notes that voters see an entry's poster frame, a pose, before they press play, and start judging from it. On R6: for a small or inward emote (protective, shy, cold), push the line rather than the reach: the torso hunched forward 10 to 15 degrees, the head down, both arms drawn in with a gap to the torso (starting values). Make the first frame of every emote a readable pose, not the middle of a blend.

## Anticipation and holds

- **Build energy in the hold before a big move.** Jean-Luc Delhougne (AnimSchool, "Blocking plus workflow: breakdowns"): when the key poses are right but the movement "starts or stops abruptly", shape the moving hold, "that subtle buildup of energy before a big motion". Nudge the hips down and forward and add slight opposition in the spine and head; an arm shifting forward or the head lifting slightly also helps. "It's not about big moves—it's about involving the whole body in the motion." On R6: in the 6 to 10 frames before a big action, sink torso `py` 0.03 to 0.08 studs, nudge the torso a little forward and tilt the head 2 to 4 degrees against it (starting values); `Feet.flat` keeps the soles flat. The pull-back of lesson-videos.md ("The same tricks in Poser (our translation, 60 fps)") still comes before the load.
- **Leave the anticipation out only for a surprise.** Gwilym Morris (AnimSchool, "Acting through anticipation"): a "surprise gag" leaves the anticipation pose out to surprise the audience. Anticipation can also mislead, as when a character's reaction makes a light object look heavy. On R6: SKILL.md asks for a pull-back before every move. Drop it only in a cutscene or an emote whose point is surprise (a gag emote). In gameplay, stand summons included, keep at least one frame of pull-back (anime-craft.md, "Action beats"), and never drop it on an NPC attack the player must read (combat.md, "Telegraphs and reaction time").
- **Hold an apex longer than reality, except on attacks the player must dodge.** Cooper (chapter 3): "Real life never looks real enough"; a real action copied exactly looks slow and imperfect, so poses are "accentuated and held a little longer than in reality". A real bouncing ball peaks on a smooth parabola, but an animator "may choose to hold longer at the apex (creating anticipation in the process) before zooming back to the ground, much like a martial-arts character thundering down with a flying kick". On R6: principles.md, "Jump and landing", keeps one parabola with no hang at the apex. Override it only for a strike from the air in a player move or a cinematic, and state it as a style choice. Slow the apex with `warp` (0.4 to 0.6 over 6 to 10 frames) and play the drop at 1.2 to 1.5 (starting values). A Humanoid physics jump keeps its own arc, and the clip does not add one (lesson-videos.md, "The same rules on an R6 rig (our translation)", ballistic flight). An enemy's jump attack keeps its rise and fall similar (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Rhythm, idle and readability in action games").

## Watching your own animation

- **Flip back and forth between consecutive keys.** 11 Second Club, Helpful Hints, "Back and Forth": step between two consecutive key poses with the next-key and previous-key buttons, over and over, as a 2D animator rolls drawings. In the example from The Incredibles, Bob's main change is one arm going out, yet every part moves. The shoulder goes up with the arm, the hips shift a little toward that side ("it sells the idea of the body's inertia"), the other shoulder moves up, the other arm pulls in with its fist tighter, and the head rotates toward the pose and tilts down. On R6: capture each key alone with `_G.editStrip(clip, {t}, {facing = "rear34"})` from the same camera and flip between the images. Print each joint's change between the two keys (`Poser.posesAt` at both times). A joint at 0 while another moves 30 degrees or more is a dead part (lesson-videos.md, "3. Kuzillon: six beginner mistakes", cutting corners). For a raised arm, roll the torso so that shoulder rises, shift torso `px` a little toward it, and turn and tilt the head toward the pose by a few degrees (starting values).
- **Every frame is a drawing: test random frames.** 11 Second Club, "Every Frame Is a Drawing": make a conscious decision on every frame. Ask of each frame, in-betweens included, whether a collector would hang it on a wall like a cel, and try it on a frame chosen at random from your own scene. Something about CG "fools our eyes into taking a lot longer to see that it's not good", and "You can set keys on every body part on every single frame if you want to". On R6: capture 5 random times that are not key times with `_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "rear34"})`. Fix any frame whose line of action or silhouette breaks (an arm inside the torso, a twinned pair, a limb off its socket) with a breakdown key (the count is a starting value).
- **Review frame by frame, in a file that steps.** Animator Island, "Want an Animation Critique? Use Quicktime!": animation is an illusion made of single images, so a critique has to break it back into frames. YouTube and animated GIFs do not allow frame-by-frame stepping. A QuickTime file lets a reviewer go "one frame at a time and roll/flip the images", which gives specific notes instead of "it needs more easing". On R6: give reviewers the recorded video file (pipeline.md, "Review videos (scripts/video)") or the per-frame captures, not only a streamed upload. quality-review.md already asks for frame stepping to locate faults.
- **Check the side view before polishing to the camera.** Holiday Liu (lead animator, Steamroller Studios, on 80.lv): when a polish-stage shot feels mechanically off, she checks the side view. She often finds the action was cheated to the camera view, leaving an unrealistic side view that is hard to fix at polish. Get the motion right in perspective first, then polish spacing, arcs and silhouettes to the camera. Cooper: game actions must "look great from all angles", and knowing how bodies should look from any angle "means cheating is out of the question". On R6: SKILL.md stages for the player's camera (hands and weapon to the side it sees). That staging holds only while the side and front strips still read as a balanced body. Pass the side strip (balance, push, brace; SKILL.md, steps 4 and 5) before any arc or spacing polish from `rear34`.
- **Fresh eyes come from time away and from non-animators.** Brian Lemay, "Reviewing and analyzing your pencil test": without a teacher, set the assignment aside "for a week or so" and look at it again. Write the critique out, and keep a note of the error you keep making ("Don't forget the overlapping action!!"). Liu: work on several shots and switch between them for fresh eyes, and practise giving yourself notes. Tim Borrelli (Game Developer, "Game Animation Bootcamp: an expert roundtable Q&A"): non-animators "won't try to find an animator solution for you, they'll just tell you what feels wrong to them". On R6: keyframe-workflow.md, "Review with fresh eyes", covers the fresh reviewer and the flipped capture. Add a running list of the notes this project keeps getting (a lagged guard hand, a dead hold, a sliding brace) and check each new clip against it before review (the Review table of [clip-plan.md](../templates/clip-plan.md)). Re-review a clip in a later session before hand-off, and ask a non-animator what feels wrong, not how to fix it.
- **Take the note's intent, not its letter.** Liu: understand the reason behind a note. Her example: "reduce head drag and make it sharper and direct" may mean the character reacts to a sudden sound and too much head motion would distract. Then reduce the movement and drag, and judge the fix against that reason. On R6: when a reviewer calls a head floaty, find out what the head is doing. Reacting to a sound, it leads (about 5 frames for a reflex, keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting"). Holding a target, it counters the torso with no lag. Being carried, it gets a shorter `lag` or the stiffer `lead` spring in place of `follow`. process.md covers how to ask.

## Common notes and their fixes

- **The five comments posted most on contest entries.** Scheur, "The Top 5": "You aren't ready for the competition" or "You should return to the basics" (bounce a ball, match a pose from a magazine photo, shift the weight from one leg to the other), "You need stronger poses", "too swimmy / too computer-y / too weightless", "Your character's energy doesn't match the dialogue", and "Your camera moves are much too distracting". On R6: the weight shift is the first test for a new rig or controller (`Feet.flat` with the COM moving over one sole; body-mechanics.md, "Balance and weight shift"). The pose and swimmy notes are answered above ("Exaggeration is intensity, not size", "Fixing floaty motion"). Keep the review camera still in emote and cutscene captures.
- **"The other arm looks glued to the torso."** On an R6 combo posted to the DevForum, a reviewer asked for the non-punching arm to move up and down or rotate "to make it not look like completely glued to torso". The animator answered that it guards the head or charges the next punch, and that the torso's quick rotation hides its motion. On R6 both are right. A guard arm must not wear `lag` (principles.md, "Overlap: successive breaking of joints"), but it needs keyed motion of its own: pull the guard fist back toward the chin as the other arm strikes, as the example cross does on its contact frame (principles.md, "A worked example").
- **"The torso doesn't read as turning."** A DevForum reply to a solo developer's animations said that although the torsos move, "it still feels like they are facing the front still", perhaps because the rig is all one colour. Another reply advised making the torso's rotation speed go "slow → fast → slow again". On R6: push the twist until the shoulder line visibly changes from `rear34`; turns of 56 and 64 degrees read as force (principles.md, "Weight and support"). A turn that ends in a pose splines slow-fast-slow under `auto`. A turn that drives a strike keeps its speed through the contact key (SKILL.md, "Follow-through and through motion"). Carry the arms with it.
- **"Like hitting the replay button."** On a looping animation posted to the DevForum, a reply said it "looks a bit static, like someone is just hitting the reverse/replay button over and over". It advised easing in and out at the turnarounds and making "a few slight variations of the loop". On R6: `auto` already eases each turnaround. Spread the joints' reversal frames 2 to 4 frames apart so the body never reverses on one frame (`Poser.check` unison), and put the variation in idle variants rather than in the loop itself (keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing"; the next section).

## Game polish: priorities, blends and loops

- **Polish what the player sees most, first.** Overwatch 2 animation team: they start with the common movements, because a player is much more likely to spend time standing in the lobby, using the primary fire or running around the map. They perfect those for the hero's personality before complex animations like Ultimates. A "rough draft" borrowed from other heroes gets a new hero into playtests early, and playtesting runs throughout. On R6: order the work by screen time: idle, walk and run, the basic attack chain, then specials and emotes. Put every move in the game first as a placeholder (an existing clip or the stepped blocking) and playtest before polishing any one of them.
- **One main idle, rare variants, one shared end pose.** The Animate script quoted in a DevForum thread lists three idle animations weighted 1, 1 and 9. An R6 copy of the Animate script quoted in another thread keeps two idle entries weighted 9 and 1 (both pointing at the same custom clip there). In the first thread, one reply suggests that the variants add variety and that one main idle should play most of the time. It agrees that every variant should end in about the same pose, and warns that too many variants make that shared ending repetitive. On R6: in the native Animate script, keep the R6 default of 9 to 1, one main idle and a variant shown about 10% of the time. Weight variants at 10 to 33% depending on how distinct they are: a distinct variant near 10%, a subtle one up to a third (our choice; r6-craft.md, "Idle variations play by weight"). Start and end each on the main idle's first pose so any pick loops cleanly. principles.md, "Idle or held stance", gives the variation cadence.
- **Ease the cross-fade between clips.** DevForum, "How to make a smoother transition between two animations": the poster found that the fade of `Play(fadeTime)` looked linear. Replies drove `AdjustWeight` every frame from a tweened value (Exponential InOut over 1 s; Exponential Out over 0.5 s) for a smoother hand-off. One noted that a weight of exactly 0 broke replication and used 0.05 or 0.001 instead; another got a similar result from `Stop(0.25)`. Roblox's AnimationTrack reference says the weight moves from WeightCurrent to WeightTarget over the fade time but does not name the curve. On R6: Poser's `blend = "inertial"` already eases its offset on a smoothstep over `fadeIn` (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract", which also sizes the fade from the pose distance). For native tracks an eased weight ramp is runtime code in the game's controller, not a clip field (stylized-and-roblox.md, "The Animator: fades, priorities and weights", covers near-zero weights).
- **A walk and a jog cancel each other's bounce.** Jonathan Cooper, "Basics: Animation Blending" (gameanim.com, 2005): blended cycles must be of equal length, sync, and start on the same pose (he uses the right-foot-up passing pose). Blending a walk with a jog makes the character "plane along without a bounce midway between the blend", because the walk's passing pose is its highest point while the same pose is the lowest, weight-taking frame of a jog. On R6: principles.md, "Walk", has the same phase difference (a walk bobs highest over the stance foot, a run lowest). Before blending two cycles by speed, compare their torso `py` at the same phase, and play the blend at the middle of its band to see whether the bob flattens (the locomotion starter blends to the run over speeds 18 to 26, pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"). Poser plays one clip per rig, so a weight blend of two cycles needs runtime code; the starter's procedural clip is one way.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Leaving the shoulder open when the whole arm is placed where a real forearm would be (a reply in the R6 combat thread on the DevForum, "How could I improve on the impact of these anims?"): the open shoulder breaks the arm contact rule (SKILL.md, "Feet rule", which holds for arms too). The forearm pose itself transfers when the arm slides until its top end (the elbow) rests on the torso (r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons"); the alternative is the bend as a 0.3 to 0.6 stud piston (SKILL.md, "No elbows or knees").
- Rotating and scaling limbs past the rig so the elbows rest on the knees (11 Second Club, "The Top 5"): R6 parts cannot scale and have no elbows or knees.
- Breaking the rig on fast frames for smears (McCree's arm and D.Va's leg in Gibson's talk, in Sokolova's notes; "breaking bodies" in Mariel Cartwright's Skullgirls talk, on 80.lv): R6 parts cannot stretch; combat.md, "Smears and trails", gives the Trail substitute.
- Eye poses and chosen blinks as the life of a moving hold (Kelly's moving-hold newsletter): R6 has no eyes by default; the head leads and a face decal swap stands in (keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting").
- Finger curls and finger overshoots (11 Second Club, "The Top 5"; Navone's spline-hygiene exception): R6 has no fingers.
- Drag through the mid and upper spine (Lango's offset example): the R6 torso is one part, so the drag moves to the arms and the head and is keyed there ("Drag down the chain").
