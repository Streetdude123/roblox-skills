# R6 craft: making six parts read human

How R6 animators make six rigid blocks read as a human body: why motion carries the read, how slides and pistons stand in for knees, elbows, a hunch and toes, when a limb may leave the torso, how the head acts without a face, how far to push a pose, what R6 keeps and loses against R15, how battlegrounds games build and time their move sets, how locomotion, idle and emote sets are structured, and the tools R6 animators use. Every finding was checked against its source ([sources.md](sources.md), "R6 craft: making six parts read human research"). Most sources are R6 animators' posts on the Roblox DevForum, and each claim is stated as strongly as its poster stated it. Frame counts are at 60 fps; conversions from seconds are ours.

## Why six blocks can read human

- **Joint motion, not body shape, carries the human read.** A 2025 review of biological motion research (Frontiers in Psychology) restates Johansson's 1973 result: observers immediately recognise human motion from sparse point-light displays that show only the movement of the major joints. Spatial scrambling breaks the body's layout but keeps each joint's own movement, and people can still read walking direction from it (Troje and Westhoff). Inverted displays are rated less animate, which the review says may reflect the direction of gravity in the local motion. On R6: the six parts and the root give seven paths, and the body reads when each path moves like the joint it stands for. Print the hand, foot and head-top paths (SKILL.md, method step 3, "Arcs and paths") and check two things. The arms and the torso twist run in counter-phase to the legs (locomotion.md, "Walk body motion"). Every fall shows gravity (our reading of the gravity result). Under `auto` a key where a channel turns around gets a zero tangent (`tangents` in `scripts/Poser.lua`), so a low point keyed that way eases in and floats. A part that falls into a contact, or to a bottom it bounces off, gets `e = "quad", d = "in"` on its low key, keyed from the rest pose above it, or a key one frame past the contact so the contact is no longer a turn-around (polish-and-critique.md, "Pick the tangent by the cause of the motion"). A part that only swings through its low point, such as a pendulum arm, keeps `auto`, because its channel does not turn around there. A rising part slows into a turn-around key at the top. A still frame may look blocky; the paths decide whether it reads as a body.
- **Embrace the rigid parts and set each character's range of motion.** Rob Coleman, Animal Logic's head of animation (overseeing The LEGO Movie), says the team embraced the limits of hard bricks (no bending, no squash and stretch, no motion blur) to build a faux stop-motion style, and that The LEGO Batman Movie changed its animation style because Batman needed a greater range of motion than Emmet. On R6: never fake bends with extra parts or scaling; use the substitutes in this file. Set each character's range in the clip plan before keying. An everyday walker stays near the walk ranges (torso twist about plus or minus 3.5 degrees, locomotion.md, "Walk body motion"). A floating stand or companion may use the range of the decoded stand strikes, a torso twist of 110 to 124 degrees per strike (the-world-clips.md, "Recorded interpretation of these clips"). A grounded fighter's twist is limited by its planted legs: drop the torso with the turn and pivot the feet with the hips (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", rigid legs and toe yaw).
- **Sell a bend on the move, not in the held pose.** Matt Everitt, animation director on The LEGO Ninjago Movie at Animal Logic: "You can't really bend the elbows of a minifig, say, but we can make the 'feeling' of a bent elbow when you watch it on the run." Each frame was hand-crafted, and instead of motion blur the animators added extra legal bricks to exaggerate a move or stand in for a smear frame ("brick blur"). On R6: put the biggest fake bends on the fastest frames (our reading of the quote). Before a strike the striking arm slides back along its own length; on the strike frames it shoots forward, 0.3 to 0.6 studs in the reference punches (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", no elbows or knees). A stepping leg slides up into the hip around its passing frames (r6-mechanics.md, "Walk and run legs": 0.37 studs in a reference walk, 0.7 to 0.95 in a run). The R6 stand-in for brick blur is a Trail, not an extra part (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Smears and impact frames").
- **Straight limbs are part of the R6 look.** Under a 60 fps R6 dance emote on the DevForum, a developer noted that animators usually add elbows and knees to a character that lacks them ("Minecraft animations always have those weirdly bendy limbs"), but R6 animators keep the limbs straight, "and the funny thing is... it works". On R6: never add bend parts, bent meshes or extra joints to a player character for a clip. A rig with extra joints is a custom rig (custom-rigs.md).

## Faking joints with slides and pistons

- **The knee sits at the hip: pose the leg as a shin.** An R6 developer who makes all the animations for their own games advises pretending that the R6 knee is located at the hip, and says the same applies to the arms to a degree. A procedural R6 foot-planting answer on the DevForum does the same in code: when the foot comes closer to the hip than the 2 stud leg length, displace the hip joint, which "will make it look like as if the leg is 'bending' it's knee"; its pseudo-code offsets the hip joint by up to 0.5 studs up and 0.05 forward, blended by the foot's distance over the leg length. On R6: give a lifted or airborne leg whose knee must read (a knee strike, a tuck, the front swing of a run) the reference shin's angle, not the thigh's (our reading: the visible leg is the shin); the knee a viewer reads on a lifted leg is its far upper edge (stylized-and-roblox.md, "R6 critique from experienced animators"). On stance and contact frames, where the sole must land where the human foot lands (`Feet.flat`), and through a walk's swing, use the hip-to-foot chord instead (human-motion-data.md, the knee rule at the top). A stepping or kneeing leg slides up by `p = Vector3.new(0, 0.5 * k, -0.05 * k)`, with k from 0 at full reach to 1 when the foot is tucked under the hip (our mapping of the answer's intent); a run's swing leg goes further, 0.7 to 0.95 studs (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", runs). A planted leg keeps its sole flat (feet rule), and vertical legs slid up into a dropped torso read as standing, so show a crouch's weight with a spread brace (r6-mechanics.md, "Centre of mass and balance", weight shows in a spread brace).
- **Pose the arm as the forearm when the elbow matters.** Answering a claim that R6 cannot show a punch being prepared and an elbow extending, a developer wrote that it can: "move the character's full arm based on the position of a real person (or R15 character)'s forearm", which "just requires being willing to disconnect the 'shoulder' of the arm from the torso". On R6: for a cocked hook or an uppercut load, take the forearm direction from the reference, solve the arm to it (r6-mechanics.md, "Direction and rotation checks"; a raised forearm uses the up form), then slide the arm until its top end (the elbow) rests on the torso's side or front. An arm pointed up puts the fist at head-top height (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", overhead reach), so slide it down to bring the fist to the chin. On the strike, turn the arm to the punch line and slide it home as the fist travels. A 120 degree change needs at least 5 frames under the 25 degrees per frame limit (SKILL.md, "4. Measure before you look"). Check the path from the player's camera with `_G.editStrip`. For a boxing guard use principles.md, "Guard, block and parry". The source's open shoulder conflicts with this skill's contact rule; see "Limbs on the body: when a gap is allowed".
- **Lower the arm to put a hand at the belt.** The unofficial Moon Animator 2 guide on the DevForum (323 likes) warns R6 beginners not to raise an arm too high in a sword idle, because it then reads as lifting the sword; its fix moves the part down, with "no need to bend an R6 physically". The four decoded community R6 walk clips carry their arms 0.07 to 0.36 studs below rest (walk-cycles.md, "Ranges per clip"). On R6: for a hand at the hip or belt (a sheathed hilt, a relaxed guard, a hunch), slide the arm down `p.y` -0.2 to -0.35 instead of lifting it further. Most of the arm still overlaps the torso side, so it keeps contact.
- **Recoil is a torso turn plus an arm piston.** Reviewing an R6 firing animation, a developer suggested rotating the torso a bit and moving the arm back "a stud or two" and then forward again for a better recoil. On R6: on each shot, turn the gun shoulder back (torso twist -4 to -8 degrees for a right-hand gun) and slide the gun arm 0.2 to 0.4 studs back along its length over 2 to 3 frames, then return over 6 to 10 frames on `auto` keys. The head keeps the eyes on the target: it counters the torso turn on the torso's frames and gets no `lag` (SKILL.md, method step 3, "Offset the chain"). The sizes are our starting values for a pistol; the source's "a stud or two" is larger than the 0.3 to 0.6 stud strike pistons in SKILL.md ("No elbows or knees").
- **A hunch pitches the torso, sinks the head and drops the arms.** Reworking an R6 walk after critique, its author lowered the arms, placed the head lower on the torso looking slightly down, exaggerated the up and down motion of the torso and arms, and slowed the clip to the character's in-game speed. For the same author's hunched animation, a reviewer asked for both arms very slightly angled into the torso and slightly lowered, which "implies more weight", and another asked for the head to look down more. On R6: for a hunched, tired or wary posture, start by pitching the torso 8 to 15 degrees forward from the waist (`waist`, r6-mechanics.md, "Lean from the waist"), as polish-and-critique.md, "Exaggeration is intensity, not size", hunches an inward emote 10 to 15 degrees, and counter-pitch the head so its eye-line suits the beat: about 8 to 15 degrees down in the world for a tired look, level for a wary one. Then hold the head sink and the arm drop as offsets: key the head at `p = Vector3.new(0, -0.15, 0)` (0.1 to 0.2 down, sunk into the torso; our reading is that this reads as raised shoulders). Slide both arms down 0.1 to 0.2 studs and angle them a few degrees in and forward, so the hands hang just in front of the thighs. Hold these offsets through the clip and let `life` keep the rotations drifting. For sadness, use the head drop in locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk" (about 19 degrees).
- **The decoded stand clips never translate the head.** Measured on the eight decoded clips in `references/decodes/` (a stand's seven strikes and its idle, on a standard R6 rig): the Head's `p` is 0.00 on every key, so all head motion is rotation; the torso moves at most 0.46 studs on any axis and the limbs up to 1.64. The four decoded community walk clips agree (head y 0.00 to 0.02, walk-cycles.md, "Ranges per clip"). On R6: keep the `Head` keys' `p` at zero in combat and locomotion; turn the head and let the torso carry it. Keep a head sink for a held posture (the hunch above), never per beat.
- **No toes: a rise onto the toes becomes a small hop.** Critiquing an R6 sword swing copied from a video, a reviewer pointed out that the reference extends her legs and rises onto her tiptoes at the end, which Roblox cannot do ("no toes"), "so a hop might work instead". On R6: at the extreme where the reference rises, lift both feet and the torso 0.15 to 0.25 studs and land into a compact pose. At the default gravity of 196.2 studs/s^2 (body-mechanics.md, "Gravity, jumps and falls on Roblox") such a hop lasts 2 x sqrt(2h / g) = 0.078 to 0.101 s, about 5 to 6 frames (our calculation). A foot target may be a function of time that returns a lift (pipeline.md, "Planted feet"):

  ```lua
  local function hop(x, z, yaw, t0, h, d)
  	return function(t)
  		local u = math.clamp((t - t0) / d, 0, 1)
  		return {x, z, yaw, 4 * h * u * (1 - u)}
  	end
  end

  post = Feet.flat({r = hop(0.5, 0, 0, 0.4, 0.2, 0.09), l = hop(-0.5, 0, 0, 0.4, 0.2, 0.09)})
  ```

  Key the torso `p.y` on the same arc, so the legs keep their place against it: 0 at 0.4, 0.2 at 0.445 with `e = "quad"` (direction `out`), and 0 at 0.49 with `e = "quad", d = "in"`. These two eases trace the feet's `4 * h * u * (1 - u)` exactly (our derivation). On `auto` keys the takeoff and the landing get zero tangents, so the torso would ease off and onto the floor while the feet move at full speed, and the legs would slide up into the torso mid-hop.
- **A foot that slides on purpose must leave the ground.** In critique of R6 skill animations, a reviewer said a planted leg should stay still unless the move is a jump; when the author explained that the back foot's slide was a push-off for power, the reviewer answered that if the slide is on purpose, the player should hop off the floor while sliding. On R6: a planted sole never slides (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", feet rule). For a push-off drag, lift that foot at least 0.12 studs with its target's `lift` value on the frames it travels (r6-mechanics.md, "Stance changes as steps": a 0.06 stud lift read as a drag), or keep it planted and move the torso instead.
- **A rigid-leg walk keeps the back foot down and the torso upright.** Josh Whitehouse (Go Chatter Studios; Best of Brickworld Chicago Film International Award, 2024) on a minifigure walk: after the leading leg moves off its stud, "balance the minifigure's back foot on the stud, while keeping the torso upright", then place the front foot on the next stud; opposite hands and legs always move in the same direction. An R6 reviewer gave a beginner whose walk looked like "a sad person is moonwalking on ice" the same reference: for R6, "the best practice is lego stop motion". On R6: this is double support (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", walks need double support: each foot planted about 55 to 60% of the cycle) with the torso lift inside the walk's plus or minus 2 degrees (locomotion.md, "Walk body motion"). Plant both soles with `Feet.flat`, and swing each arm with the opposite leg (stylized-and-roblox.md, "R6 critique from experienced animators").

## Limbs on the body: when a gap is allowed

- **R6 animators split on detached limbs; a player character keeps contact.** Some R6 animators detach limbs on purpose: one says limb detachment "is only way I know how to make good animation for r6"; another calls detaching a limb "a good option when exaggerating"; a third says that in almost any game with R6 animations the arms "99% of the time will disconnect from the torso at some point", because with flat colours seen from one angle an arm must be separated for the player to see how it is posed. Reviewers in the same threads push back: "quite a lot of obvious limb detatchment", "the arms are too far off the body", and, under a showcase set of four M1s with an uppercut and a downslam, "Pretty good for anything except actual gameplay. To make it compatible and gameplay friendly, I would not disconnect the limbs and drag them too far away from the torso." This conflicts with the skill's contact rule (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", feet rule; r6-mechanics.md, "Joint gaps"), which stays the rule for player characters, because other players see them from every side. A gap is allowed on a floating stand or companion (the-world-clips.md, "Recorded interpretation of these clips", limbs float) and in a cut-scene seen from one fixed camera, where one reviewer's fix was to change the angle to hide the floating arm. To make a joined arm read, pose it outside the torso's outline from the camera (principles.md, "Poses first: the posing checklist", negative space) and tilt a far-reaching arm's top back toward its socket (stylized-and-roblox.md, "R6 critique from experienced animators").

## The head and acting without a face

- **Look, look, look, then act.** For The LEGO Ninjago Movie, Animal Logic studied how Jackie Chan, in the middle of a fight, "has an idea, looks at an object, looks at an opponent, looks back at the object and then figures out how to use the object", and applied that to its scenes. On R6 the default head has no face rig, so the head turn is the eye-line. Key `Head` twist and lift to the object, to the opponent and back to the object. Make each look a quick dart of 8 to 12 frames (principles.md, "Timing and spacing in frames", head turn) into a hold that keeps drifting (principles.md, "Moving holds and settles"). A reflex dart holds one or two frames; a look that shows a thought, as here, holds longer, and 10 to 20 frames is our starting value. The head leads, and the body's action starts only after the last look (keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting").
- **Animate on a head with a face.** A reviewer of R6 walks advised that it is "much easier to animate if you use a ROBLOX avatar with the standard ROBLOX head so you can see exactly what's happening". On R6: keep the face decal on while judging acting and head direction in strips and captures; remove it only for the silhouette check (SKILL.md, "Appeal" row).
- **Do not stare at the floor in a walk.** Feedback on a first R6 walk named its fault "bouncy horror look at the floor syndrome". On R6: in a neutral walk keep the head lift inside the decoded walk clips' -4 to +8 degrees (walk-cycles.md, "Ranges per clip") and the bob at the measured walk size (torso `p.y` range about 0.14 studs, locomotion.md, "Walk body motion").

## How far to push on six parts

- **Push the torso and the timing, not the resting limbs' lift.** R6 animators call for exaggeration: one ties it to rigs with fewer joints than a human and to common practice in Bionicle stop-motion. But over-lifted limbs read wrong: "any R6 walk animation that raises the legs above the waist like that instead of just doing something like the normal 'swinging' motion just looks very weird" (a personal opinion with 13 likes); a sword-idle arm raised too high reads as lifting the sword (the Moon Animator 2 guide); and walk legs that "jutt extremely far out" read as a crouch. On R6: in locomotion and idles put the exaggeration into torso twist, lean and drop and into the timing contrast; in strikes and gestures also push the acting limb's arc and piston (SKILL.md, "Exaggeration" row). In a walk keep the legs inside the three decoded community walks' -54 to +30 degrees (Walk1 and Walk3 swing -27 to -31 back and Walk2 -54; all three +24 to +30 forward; walk-cycles.md, "Ranges per clip") and each leg's top on the torso (SKILL.md, feet rule); Walk4's +51 belongs to a run. A run is the exception: its swing leg goes up to about 80 degrees against the torso (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", runs).
- **One style per clip: a one-frame jump in smooth motion looks odd.** In feedback on a beginner's dive animation, a reviewer pointed out that at 0:05 to 0:06 "the arms seems to move in just about 1 frame while the other animations are very smooth", and said a 1-frame move is fine as a style (like stop motion) but looks odd here. On R6: choose smooth (spline, springs, `life`) or stepped (`e = "step"` keys with a hold list, stylized-and-roblox.md, "Stepped anime timing") for the whole clip. In a smooth clip give a 90 degree change at least 4 frames (25 degrees per frame, SKILL.md, "4. Measure before you look"). A contact pose held for an impact frame or hitstop is a hold, not a jump, and stays allowed (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Smears and impact frames").
- **A crouch walk holds the arms and tucks the legs.** Advice on an R6 crouch walk: the torso's "Local-X orientation" should be lower, the legs "more huddled in to the chest", the head angled up from the torso or down, and "keep the arms at a fixed position rather then moving them with the legs". On R6: lean the torso forward from the waist (our reading of "Local-X lower"; 15 to 25 degrees is our starting value; `waist`, r6-mechanics.md, "Lean from the waist"), drop it so `Feet.flat` slides the legs up into it, hold the arms in one guard pose with only `life` drift, and take the cycle numbers from custom-rigs.md, "A crouch walk".

## R6 against R15

- **R15 needs its middle joints; R6 motion does not.** A reviewer of a stiff R15 walk advised using all the joints, "instead of just the ones connected to the torso" (knees and elbows), to bring flow. Going the other way, replies in another thread say R15 games that want the R6 look animate only the UpperArm to mimic the whole R6 arm, or convert R6 animations with a plugin. One reviewer said a crouching R6 walk "would be hell to animate with R15", citing overlapping parts and fluidity "quite hard to replicate compared to R6". On R6: an R15 reference with bent elbows and knees maps onto R6 through the shin and forearm rules above, not by copying the upper-segment angles. Drop R15 clips as direct sources for an R6 rig (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference", step 3).
- **The Strongest Battlegrounds is said to run on R6.** Asked whether The Strongest Battlegrounds and Deepwoken run R15 rigs that look like R6, one developer was "fairly certain" that both games use R6, and another confirmed it for The Strongest Battlegrounds in game. The first added that R6's collision and weight are the same whatever bundle a player wears, and that R6 suits a game that must be completely fair. On R6: a move set plays on one body size for every player, so the poses and the timing carry the character; author on a clean R6 rig and apply avatars on spawn (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays").
- **Roblox's own R15 content does not serve R6.** Roblox's emote pilot FAQ says R15 emotes with additional joints "will technically play back" on R6 avatars, but "the result won't look correct. R6 support is on our future roadmap." Animation Capture - Body, the editor's video capture, generates animations for R15 rigs. On R6: these emotes and capture output are not R6 sources; author R6 sets by hand from references, or copy a video with video-rotoscope.md.

## How battlegrounds sets are built and timed

- **A battlegrounds M1 set: four hits with three endings.** The Jujutsu Shenanigans fan wiki documents its M1: startup 0.16 to 0.23 s, often 0.2 or 0.23 s; a landed M1 stuns about 0.75 s and pulls the attacker about 1 stud toward the target; a blocked M1 has double the endlag; up to four hits chain (3 + 3 + 4 + 4 damage), then 2 s of downtime. The fourth hit ragdolls and has three variants by vertical movement: neutral (knockback, the shortest ragdoll), uppercut when rising or holding jump, and downslam when falling (unblockable). A player's summary of battlegrounds games on the DevForum gives the same shape (four M1s, the fourth ragdolls, a downslam from jump then M1, an uppercut from holding jump), and an R6 animator's showcase set holds "4 m1s an uppercut and a down slam". On R6: author M1_1 to M1_4 with contacts on frames 10 to 14 (our conversion), each ending in the next one's load (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", combo flow), plus an uppercut and a downslam as alternative fourth hits. Drive the 1 stud advance per landed hit from a root travel curve (pipeline.md, "Networked actions, hits and knockback", root travel from the clip). These contacts are later than the fighting-game light attack in combat.md, "Frame budgets: startup, active, recovery" (contact on frame 2 to 7) and than SKILL.md's 1 to 8 frame player anticipation: match the game's own hit time.
- **The dash set: a travelling jab forward, quick-steps to the side.** In Jujutsu Shenanigans the forward dash slides about 25 studs while winding up a jab, then punches (6 s cooldown); side and back dashes are quick-steps of about 17.5 studs that the player can steer by turning and can use during the startup of an M1 or ability (2 s cooldown). The DevForum summary lists front, side and back dashes, with front and back sharing a cooldown. On R6: the forward dash is one clip whose travel pose already holds the cocked jab (torso twisted away, arm slid back) and whose strike lands at the end of the travel. A side or back quick-step is a short push, a lean into the travel and a braced stop (SKILL.md, method step 3, "Push before speed, stopper before stop"). It can start during an M1's startup, so build it as legs and torso and let the arms come from the M1 in a composite clip (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays").
- **A short combo keeps the feet planted; the free arm guards or loads.** A commission animator explained an R6 three-hit combo: the legs stay in place because switching them would happen too often in only three hits, and with five or more hits the legs would switch; the non-punching arm "is either guarding the head or charging up for the next punch", and the punches are fast "because it's for an anime game". Reviewing the same animator's next set, another developer asked for faster punch startup on basic mouse-click attacks. On R6: in a three-hit set keep both feet on their spots and take the reach from torso twist, pivoting each foot's yaw with the hips (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", toe yaw); in a chain of five or more, switch the lead foot. Keep the free hand turned in toward the head or loading the next punch (stylized-and-roblox.md, "R6 critique from experienced animators"), and keep a light attack's anticipation at 1 to 8 frames (SKILL.md, "Anticipation" row).
- **A cut-scene camera can ride the rig as an animated part.** A VFX artist's breakdown of a recreated "Serious Punch" move welds a part to the player's HumanoidRootPart, animates it in Moon Animator 2 as the camera (Moon's camera AttachToPart property previews it), exports it with the animation, and sets the client camera to that part every frame; AutoRotate must be off during the cut-scene or shift lock spins the camera. A reviewer of the move asked for "more acceleration coming into" the punch. On R6: a camera part on its own Motor6D is one more Poser joint, keyed like a prop; following it with the client camera is game code, and SKILL.md asks before any camera takeover. Bring the fist into the contact at rising speed, never easing into it, so the contact frame or the frame before it carries the fist's peak speed (SKILL.md, "Spacing, slow in and slow out" row). The measured loss of about 20% before contact (human-motion-data.md, "Punches") is for a realistic NPC or cinematic straight only.
- **Classic-style R6 games ask for simple, readable, responsive motion.** A developer hiring for a city game modelled on 2013 to 2014 Roblox asked for R6 work "that feels simple, readable, and responsive rather than overly realistic". On R6: for a classic style use fewer key poses, short anticipation and quick recoveries, readable from the player's camera, and keep the motion layers (spline, key offsets, `life`) that make simple poses feel alive.

## Locomotion, idle and emote sets

- **A locomotion set has seven states and hard limits.** Roblox's Marketplace animation packs (R15) cover seven locomotion states: Idle, Walk, Run, Jump, Fall, Climb and Swim (with a Swim Idle), up to two weighted idle clips, 8 to 9 clips in all, and every clip loops except Jump. Upload validation rejects a clip that moves the root joint too far from its start, exceeds a maximum frame-to-frame speed, translates a body part more than 1.5 studs from its starting position in any frame, or runs longer than 10 seconds. On R6: these rules are written for R15 packs; use the limits as a sanity check (our choice), but take the states from R6's own Animate script. A copy of it quoted on the DevForum lists idle (two entries weighted 9 and 1), walk, run, jump, fall, climb and sit, plus the emotes wave, point, three dances, laugh and cheer. Its `onRunning` plays the walk clip at every speed above 0.01, so the run entry never plays; it scales the playback to speed / 14.5 only when the walk is the default walk asset (180426354), so a custom walk plays at 1x whatever the WalkSpeed, and `onSwimming` only switches the pose between Running (the walk clip) and Standing: R6 has no swim clip. These clips play at Core priority. While a tool with a Handle is held, its `animateTool` plays toolnone at Idle priority, and toolslash or toollunge at Action priority when the tool parents a `toolanim` StringValue of "Slash" or "Lunge"; a second thread quotes the same function. So the R6 set is idle and a rare variant, walk (it also covers running and swimming), jump, fall, climb, sit and the three tool states. toolnone outranks the Core clips on the joints it keys, and a custom clip at Idle priority only blends with it (stylized-and-roblox.md, "The Animator: fades, priorities and weights"): a clip that must move the arms while a tool is equipped plays at Movement or higher (Action for an attack), or the game replaces the toolnone animation in its Animate copy. The slides in this file (0.3 to 0.6 stud pistons, leg slides under 1 stud) fit inside 1.5 studs; loops end on their first pose; Jump does not loop.
- **A combat set pairs each punch with its own reaction; a movement set adds transitions.** BIue_k's "[R6] High-Tier Combat System Animation Pack" on the Creator Store lists 14 clips: attacks (Light Punch 1, 2 and 3, Heavy Punch, Light and Heavy Throw), reactions (Light Punch Reactions 1, 2 and 3, a Heavy Punch Reaction, Knocked Floor, Flying and Flying Recovery) and a Block. The same creator's "[R6] High-Tier Movement Animation Pack" lists 9: Idle, Walk, Run, Jump, Fall, Crouch Idle and Crouch Walk, plus Land and Crouch Transition in a separate group. On R6: plan a combat set as attack and reaction pairs, the reaction keyed from the attack's contact frame (combat.md, "The victim on contact"), with the knockdown states (on the floor, flying, recovering) as their own clips. Plan a movement set with the transitions between its loops (land, crouch in and out) as one-shots that start and end on the loops' frames.
- **Idle variations play by weight.** In the default Animate script Idle has two variations, and each plays with probability weight / total weight (the docs' example: weights 5 and 10 play one third and two thirds of the time). On R6: R6's own Animate script weights its two idle entries 9 and 1, so the variant shows about 10% of the time (polish-and-critique.md, "One main idle, rare variants, one shared end pose"). Author a main idle and a variation that starts and ends on the main idle's frame, and weight the variation at 10 to 33% depending on how distinct it is: a distinct variant near 10%, a subtle one up to a third (our choice; macro idle variants, keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing").
- **One R6 emote brief: 5 to 10 seconds, copied from dance videos.** The team behind the game Noob Mayhem, hiring an R6 emote animator, supplied YouTube dance references and asked for 5 to 10 second emotes "in the form of an R6 roblox animation". On R6: copy the dance from the reference video (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference"; video-rotoscope.md for an exact copy) and place the beats on the music's loudness peaks (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays").

## Tools and workflow

- **Moon Animator 2: mirror, looper, time stretch and wiggler.** The unofficial Moon Animator 2 guide lists Mirror (for walks and left or right-handed actions), Looper (walk cycles), Time Stretch, Wiggler (two keyframes vibrating on waves) and grouped keyframes for pauses, and suggests spamming linear keys at 3 to 10 frames per second to imitate a graph editor for arcs, fake physics and ragdolls. It says CFrame tracks need scripts and do not export like Motor6D keys; a reply in the thread adds that scaling cannot be exported to Roblox either. In another thread a developer answers that Moon's onion skin shows neighbouring poses but does not move the rig; travel comes from keys on the root. On R6 in Poser: `curve = "spline"` gives the curves those dense linear keys imitate, and `Poser.bake` writes dense linear samples at export (pipeline.md, "Baking and export"). Time Stretch is the even stretch in the same section (scale every key time, marker and event time), or `warp` for a speed curve. `loop = true` plays a cycle, and `life` is the nearest thing to the Wiggler (slow noise, not a vibration). Poser has no mirror field: mirror a clip by the method in pipeline.md, "Importing sequences" (reflect each part's root-space frame, swap left and right, solve back through C0 and C1).
- **Animation Editor traps on R6.** Moving an R6 torso down for a crouch in the Animation Editor moved the whole grid; the poster's fix came from a reply pointing to anchoring the HumanoidRootPart. In 2026 the Adaptive Animations beta stopped the R6 Torso from rotating or moving in the editor until the beta was turned off, and R6 animations imported from Blender failed with "Torso has animation data but isn't moving" or played stiff with limbs not moving (reported May to July 2026, unresolved in those threads). On R6: after `Poser.bake`, play the KeyframeSequence on a clean R6 rig in Studio and check that the Torso pose changes and every limb moves before delivery.
- **Blender R6 rigs add IK, torso influence and onion skins.** Under a free R6 run made in Blender, its animator found Studio easier but Blender "way higher" in quality, and another developer recommended Moon Animator for higher quality inside Studio. Aeresei's R6 IK + FK Blender rig (363 likes on its DevForum post) blends IK and FK per limb, has a torso-influence setting so the limbs can ignore torso motion, a head that can follow a target, grab points, and three torso controls (UpperTorso, Spine, LowerTorso) that drive the single torso, whose pivot sits slightly lower than Roblox's. It needs Cautioned's Blender Animations plugin, and its IK and FK exist only in Blender; the Studio rig is a normal R6. Another R6 IK rig toggles leg IK and ships an onion-skin add-on. A 2026 Studio plugin, Apex Animator, plants feet and hands while the body moves, lets an arm stretch to reach, and bakes everything into ordinary joint keys. On R6: torso influence 0 on the legs is what `Feet.flat` does after the torso is final; an arm that stretches to reach is an arm slide, which must keep torso contact; the lower torso pivot is `waist` (r6-mechanics.md, "Lean from the waist").

## Does not transfer to R6

- Bendy limbs (the Minecraft style) and middle joints: an R6 player character has six rigid parts; extra joints make a custom rig.
- Squash and stretch by scaling: parts cannot scale in a clip, and Moon Animator scale tracks do not export (SKILL.md, "Squash and stretch" row).
- Brick blur (extra pieces added for one frame): a clip cannot add body parts; the substitute is a Trail (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Smears and impact frames").
- Rising onto the toes or rolling through the foot: R6 has no toes or ankles; the substitute is a small hop at a held extreme, and a back swing plus the swing slide in a walk or run toe-off (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", no toes).
- Facial acting: the default R6 head has no face rig; eye-lines become head turns.
- A wrist flick or a finger pose: the R6 hand is the end of the arm block; only the arm's twist turns the hand face (a held prop turns on its own grip joint, weapons.md).
- Video capture to animation (Animation Capture - Body): R15 rigs only.
- A detached limb on a player character: other players see it from every side (see "Limbs on the body: when a gap is allowed").
