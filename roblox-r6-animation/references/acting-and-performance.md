# Acting and performance on six parts

What senior character animators know about performance, translated to an R6 rig with no face rig, no elbows and no knees: thinking characters, subtext, gesture choices, character and status in the body, the head as the eyes, arm acting, rhythm and stillness, physical comedy, readability from the player camera, reference, and performance in games. Every finding was checked against its source ([sources.md](sources.md), "Acting and performance on six parts research"). Film sources count at 24 frames per second; every frame count here is converted to Poser's 60 fps (multiply by 2.5). Signs follow r6-mechanics.md, "Transform convention": positive `lift` lifts the gaze, swings a limb forward and leans the torso back; negative `p.z` is forward. Numbers marked "our" are starting values chosen for R6, not measurements. The acting rules in keyframe-workflow.md ("Acting"), anime-craft.md ("Acting with restraint", "Personality in movement") and r6-craft.md ("The head and acting without a face") are not repeated here; findings point to them where they meet.

## Thinking characters

- **Animate the thought behind the action, not the action.** Ollie Johnston's notes, written down by Glen Keane and published by John Lasseter in his SIGGRAPH 94 course notes: "Don't illustrate words or mechanical movements. Illustrate ideas or thoughts, with the attitudes and actions." "It is the thought and circumstances behind the action that will make the action interesting." His example sets "A man walks up to a mailbox, drops in his letter and walks away" against "A man desperately in love with a girl far away carefully mails a letter in which he has poured his heart out." Keane, interviewed by Phil on Film in 2011, passes the rule on as "Don't animate what the character is doing, animate what the character is thinking". On R6: write the circumstance beside the action in the clip plan ([clip-plan.md](../templates/clip-plan.md)) and let it change the timing, the head target and the hold, not the path. For the same give or drop clip (our numbers): the casual version keeps the `Head` on the walk direction and releases within 18 to 24 frames. The loaded version turns the `Head` to the object 12 to 20 frames before the arm moves, moves the arm at about half the speed, and holds the hand at the release point 20 to 30 frames as a moving hold. It pitches the `Torso` forward 5 to 8 degrees (negative `lift`, bent at the waist with the `waist` helper in ExampleClips.lua) and lifts the head a few degrees only after the release.

- **One attitude per thought; the speed of the change shows the kind of thought.** The Illusion of Life (Thomas and Johnston, "Animating Expressions and Dialogue"): "For each single thought, there is one key expression"; it may change in intensity but keeps its feeling. It changes only when the character gets a new thought or a realization, "with the timing of the change reflecting" what he is thinking. "If the thought process is a sudden realization," the change is quick. Schemers who took time to show their evil thoughts (the stepmother in Cinderella, Prince John, Captain Hook) changed more slowly, and a deliberate change, such as to a smile or a puzzled look, "is likely to remain there longer". In pantomime, the only key to the timing of these changes is the character's personality. On R6: with no face rig, the body attitude carries the key expression. Give each thought one attitude (head `lift` and `side`, torso `lift` and `side`, the arm shape) and only scale it inside the thought, as anime-craft.md, "Acting with restraint", sets the share per beat. Switch to a new attitude only on a new thought, head first (keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting"). Our timing: a sudden realization reaches the new attitude in 4 to 8 frames; a deliberate or scheming change takes 20 to 40 frames and holds at least as long again as a moving hold.

- **Think faster than life: skip the dull progressions.** Thomas and Johnston quote director Ham Luske: when somebody "opens a door or sits in a chair, we want to do it as simply and professionally as possible." "Our actors must be more interesting and more unusual than you and I. Their thought process must be quicker than ours, their uninteresting progressions from one situation to another must be skipped." On R6: in an emote or a cutscene, cut the moves between ideas that carry no thought: a re-positioning step, a turn to square up to the next prop, a fidget that belongs to neither thought. Do a mechanical action (sitting, opening a door, picking up a prop) on its simplest path, with one anticipation, one move and one settle. The time a change of thought takes stays set by the thought (above). The phrases still overlap (keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing"); what goes is the dead travel, not the overlap.

- **Lead the voice with the body by three to four film frames.** Thomas and Johnston list rules for dialogue "based on, and refined from" the principles Ham Luske developed. The first: "Anticipate dialogue with head, body" three to four frames "ahead of the heavy modulation, but mouth sync should be right on the modulation." For an extreme close-up synced with the eyes only, the list starts the eyes "at least three frames ahead of the accent"; a "mushy, soft line" needs a lead of only a frame or two, and strong accents four to five frames: "By 'lead,' we mean to have the big move that far ahead of the heavy modulation of the word." Ollie Johnston's notes: "Lift the body attitude 4 frames before dialogue modulation." On R6: find the voice peaks from `Sound.PlaybackLoudness` sampled at 60 Hz (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"), and key the head and torso accent extreme 8 to 10 frames before each heavy modulation (our conversion). The head stands in for the eyes, so it takes the eyes-only leads: 3 to 5 frames on a soft line and 10 to 12 on a strong accent (our conversion). A face-decal mouth swap, where the game has one, sits on the peak itself. keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", leads an attitude change with the head by 7 to 8 frames; use the leads here when the body accents a voiced line. pipeline.md places poses on the loudness peaks; that stays right for finding the beat and for the mouth, and the body's extreme leads it.

## Subtext and intention

- **Play what the character means and wants, not what it says.** Shawn Kelly (Animation Tips and Tricks, Volume II, "What Does an Animator Have to Learn about Traditional Acting?"): "it's often better to animate what the character MEANS rather than what they are SAYING". He asks: "What does he WANT most in the scene, and what is preventing him from having it?" Richard Williams (The Animator's Survival Kit, "Acting") adds: "What does he/she/it want? And even more interesting — why does the character want?" Notes from Ed Hooks's class (Thinking Animation): "Strong scenes come from need, not just want" and "Don't animate 'results' first—build intention, then let emotion show up as the result." On R6: write the want, the obstacle and the subtext of every emote or NPC moment in the clip plan before posing, and let the subtext pick the shape. A wave whose subtext is "please notice me" (our example) starts small, grows on its second swing and leans the `Torso` 0.1 to 0.2 studs toward the target (`p.z` toward it, with `Feet.flat` keeping the soles). A wave meaning "go away" flicks out once, and the torso turns away before the arm comes down. keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", already turns the interior monologue into thought changes.

- **One operative beat per line carries the biggest gesture.** Kelly (Volume I, Tip #3, "Operative Words"): the operative word is the word "most enunciated or most emphasized in the delivery"; "Sometimes it's simply the loudest word, but in some cases it may even be the quietest word." Ideally there is one per scene, sometimes two, three in a really long delivery; "the most common source of overacting" is choosing too many. "Your biggest gesture (or only gesture) should almost always happen during the operative word. Your biggest change in body posture should almost always happen during the operative word." A line with no operative word gets one chosen by the animator. On R6: for a voiced NPC line, a voice emote or a taunt, pick one accent per line by its meaning, not only from the loudness curve. Put the clip's largest arm arc and its largest torso change there, led by 8 to 10 frames as in "Lead the voice with the body" above, and keep every other gesture in the line under about half that size (our number). keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing", already shapes the beats into one crescendo.

## Gestures and clichés

- **Do not mime the words.** Kelly (Volume I, "Operative Words") acts out the overacted version of "I love you": "pointing to himself on 'I', clutching his heart on 'love' and pointing to his girl on 'you.'" "It's completely ridiculous. No one would really communicate like this in the real world. The only way that acting performance would work is if the characters are deaf and are using sign-language." Ollie Johnston: "Don't illustrate words." On R6: an R6 arm has one shape per pose, so a gesture per word reads as semaphore. Give a line one illustrative gesture at most, on its operative beat; carry the rest in the torso attitude and the head, which change once per thought (above).

- **Replace a stock gesture with this character's version, or do the stock gesture well.** AnimSchool, "Acting choices: Is it a cliche?" (2017): clichés are choices seen repeatedly in similar shots, or the "go to" choices such as "snapping fingers and smiling to show that the character has an idea". The spin that replaces them can come from the character's personality, its situation, or "the design or limitations of their body". Start from personality and subtext: an extrovert who gets an idea may act broadly, "jumping up and down and using the space available to him", where an introvert shows it "by having his eyes widen". Study a critically acclaimed live-action film for the choices the actors could have made instead. "If cliché has to be used, then do it well", and pair it with elements we have not seen: "If every girl character starts tucking her hair behind her ear every time she gets confused, we are going to get tired of it." Jespone's guide on the DevForum (2019; 656 likes on its first post) offers the stock version as a secondary action for a thinking character: "add a hand touching it's chin". On R6: list the genre's stock gestures before posing (the fist to the chin, the facepalm, the hand behind the head for embarrassment, the point at the camera; our examples) and replace them through personality and through the rig's own limits. An extrovert's idea (our example): drive the torso down, throw both arms up and out 30 to 45 degrees from the sides on the push, hop off both feet and land into a compact pose (body-mechanics.md, "Takeoff and landing"; time the air with "Gravity, jumps and falls on Roblox"). An introvert's idea, where the source widens the eyes (our example): the arms stay still, the `Head` alone lifts 3 to 5 degrees in 4 to 6 frames with a small overshoot, the torso straightens 2 to 3 degrees two or three frames later, and a moving hold follows. When the stock gesture stays, bend its timing or size to this character (anime-craft.md, "Bend textbook gestures with the feeling").

- **Twin the arms on purpose for authority.** Williams (The Animator's Survival Kit, "Symmetry or 'Twinning'"): symmetry "has gotten a bad press because of bad animation acting." Politicians, preachers and leaders "laying down the law" twin their arms and hands; he calls symmetry "an expression of harmony, beauty, balance, order and authority". People break it to point and return to it to express wholeness. One way "to take the curse off twinning is just to delay one of the hands or arms" (the scanned text garbles the frame count), or to tilt another part. On R6: this conflicts with SKILL.md, "Solid drawing", and keyframe-workflow.md, "Posing" ("Break a twinned pose through a chain"), which forbid twinned limbs. Keep those as the default for every clip. For a commanding pose (a leader rallying a crowd, a boss announcing a rule, a referee), twin both arms on the key and delay one arm's keys by 2 to 4 frames (our value). Shift the keys by hand rather than adding `lag`, which SKILL.md, "Offset the chain", keeps off a part that acts on purpose. Tilt another part as Williams suggests: roll the torso 2 to 4 degrees so the outline is not a mirror. Break the twin when the character points.

## Character, status and mood in the body

- **Give each emotion its movement qualities.** Shafir, Tsachor and Welch (2016, Frontiers in Psychology): six senior Laban Movement Analysis experts listed the motor elements common to validated video clips of whole-body anger, fear, happiness and sadness. Eighty people then moved 1241 motifs built from 29 elements and rated the emotion that moving each one evoked; the study measured what the movers felt, not what viewers read. Anger was predicted by Strong, Sudden and Direct effort and an Advancing shape. Fear was predicted by Retreating, Condensing and Enclosing shapes, Bound flow and moving Back. Happiness was predicted by Jumping, rhythmic re-initiating, a Spreading shape, Free and Light effort, moving Up and a Rising shape. Sadness was predicted by Passive weight, the arms brought to the upper body, a Sinking shape and the head down. On R6 (our mapping): anger moves the arms in short direct moves of 4 to 6 frames aimed at the target, each ending on an `e = "flat"` stop into a moving hold. The `Torso` advances 0.2 to 0.3 studs toward the target, with a step when the COM would leave the support (SKILL.md, "Measure before you look"). Fear binds the body: small moves, the torso backing onto the rear foot and lowered with the legs sliding up into it, the arms drawn in and across the front of the chest, and the feet closer together (`Feet.flat` targets 0.2 studs inward). From `rear34` the torso hides arms held in front of it, so the hunched, backing outline carries the read. In a walk, use the fear walk of locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk" (arms back and in). Happiness spreads the arms 25 to 45 degrees from the sides, lifts the head 5 to 10 degrees and repeats small hops or bounces on a steady rhythm. Sadness lets the weight go: the `Torso` sunk 0.1 to 0.2 studs with the legs sliding up into it, the head down about 19 degrees as in locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk", and one or both fists drawn slowly toward the chest. An arm left hanging can take `springs = {["Left Arm"] = "heavy"}`; r6-craft.md, "A hunch pitches the torso, sinks the head and drops the arms", has the posture.

- **Choose where the character leads from.** Notes from Ed Hooks's 1999 class (Thinking Animation): "Shifting the body's 'engine' changes the whole character." The notes list power centres: "Hips: swagger", "Chin: regal / official", "Chest: heroic/combative", "Forehead: intellectual", "Belly: heavy / grounded", "Knees: street strut"; and "Low status: looking down, face-touching, tension". On R6 (our mapping), lead every step and turn from one part. A turn still starts at the head (SKILL.md, "Turn the head; do not slide it"); the power centre sets what follows it. Chest: the `Torso` 4 to 8 degrees back (positive `lift`, bent at the waist) and pushed toward the travel, with the head's lead at the low end of SKILL.md's 10 to 20 degrees, so the chest seems to arrive first. Chin: the `Head` lifted 5 to 10 degrees and leading turns by 4 to 6 frames. Forehead: the `Head` pitched 3 to 6 degrees forward and reaching first. Hips: the torso translation (`p`) leading, with `lag` of 2 to 3 frames (`2/60` to `3/60`) on the `Head` and the arms, and a wider torso roll for the swagger. Belly: the torso sunk 0.05 to 0.1 studs and leaning back 3 to 5 degrees, with slow weight shifts. Knees: R6 has no knee, so the strut leads with the swinging leg's lift (r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons") and the torso follows 2 to 3 frames later. keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", already reads status from stillness, a level head and a dropped, fidgeting one. Hooks adds face-touching and tension for low status: smaller moves, the arms close, and a fist brought up to the side of the head for 10 to 20 frames (the anxious mannerism of anime-craft.md, "Personality in movement").

- **Victory spreads and lifts the body; defeat slumps the shoulders, not the head.** Tracy and Matsumoto (2008, PNAS) coded the spontaneous responses of judo athletes from 37 nations to winning or losing a match at the 2004 Olympic and Paralympic Games. Sighted, blind and congenitally blind winners showed the pride display: head tilted back, arms out from the body, arms raised, hands in fists, chest expanded, torso pushed out (sighted athletes, win against loss: arms out d = 1.12, arms raised d = 1.03, chest expanded d = 1.09). Sighted losers slumped the shoulders forward (d = 0.82) and narrowed the chest (d = 0.62), but losing did not predict a head tilted down or a hidden face; blind athletes also slumped and narrowed after a loss. On R6 (our numbers): a victory pose raises both arms over their own shoulders (`lift` above 90 degrees in the up form, r6-mechanics.md, "Direction and rotation checks"; within SKILL.md, "Overhead reach") and out 20 to 40 degrees. The `Torso` leans 8 to 12 degrees back and the `Head` lifts 10 to 20 degrees. Break the twin: one arm 5 to 10 degrees higher or 2 to 4 frames later. A defeat pose pitches the `Torso` 10 to 15 degrees forward from the waist and slides both arms 0.1 to 0.15 studs forward and slightly down, turned a few degrees in, so the shoulders read as rolled forward and the chest as narrow. Each arm keeps its torso contact (r6-mechanics.md, "Joint gaps"). Keep the head near the torso's line; a dropped head is a further choice, not the default.

- **An unsure character stands with its feet together, a little off balance, angled away.** David Gibson's GDC 2016 talk on Overwatch's Mei (Eva Sokolova's notes): Tracer and Widowmaker stand in an A-pose, feet apart, head up; Mei, younger, less confident and shy, was meant to stand with "feet together, slightly off-balance, body angled a little away from camera and weapon". 80.lv's notes on the talk add that the superhero pose with shoulders back and chest out did not work for her, that Gibson tried many idle poses and "wanted the character to feel a little off balance", and that the team liked the result. On R6 (our numbers): bring both `Feet.flat` targets 0.2 to 0.4 studs closer than the default stance, turn one toe in (leg twist against the torso under 45 degrees, SKILL.md, "Toe yaw"), and shift the weight so the COM sits 0.15 to 0.2 studs off the stance centre. For a character seen from the front (an NPC, a lobby or menu pose), yaw the torso 10 to 20 degrees away from the camera and the weapon. This keeps SKILL.md's rule that a held COM stays over the support ("Measure before you look" allows 0.26 studs forward or back and 0.33 sideways): "off balance" here is a look near the edge of the support, never a pose that would fall. anime-craft.md, "Temper each gesture to the person", sets a shy character's range of movement; this sets its stance.

- **A confident idle pushes the torso and holds the head on the goal.** Jespone's guide to animations (DevForum, 2019): for a brave, confident character, "make an idle of the character where the character's torso stands out, and make the head look straight to the objective." On R6: chest up 3 to 6 degrees (positive `Torso` `lift`, bent at the waist), arms 10 to 15 degrees out from the sides with a visible gap, and a level head aimed at the objective. Lower the head's noise so the look holds: `life = {Torso = 1, Head = 0.6, ["Left Arm"] = 1.2, ["Right Arm"] = 1.2}` (our values) instead of the 1.4 that `life = 1` gives the head.

- **Build from a real person and find the hook.** Jonathan Cooper's notes from an Andreas Deja masterclass (Game Anim, 2013): Deja drew King Triton chastising his daughter from memories of his own father pointing and yelling when his sister came home too late from a date. Lesson one was to use life experience in acting choices, "trying to always 'find a hook for your character'". Glen Keane (Phil on Film) based much of Rapunzel on his daughter Claire. On R6: name in the clip plan the real person whose behaviour you borrow and the one gesture that is the hook (our example: a parent's double jab of the pointing arm, the `Torso` leaning in 0.1 studs on each jab). Reuse that hook across the character's idles, emotes and reactions, as anime-craft.md, "Personality in movement", does with signature mannerisms.

- **Next to a loud character, keep the schemer still so the audience sees it think.** Cooper's Deja notes: facing the Genie's "bombastic on-screen presence" set by Eric Goldberg, Deja made Jafar subtle "to provide a contrast – allowing the audience to 'see him think'". On R6: when two characters share a moment (a boss and its minion, a user and its stand), give the quiet one the slow head turn of principles.md, "Timing and spacing in frames" (20 to 28 frames), into long moving holds, with the arms held close and moving only 5 to 10 degrees (our numbers). In between, keep only the breath and `life`, as principles.md, "Emote or acting", keeps a commanding character quiet. Each head turn then reads as a thought. keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", already gives one character the primary action per beat.

- **A heavy prop sets the character's range.** 80.lv on Gibson's talk: heavier, bigger characters "seem tired and strained, holding their huge guns", and because a tube was connected to Mei's gun, "she couldn't possible do a lot of crazy movements like Tracer". Riot's animator Rory "RiotLamz" Alderton (2014 dev blog): with Braum roaming one-handed, his shield "quickly lost any sense of weight and power", so the team changed his grip and posture to two hands as he moved. On R6: with a heavy or tethered prop, keep the gestures smaller and later than the unarmed character's (our reading). Let the prop's weight show in the counter-lean and the lower torso of locomotion.md, "Idles and breathing" (weight from equipment), and carry a big prop with both hands (weapons.md, "Weight: a heavy blade, two hands, a step every swing"), inside the grip limits of weapons.md.

## The head as the eyes

- **A head turn seen from behind steers the viewer's gaze.** Schmitz, Miksch and Einhäuser (2026, Scientific Reports): 16 participants, told only to walk behind an avatar through virtual corridors, looked more to the side of the posters the avatar looked at, although its looking had no relevance to them. The effect held for a robot avatar that "glided in front of the participant without any limb articulation, but expressed only head rotation". On R6: the player camera sits behind the character, and a head turn alone can steer attention. For a companion or guide walking ahead of the player (our numbers), yaw the `Head` 30 to 45 degrees toward the point of interest, lead with it as in SKILL.md, "Turn the head; do not slide it", and hold the look 1 to 2 s before returning. Let the torso follow 5 to 10 degrees a few frames later, so the head does not read as a swivel on a still body. The study's robot turned only its head, so the head carries the cue when the torso is busy.

- **Hold the head on another character only for conflict or intimacy.** Notes from Ed Hooks's class (Thinking Animation): "Eye contact is selective; intense eye contact is usually intimacy, conflict, or threat". On R6: the head's aim is the eye contact. An NPC that tracks the player without a break reads as a threat or a stare. For a friendly NPC, look at the player for 1 to 2 s, then away to its task or the scene for 2 to 4 s (our numbers), using the quick dart and hold of principles.md, "Timing and spacing in frames". Keep the unbroken stare for a rival, a guard or a boss.

## Arms and hands

- **Aim the hand: the whole body relates to it, out in the clear.** Thomas and Johnston, "The Principles of Animation": Walt Disney called his remedy for gags the audience missed "aiming". "If Oswald the Lucky Rabbit is to put his hand in his pocket to get a sandwich for lunch, the whole body must relate to that hand and to the pocket. When the hand is aimed, it must be 'out in the clear' so everyone can see it and anticipate what is going to happen." "The head cannot be looking off somewhere else". Deja (Cooper's notes): "The face and hands are the most important elements of acting." On R6: with no face rig and no fingers, the arm is the main acting tool. keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", already turns the head to the target first, and SKILL.md's Staging row moves the hands to the side the camera sees. Add the rest of the body: before a hand acts (a reach, a give, a point, a draw), turn and lean the `Torso` 5 to 10 degrees toward the target (our numbers), and keep the head on the hand's goal until the action lands. Check it with `_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "rear34"})`: an aimed arm in front of the chest is hidden from the player camera.

- **An arm gesture across the front of the body may not read from the player camera.** "How are my animations?" (DevForum, 2024): an idle on a custom nine-part rig was made "with a more shy character in mind. the right arm is grabbing onto the left arm". A reviewer answered "That looks like more confidence than shyness to me", and the author replied "I would think that you would think otherwise if you saw it from the front". Later in the thread, asking for the rig's leg gaps to be fixed, the same reviewer wrote that "players would probably see the players behind more". Ollie Johnston's notes allow the fix: "Actions can be eliminated and staging 'cheated' if it simplifies the picture you are trying to show and is not disturbing to the audience." On R6: arms crossed or clasped in front of the torso are hidden behind it from the player camera, so the read falls to the head and torso, and an arm gesture may be cheated out to the side the camera sees. For a shy idle seen from behind, use the shy range of anime-craft.md, "Temper each gesture to the person" (head down 5 to 10 degrees, arms 5 to 15 degrees out with a gap), and add the `Torso` 4 to 6 degrees forward and the feet closer together (our numbers). Judge the posture with the face decal on (r6-craft.md, "Animate on a head with a face"), then check the read in the `rear34` strip with the face decal removed (SKILL.md, "Appeal" row).

## Rhythm, pauses and stillness

- **Grief holds a strong attitude and moves only a sagging head.** Thomas and Johnston, "Acting and Emotions", on the dwarfs mourning Snow White. The animator said: "It just felt like they should all move as little as possible"; "They'd have strong body attitudes that could be held for the most part, and maybe a sagging move on the head here and there, just enough to keep it alive. Even a sniff seemed too much action for the mood." His problem was "not breaking the spell", and "we didn't want any jitters, but we didn't want any short, jerky moves either". Grumpy, "true to his personality", turns away and cries alone. On R6 (our numbers): hold the attitude, with the `Torso` forward 10 to 15 degrees, the `Head` down 20 to 30 degrees and the arms hanging close on `heavy` springs. Keep `life` at the low end of SKILL.md's 0.6 to 1.5 degrees, a breath at 4 to 5 s, and one head sag of 2 to 4 degrees over 40 to 60 frames every 2 to 3 s. No move in the beat is shorter than 20 frames. A character whose personality hides its grief turns its back to the camera (`Torso` `twist` 120 to 180 degrees through planted-foot steps) to grieve alone.

- **Hit music accents about three film frames early.** Thomas and Johnston, "Animating Expressions and Dialogue": "When the whole action was exposed three frames ahead of the actual beat, the character appeared to be moving exactly on the beat!" "For a jump, a land, a hit, a take, three frames nearly always gave a better illusion than having the extreme drawing right on the beat." But in "a tempo of eights (a beat coming every eight frames)" a three-frame shift hit the half-beat and lost the sync. The book finds no fixed rule for sync on dialogue or music: one two-word line in Pinocchio had to be shifted a full eight frames ahead of the sound, and the animators keep a shift that works and experiment when it does not. On R6: in a dance or music emote, key each accent extreme 7 to 8 frames before the beat found on the loudness curve (our conversion). At a beat every 20 frames or less at 60 fps (180 beats per minute or faster), shift less, 2 to 4 frames (our choice), and check every shift on the character in Play with the sound. r6-craft.md, "Locomotion, idle and emote sets", places emote beats on the music's loudness peaks: use the peaks to find the beat, and let the visible accent lead it.

## Physical comedy

- **A take: a hold while the message travels, a squash, an overshoot, then the held reaction.** Harold Whitaker and John Halas (Timing for Animation, "Character Reactions and 'Takes'"): between seeing something and the message reaching the brain "This would be at least five frames, but could be a lot longer depending on the character's mental ability." "The legs, arms, hands, the position of the body: all must contribute to a reaction." The character holds, goes down to an anticipation or squash, rises past its final position ("This 'overshoot' ... is the drawing which gives the feeling of surprise"), and settles into a hold, "the drawing which the audience sees"; one example holds it "for perhaps 12 frames". "A big brainless character requires a longer time to react than a small excitable character." On R6 (our numbers after the conversion): hold at least 12 frames as a moving hold. Squash over 4 to 6 frames: `Torso` `p.y` down 0.15 to 0.25 studs with the legs sliding up under `Feet.flat`, head down 10 degrees, arms in. Overshoot over 4 to 6 frames: torso back 8 to 12 degrees, arms out and up, head back, or a hop of 0.15 to 0.25 studs as in SKILL.md, "No toes". Then settle into the reaction pose, the pose that carries the whole reaction, and hold it 30 frames or more. A slow-witted brute waits 25 to 40 frames before the squash; an excitable sidekick, the minimum 12.

- **Hold a gag pose long enough to read.** Whitaker and Halas give holds for gags: a static gag pose held "for 24 frames before a pan"; a "freeze" at the end of a fast throw, "held for about 12 frames"; a man who stands up quickly held for 8 frames; a look off-screen that needs "a hold of about 12 frames" before cutting to what the character sees. "It does not matter how off-balance or awkward an extreme looks, provided it is reached properly and is moved out of fairly quickly." On R6 (our conversion): a held gag pose about 60 frames, a freeze after a fast throw about 30, a quick stand about 20, and a look that must read before the game shows what is seen about 30, each as a moving hold (SKILL.md, "Moving holds"). An awkward extreme may pass through a few frames off the support, but a held one keeps the COM over the feet (SKILL.md, "Solid drawing").

- **The follow-through is the punch line.** Thomas and Johnston, "The Principles of Animation": "The way in which an action is completed often tells us more about the person than the drawings of the movement itself. A golfer takes a mighty swing, which covers only a few frames, but what happens to him afterward can easily take five feet of film and is much more revealing, whether he is graceful and slick in his follow through, or wraps himself up in a knot." The anticipation sets up the action, "the action whizzes past, and now we come to the 'punch line' of the gag, the follow through". The book counts 16 frames to a foot of film, so five feet is 80 frames, 3.3 s at 24 fps (our conversion). On R6: in an emote, a cutscene or a comic failure, keep the action itself fast (6 to 10 frames) and spend 1.5 to 3 s on the ending (our numbers). A graceful character spirals the `Torso` `twist` past the swing and settles with keyed overshoots that shrink (SKILL.md, "Follow-through and through motion"), with springs on the arms. A clumsy one overshoots the twist and takes a catch step through `Feet.flat` targets. A player move that the game must chain or cancel keeps the short recovery of SKILL.md, "Recoveries".

## Silhouette, distance and the player camera

- **Tell it in pantomime, as if there were no sound.** Williams (The Animator's Survival Kit, "Body Language"): his Oscar-winning A Christmas Carol was run for an audience in Iran who did not understand a word of it, and "A Chuck Jones cartoon came on after and blew us out of the water. So for us, we should keep words to a bare minimum and make everything as clear as we can through pantomime. We should feel we have only the body to tell the story." He calls studying silent movies a great idea: much of their acting is hammy and corny, but "it's all very clear". On R6: anime-craft.md, "Act with the body; the face may not be seen", already checks every emotion from `rear34` with the face decal removed and asks a fresh reviewer (keyframe-workflow.md, "Review with fresh eyes"). Run that review muted as well, and ask what the character feels and wants. If the answer needs the sound or the face, push the torso attitude and the arm shapes.

- **Poses crammed into a small space vanish.** RiotLamz (Riot Games dev blog, 2014): an early version of Yasuo's ultimate, slicing his opponent upward for the full one-second cast (30 animation frames), "wasn't nearly impactful enough during playtests as he'd end up obscured behind the ability's visual effects (VFX), and his poses were all contained in too small a space. You could barely see him." The approved version leaps into a three-hit combo built on three airborne key poses, "the three moments of the animation that end up being seen for the longest amount of time". On R6: give an emote, a victory pose or a finisher two or three key poses that reach well outside the torso's outline (arms 45 degrees or more from the body, a torso lean, a step or a hop; our numbers). Give those poses the longest holds, and place them where the game's effects do not cover the body. combat.md, "Readability from a pulled-back camera", sets the same for strikes.

## Reference without copying

- **Copy live-action reference verbatim and it looks robotic or floats; push it.** Kelly (Volume I, "Reference Materials"): "Do NOT just copy it. Copying reference verbatim generally results in a robotic lifeless feel. Your job as an animator is to take that reference and apply your ART to it." Williams (The Animator's Survival Kit): a traced live-action walk "floats. Nobody really knows why. So we increase the ups and the downs". Cooper's Deja notes: on Beauty and the Beast, other animators stuck to the rotoscope-like actor reference, while Deja picked what to use, adding and leaving out; Cooper's teams follow that lesson, "whereby animators must observe and then 'plus' reality." AnimSchool's reference analysis (Scott McWhinnie on Jeff Gabor, 2025): "Rather than simply copying the reference, push it further". On R6: this conflicts with SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference", step 4 ("Copy the pick as authored"), and with anime-craft.md, "Perform the reference bigger than life", which films a bigger take and then copies it as authored. Each applies to a different reference. Copy as authored when the reference is animation (a professional clip, a source game's frames, a pick the requester wants matched; video-rotoscope.md for an exact copy) or a take you directed bigger than life. Push when the reference is ordinary live-action footage that you cannot reshoot: keep its timing, pick its key poses, push each 10 to 20% (SKILL.md, "Exaggeration" row), and make the torso's vertical motion larger than traced (our starting value: 1.3 times). Then check it against the R6 ranges in locomotion.md, "Walk body motion".

- **Shoot reference like a take: the game's framing, real props, one critiqued take at a time.** AnimSchool (2025): Gabor frames his reference to match the intended shot ("If it's a close-up in the animation, he shoots a close-up"), keeps the shooting space clear of clutter, wears clothing that shows body movement, and uses real props. He "records one take at a time, reviews it, critiques it, and then makes purposeful adjustments before shooting again", with takes that restrict head movement or emphasize a different physical choice. Kelly (Volume II, "Making the Best Acting Decisions"): act it out in front of a camera "over and over, until you stop thinking about what the character is saying"; in "What Does an Animator Have to Learn about Traditional Acting?" he adds that when he does not become the character, "my reference always ends up being useless and predictable." On R6 (our choices): film from behind and above at about the player camera's angle, plus one side view for timing. Shoot one take with the elbows and knees kept straight, R6's limits, so a beat that needs a bend shows up early. Shoot one with the head held still, as Gabor does, to see how much the body carries without it. Review each take before the next, and keep the one where the thought is visible in the head and torso.

- **Gather references for the feeling, from many places.** Gibson's GDC 2016 talk, in Sokolova's notes: references "don't have to be exact", and can be "direct looks to movement style, posture, personality traits"; Mei's came from Zooey Deschanel, Rapunzel in Tangled and Giselle in Enchanted, and from asking everyone on the team. 80.lv's notes: he looked for "things that have the same feeling", and "don't copy anything. Reference helps to plan, it's a guide. It's not for copying." On R6: when designing a new character, collect 5 to 10 clips from film, anime, games and people you know, and write one property taken from each into the clip plan: stance width, head angle, tempo, a mannerism (our method). This shapes the character before any clip exists; once a clip has a picked reference, SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference", step 4 applies.

## Performance in games

- **An idle that moves too little reads as a still image.** "Idle Animation feedback!" (DevForum, 2025), a shopkeeper idle: one reviewer "thought it was a still image for the first few seconds" and asked for a bob that is "more frequent and a bit more exaggarated". Another suggested "rotating the head down as the character it exhaling and rotate back up while inhaling", and on the revised version asked for movement that "continues to happen" instead of moving, pausing and moving again. On R6: for an NPC that players walk past, use the short end of the calm breath and the top of its lean (principles.md, "Idle or held stance": 3 to 5 s, lean 2 to 4 degrees). Let the `Head` pitch down on the exhale and up on the inhale, a fifth of a cycle behind the torso at half its size, as that recipe describes. Leave no pause in the loop (`Poser.check` frozen 0%), and check it from the player camera at the distance where players meet the NPC.

- **Let a personality idle wait before it plays.** "AFK Animation System, need feedback!" (DevForum, 2024): a system played a random idle after 3 s without input; a reviewer said "I would wait a bit longer for the animations to kick in", and the author agreed. On R6: play an AFK or personality variant after 10 to 20 s without input (our choice), never during combat, and leave it at once on input with the blend sized from the pose distance (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). The main idle keeps its weighted rare variants (r6-craft.md, "Locomotion, idle and emote sets").

- **A victory pose must show this character, not a generic pose.** PC Gamer (2016): Blizzard said it would replace Tracer's "over-the-shoulder" victory pose after a player wrote that "there is no facet of Tracer's silly/spunky/kind personality in the pose", while D.va's pose "at least still preserves some of her existing characterization". Director Jeff Kaplan: "We'll replace the pose. We want *everyone* to feel strong and heroic in our community." On R6: build each victory pose, taunt and emote from the character's own signature gestures (anime-craft.md, "Personality in movement") and its power centre (above), on top of the victory display (above). Test it blind: show the `rear34` capture with names hidden and ask which character it is.

- **A walk's or run's character lives in two keys.** Gibson's talk, in Sokolova's notes, on his process for a run or walk cycle: "if you get the full extension keys and passing pose to really match the character, the rest of the animation is pretty much just adjustments." 80.lv: Mei "moved soft, floating in space". On R6: put a walk's personality into its contact (full extension) and passing poses (the stride, the torso lean and roll, the head angle and the arm swing), and build the rest as breakdowns. Keep the walk rules of SKILL.md ("Walks need double support", "Feet rule"). A soft, unsure walk (our example) takes a shorter stride, a small torso bob and the arms swinging more forward than back, with `lag = {Head = 3/60}` so the head floats on the step.

## Notes veterans give juniors

- **Body mechanics come before acting.** Kelly (Volume II, "How Important Is an Acting Shot for Getting a Job in the Gaming Industry?"): "The best acting shots can be destroyed by very small mistakes in body mechanics", and "a decent acting scene with bad body mechanics is a demo-reel killer"; a shot with great mechanics and only "OK" acting "certainly doesn't ruin the reel". On R6: pass the COM, `_G.feet` and `Poser.check` gates of SKILL.md, "Measure before you look", before judging an acting clip; a gesture on a sliding foot reads as a mistake, not a choice.

- **Moving nicely is not enough: pick the idea and protect it.** Williams (The Animator's Survival Kit, "Acting") quotes Frank Thomas in 1972 on an assistant who knew "everything you could teach about movement", weight, depth and balance, but had "a very poor choice of what to do in animation — so his animation was always flat. It always moved nicely, but nobody wanted to look at it." Milt Kahl: "It's a matter of picking the right thing to do and making up your mind about that. And then not letting any other ideas interfere with it." Williams: "Do one thing at a time and be crystal clear." On R6: a clip that passes every check can still be dull. Name the clip's one idea in the clip plan, cut any gesture that does not sell it (Ollie Johnston: "Does the added action in a scene contribute to the main idea in that scene?"), and record the fresh reviewer's answer as the acting check (keyframe-workflow.md, "Review with fresh eyes").

- **Juniors copy their idols and lack contrast.** Cooper's Deja notes: young animators tend "to try to replicate your idols. Instead, put 'yourself' into it via self expression – apply your own sense of humour." "Young animators also have a hard time creating contrast – spend time with your key poses, but make bold choices on inbetweens and get between them faster." He also urged them to "plus" things and to look for chances to insert comedy. On R6: spend the time on the key poses and move between them faster than feels safe; a gesture that takes 15 to 20 frames usually reads stronger in 8 to 12 with a held pose either side (our numbers), which `warp` can test without rekeying (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract").

## Does not transfer to R6

- Mouth sync on the voice's modulation and mouth shapes that reflect the voice (Ham Luske's rules): the default R6 head has no mouth rig; a face-decal swap needs new runtime code.
- Eyes that flicker around while listening on the phone, reflecting the listener's shifting thoughts (Williams), and searching eye movements timed off the interior monologue (Kelly): no eyes to animate; the head turn stands in (The head as the eyes).
- A brow lowered into a frown, then one brow lifted with a glance, as a change of thought (Eric Larson, quoted by Williams): no brows; the change becomes a new head tilt and torso attitude.
- Sad eyes, slow blinks and tears in the dwarfs' grief: no face rig and no tears on the body; the held attitude carries it.
- The smile of the pride display and the hands in fists (Tracy and Matsumoto): no face rig and no fingers; the raised and spread arms carry the read.
- The hair tuck behind the ear and the finger snap (AnimSchool): no hair and no fingers; the whole arm acts instead, with care not to fall into the same clichés. A fist brought to the chin does transfer (r6-craft.md, "Pose the arm as the forearm when the elbow matters").
- Squashing and stretching the whole body for attitudes (Ollie Johnston): R6 parts cannot scale; a compact or a long pose replaces it (SKILL.md, "Squash and stretch").
- Using cutting and the camera to accentuate an emotion (The Illusion of Life, "Points to Remember When Animating Emotions"): the player controls the gameplay camera; this applies only in cutscenes.
- Mei's jiggling backpack and Yasuo's simulated hair and ropes: no such parts on a standard R6 body; like hair, they need a rigged accessory (anime-craft.md, "Does not transfer to R6").
