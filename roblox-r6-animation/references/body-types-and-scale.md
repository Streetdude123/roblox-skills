# Body types, weight and scale on six parts

How size, mass, age, strength, load and fatigue change motion, and how to give each R6 character its own timing and posture when every R6 body is the same six blocks. Every finding was checked against its source ([sources.md](sources.md), "Body types, weight and scale on six parts research"). S is a character's scale against the stock R6 rig (`Torso.Size.Y / 2`, r6-mechanics.md, "Scaled rigs"); a stock R6 has S = 1, a 2 stud leg and its soles 1 stud apart. Frame counts are at 60 fps. The square-root timing rule itself is in body-mechanics.md, "Gravity, jumps and falls on Roblox" ("Timing tells size"); this file holds the evidence behind it and what else changes with size and build.

## Size and timing: dynamic similarity

- **Equal Froude numbers make different sizes move alike.** R. McNeill Alexander (2004, Journal of Anatomy, "Bipedal animals, and their differences from humans"): where gravity matters, two systems can move in a dynamically similar way only at equal Froude numbers, speed squared over (length times gravity). Alexander (1976) and Alexander and Jayes (1983) proposed that similar animals of different sizes at equal Froude numbers tend to move alike, and two animals that move alike have equal relative stride lengths and equal duty factors. The length they used is the hip height. On R6: the hip height is the leg, 2 S studs. To build a character at scale S from a stock cycle, keep the stock Froude number: multiply the speed by sqrt(S), the stride per cycle by S and the cycle time by sqrt(S), and keep the duty. The walk of pipeline.md, "Custom r6-style rigs" (4.8 studs/s, a 0.84 s cycle, duty 0.59), becomes 9.6 studs/s on a 1.68 s cycle at S = 4, still at duty 0.59 (our calculation).
- **The walk-run switch moves with the square root of leg length.** Kram, Domingo and Ferris (1997, Journal of Experimental Biology): humans and other bipeds with different leg lengths switch from a walk to a run at different absolute speeds but at about the same Froude number, 0.5. Under simulated low gravity the switch came at slower speeds but at about the same Froude number. Usherwood et al. (2018, Journal of Biomechanics) place the familiar adult switch at a dimensionless speed `v / sqrt(g L)` of about 0.75 (0.56 as a Froude number, our square); their work-minimizing model predicts it, and walking data from 487 people, mostly 11 to 18 years old, supported the model. On R6: locomotion.md, "Speeds, cadence and the walk-run boundary", gives 5.9 studs/s for the stock leg at 35 studs/s^2. At scale S the switch is 5.9 sqrt(S) studs/s: about 4.6 at S = 0.6, 8.3 at S = 2 and 11.8 at S = 4 (our calculation). Give each character its walk or run clip from its own switch speed, never from the stock 5.9.
- **Froude scaling fixes the duty factor better than the stride.** Donelan and Kram (1997, Journal of Experimental Biology): at equal Froude numbers under simulated gravity from 0.25 g to 1.0 g, subjects walked with nearly the same duty factor, but their relative stride lengths differed by as much as 67%, so the authors rejected the dynamic similarity hypothesis. They note that a small child must run to keep up with an adult who walks at a comfortable pace. On R6: trust the scaling for the gait choice and the planted share (a scaled walk keeps 55 to 60%, SKILL.md, "Walks need double support"). Set the stride from the scaled leg's reach instead: a stance of about 2.4 S studs (SKILL.md, "Locomotion speed", scaled by S). When a child-sized character keeps pace with an adult, play its run while the adult walks.
- **Games and film slow big things by the square root of their scale.** Tore Knabe (blog post "How much to slow animation down for giant creatures"): pose and time the creature at human scale, scale it up, then divide the animation speed by the square root of the scale factor, because a creature x times as big needs sqrt(x) times as long for its centre of mass to fall x times as far. Without root motion, multiply its movement speed by sqrt(x). Film miniatures use the same law: 24 x sqrt(D/d) fps, so a 1/48 model is shot at about 166 fps and a 1/36 model at 144 fps (Cinematography Mailing List, Jim Sofranko and Geoff Boyle). On R6: play the stock-scale clip on the scaled rig with `rig:play(clip, {speed = 1 / math.sqrt(S)})` (0.71 at S = 2, 0.58 at S = 3, 0.5 at S = 4) and set its WalkSpeed to the clip's built speed times sqrt(S). Poser steps springs with the clip-time advance and samples `life` and `lag` in clip time (`Poser.lua`, `step`), so the same `speed` slows them by the same factor with no retuning. locomotion.md, "Frames per step", says a giant takes about one step per second; from a stock walk stepping every 0.42 s that is S of about 5.7 (our calculation). The speed factor is only the start: Tanaka found a stretched clip still looked light ("Giants and small bodies: mass, momentum and detail").
- **Viewers read size from stride frequency.** Jokisch and Troje (2003, Journal of Vision, abstract): point-light dogs with high stride frequencies were judged smaller than ones with low frequencies, and the judgements matched the predicted inverse quadratic relation between stride frequency and size. On R6: where every avatar is the same size, cadence alone can make one character read bigger. A step rate 0.85 times another character's reads about 1.4 times as big; 0.8 times reads about 1.6 times (our use of the inverse square). A rigid leg plants only about 2.4 studs per stance, so a slower cadence at the same stride means a slower game speed for that character: set its WalkSpeed from the clip.
- **Stride frequency falls with mass by a small power.** Heglund and Taylor (1988, Journal of Experimental Biology; 16 quadruped species from 30 g mice to 200 kg horses): the minimum, preferred and maximum sustained speeds within a trot and a gallop scale with about the 0.2 power of body mass, nine-fold apart from mice to horses. The stride frequencies at those equivalent speeds, except the minimum trotting speed, scale with about the -0.15 power of mass. On R6: at a fixed shape, mass grows with S^3, so M^-0.15 is S^-0.45, close to the 1/sqrt(S) of the speed rule above (our derivation). Use one rate factor, 1/sqrt(S), for the whole character.
- **Strength grows with area, weight with volume, so giants jump low.** J. B. S. Haldane (essay "On Being the Right Size"): a giant ten times as high, wide and thick as a man weighs a thousand times as much, but its bones have only a hundred times the cross-section, so each square inch of bone carries ten times the load. The height an animal can jump is "more nearly independent of its size than proportional to it" (a flea about two feet, a man about five). Masanobu Tanaka (GenDesign CTO, animator on Shadow of the Colossus and animation director on The Last Guardian, CEDEC 2021 talk "5 Rules for Animating Giant Characters"): at three times the height a body has 27 times the mass but 9 times the bone and muscle strength, so the bigger it is, the harder it is to hold up and move. A stretched human run on his giant model jumped too high for a giant. On R6: let a giant leap about the same height in studs as a stock R6, not S times higher: keep the Humanoid's JumpHeight (7.2 studs by default, body-mechanics.md, "Gravity, jumps and falls on Roblox") or lower it, and key a giant's hops at stock heights. A physics jump of that height keeps the stock air time (31 to 33 frames at the defaults), so time the giant's air poses to it and slow only the crouch before and the recovery after (our reading). A small character jumping the same studs reads as springy, which suits it.

## Building a character at another size

- **Scale every offset by S; keep the rotations.** DevForum (2019, "R6 walking animation doesn't scale down correctly"): on a scaled-down R6 NPC the walk put the limbs, especially the legs, farther from the body than at normal size; a reply said the scaled joints' C0 differ, so each scale needs its own animation. A 2024 thread on miniature R6 characters reports a walk whose right leg did not look attached, even with a walk authored on the miniature rig. In a 2021 bug report on scaled characters, "Animated movement (not rotation)" went off: a big boss was flung further than intended and sank into the ground, and a smaller character moved less. On R6: Poser writes `Motor6D.Transform` itself, so nothing rescales its keys (our reading of pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). Multiply every `p` in the clip, every `Feet.flat` target's x, z and lift, and every root travel curve by S; keep every `r`. An imported stock clip scales on load with `Poser.fromSequence(kfs, Poser.wrapsOf(rig), {pScale = S})`. `Feet.lua` has the stock leg geometry built in. `RigFeet.new(geom)` takes the hip, leg vector, sole and floor that r6-mechanics.md, "Scaled rigs", lists for a scaled rig, but it requires leg joints whose C0 and C1 carry no rotation, and the R6 hip C0 carries a 90 degree yaw, as a DevForum script that rebuilds the R6 joints sets it. Planting a scaled rig therefore needs `RigFeet.new` with that scaled geometry once the hip rotation is handled (rotate the geometry or the pose space), or a scale input in `Feet.lua`: new runtime code either way. The scaled `Feet.flat` uses below depend on it. Scale the limb-gap limit too (0.12 studs at S = 1, SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference"; our reading).
- **The Animator scales clips by the model's scale, with known faults.** Creator docs (`Model:GetScale`): a model's scale factor "will be applied to joint offsets of Animations played on an AnimationController under that model". For Humanoid characters, a developer replying in the 2021 thread (EmilyBendsSpace) wrote that the animation system assumes clips authored on a standard R15 with HipHeight 2.0 and scales their translations by HipHeight / 2.0, because a published animation stores no rig scale. In 2023 a developer fixed an animation on a scaled-down character with `Model:ScaleTo`, after `Workspace.Retargeting` at Enabled, Disabled and Default had changed nothing. A 2026 bug report says animations glitch on characters scaled above 1, and a reply says giant NPCs that once walked well now walk glitchy. A later reply gives `Workspace.Retargeting` set to Disabled as the current fix (the docs: retargeting "adapts animations to character rigs of different proportions"). On R6: the docs promise the scaling only under an AnimationController, so test it on a Humanoid character. Size the character with `Model:ScaleTo`, bake clips on a stock rig (`Poser.bake`), play the baked clip on the scaled character and compare the torso's offset at one key with S times its authored `p`. Test with `Workspace.Retargeting` at Default and at Disabled before shipping a giant.

## Giants and small bodies: mass, momentum and detail

- **Heavy is slow to change speed, not slow.** Tanaka: mass is "the degree to which a body keeps its current speed". A truck cannot start, stop or turn suddenly; a small insect can. When Trico brakes hard it keeps moving forward for a while; a small bird stops and starts at once. Mass does not set top speed (a 350 t jumbo jet flies at 890 km/h), so top speed must not differ by mass. (gameanim.com's English summary of the talk says a large mass can stop suddenly; the slides say it cannot, and this file follows the slides.) David Clayton (animation supervisor, Weta Digital, on Rampage): big creatures "can move quickly, but they need some time to generate that speed, and they need some time to wash off that speed". Two DevForum answers on a heavy, grounded brawler ask for momentum: a skid and a non-instant turning circle instead of instant direction changes, and a slow stop instead of Roblox's instant one. On R6: keep a heavy or giant character's top speed and lengthen its starts and stops. Tanaka's rule is about momentum: it keeps a heavy runner's top speed while lengthening starts, stops and turns. A realistic heavy or obese NPC still walks about 12% slower, and a stylised menacing boss walk runs at 0.5 to 0.7 of the brisk walk's speed (Heavy bodies). Reach full speed over 2 to 3 steps with the lean growing each step (our starting values). Stop over several short braking steps, not one (locomotion.md, "Starts, stops and turns"), the last a braced leg planted ahead with the torso leaning back and sinking (SKILL.md, "Push before speed, stopper before stop"). The root's own speed ramp belongs to the movement system: custom-rigs.md, "Momentum for a Humanoid creature", has the ramp and its scaling by weight; offer it as a question (SKILL.md, opening paragraph).
- **Smooth arcs read heavy, broken arcs read light.** Tanaka: a large mass makes a point's path smooth; a small mass lets it bend sharply. Sudden starts and stops express small things (small birds, insects, faces, fingers); smoothed paths express heavy weapons, trucks and giants. On R6: on a giant, print the hand, foot and head-top paths (SKILL.md, "Arcs and paths") and round every corner. Put no `flat` key inside a path, and spread every turn-around over at least 6 clip frames, which `speed = 1 / math.sqrt(S)` stretches to 6 x sqrt(S) real frames (our starting value). On a small or light character, allow corners: a `flat` key and a restart 2 to 3 frames later on a head turn, a hop or a dart reads quick and light.
- **A heavy part speeds up and slows down later.** Tanaka: living things move with optimal energy efficiency; the head is heavy, so it accelerates and decelerates more slowly than an arm, and each part speeds up and slows down to suit its mass. On R6: in a move made on purpose, ease each part by its share of the body (r6-mechanics.md, "Centre of mass and balance": torso 0.497, head 0.081, each arm 0.050). Into a held pose, give the torso the longest settle, the head the next and the arms the shortest, for example 12, 9 and 6 frames at S = 1 (our starting values; the `speed` rule above stretches them on a giant). Carried, hanging parts still trail their driver and settle after it (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain"); this rule is for parts that move by their own muscle. The head still starts a turn first (SKILL.md, "Turn the head; do not slide it"); on a giant it starts first and arrives late.
- **Gravity does not scale: keep the bob's acceleration.** Tanaka: gravity is the same for every object whatever its mass, so animation time cannot simply be stretched. A jog's airborne hips roughly follow the gravity parabola. A sprint with a big up-and-down motion makes gravity look stronger and the runner look like a miniature, so the vertical range must shrink at a sprint. Clayton: Weta puts a falling "gravity cube" with a calculated parabola in a shot to check that a jumping or falling character drops at the right rate; once George grows, "when he jumps and lands and moves through frame, it's going to be slower". On R6: on a scaled character, scale the torso bob by S and the cycle by sqrt(S), so the bob's acceleration (amplitude over period squared) stays the stock value; a bigger bob at the slower timing reads as strong gravity and a toy (our derivation). Give a sprint a smaller bob than a jog. Fit a giant's airborne COM to the game's gravity as r6-mechanics.md, "Centre of mass and balance" (flight), describes.
- **A giant lifts its heavy leg only briefly.** Tanaka: his giant run, even with its paths smoothed, still looked light because it moved its own heavy limbs easily. A leg is about 15% of body weight (6 t for a giant he estimates at 40 t), so lifting it takes the whole body's muscles, and the time the leg is lifted should be short. On R6: raise a giant walk's planted share to about 0.65 (our starting value); SKILL.md's 55 to 60% ("Walks need double support") holds for a human-sized walk. A shorter swing leaves less time to lift, so keep the swing foot's lift at about half the stock lift times S (our starting value). Roll the torso 2 to 3 degrees toward the stance leg as the swing foot leaves the floor, so the body is seen working to lift the leg (our reading).
- **A slowed clip loses detail: add contact detail back.** Tanaka: a slowed motion goes blurry like an enlarged low-resolution photo, so add detail back: the shake when a running foot hits the ground, the instant of the push in a jump, the shock travelling through the body. Where 1/30 or 1/60 s frames cannot show it, stretch the time a little and cheat. A shake must slow down as it dies out, not stay fast. A little stage trickery (keren-mi) helps but is physically a lie, so use it sparingly. On R6: on each giant foot contact, key a torso `p.y` dip of 0.04 to 0.08 S studs and two rebounds, each smaller and longer than the last, about 3, 5 and 8 frames in real time, with the arms and head 1 to 2 frames behind (our starting values). On heavy contacts (a running step, a stomp) this shake replaces the small sink of the next bullet; the two do not stack. In a clip played at `speed = 1 / math.sqrt(S)`, key those segments sqrt(S) times shorter in clip time, so the jolt stays crisp inside the slowed clip. Do not use a `warp` for it in a walk or run loop: a warp speeds the planted feet as well, and they slide against the root (SKILL.md, "Break the default bell", planted feet). A stomp in place may put a `warp` of about sqrt(S) on those frames instead.
- **Planted feet hold; airborne limbs hang loose.** Tanaka: show the difference between a loaded and an unloaded limb. A foot in the air gets an exaggerated rotation to show that no force acts on it; a planted foot is fixed firmly and sinks a little. On R6: a sole cannot sink into the floor (SKILL.md, "Feet rule"), so sink the body: drop the torso 0.03 to 0.06 S studs over the 3 to 4 frames after each contact while `Feet.flat` keeps both soles flat (our starting values; on a scaled rig this needs the planting of Building a character at another size). This is the small sink of every walking step, which the shake above replaces on heavy contacts; a landing's longer sink and recovery is in anime-craft.md, "Action beats" ("The length of the impact sets the weight"). R6 has no ankle, so the loose foot becomes a loose swing leg: let it swing 5 to 10 degrees past the stock walk's forward angle, with less slide up into the hip so the foot still clears the floor by only about half the stock lift times S (A giant lifts its heavy leg only briefly), and settle back onto its plant, while the planted leg holds dead still (our starting values).
- **A giant cannot snap between poses: layer instead.** Tanaka: a giant cannot change pose suddenly, and even a small change shows from the player's view. Damage can start at any time from any pose, so the talk layers additive animations in the engine: small pose changes, the same first and last pose, as short as possible. In The Last Guardian the layer moved planted feet, so the feet took the additive through IK, stayed fixed while planted, and the centre-of-mass shift that caused was handled. On R6: Poser plays one clip per rig and has no additive layer. Build a giant's flinch while walking as the explicit composite of pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays": the walk's legs and `Feet.flat` stay (on a scaled rig, see Building a character at another size), and the flinch rides on the torso, arms and head as offsets under a weight envelope. Follow that file's additive rules (an offset from the flinch's own first frame, faded out over its last 0.12 s, "Poser clip contract") and keep it short, ending at zero offset. combat.md, "The victim on contact", has the additive shake for an enemy that cannot be interrupted.
- **A big body's ripples settle slowly.** Clayton: if a big creature stops suddenly or hits something, "the ripple effect caused by that sudden stop needs to take place slowly. That's what gives you the cue that it's big." In a crane swing, momentum "slowly built up speed" until the crane hit, then the energy passed to the target, "almost like those little Newton's cradle swinging balls". On R6: after a giant's stop or slam, the arms, head and props keep moving and settle over shrinking bounces (SKILL.md, the follow-through row); on a giant, run those bounces slowly. With `speed = 1 / math.sqrt(S)`, a `follow` spring (6 Hz at S = 1) already acts at 6 / sqrt(S) Hz. Let a heavy swing build its speed over the whole forward arc on `auto` keys, with no snap out of the load, and hold the contact by strength (combat.md, "Hitstop").
- **Small bodies start, stop and turn at once.** Tanaka: a small mass can stop, start and turn suddenly, and its paths may break. Knabe's rule, run the other way (our extension): at S = 0.5 the clip plays 1.41 times faster. On R6: on a small character (a pet, a sprite, a shrunken player), play at `1 / math.sqrt(S)`, keep holds short, allow `flat` stops on the head and hands, and put the hop of r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons", at stock height so it reads big against the small body (our reading).

## Heavy bodies

- **A heavy body walks slower on a wider base.** Ko, Stenholm and Ferrucci (2010, Journal of Biomechanics; 164 adults aged 50 to 84 in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging): preferred speed 1.20 m/s at normal weight, 1.13 overweight, 1.06 obese; stride width 10.0, 10.6 and 12.4 cm; stance 63.3, 63.7 and 64.5% of the cycle. The obese did more absorbing mechanical work at the hip in the side-to-side plane, and earlier studies also found slower speed, wider steps and longer stance. On R6: for a realistic heavy or obese NPC, widen the foot targets in the same ratio (x from 0.5 to about 0.62 on each side, our scaling of the 1.24 ratio) and walk about 12% slower. Move the torso further over each stance foot: torso `p.x` sway up to about 1.5 times the 0.17 stud walk range of locomotion.md, "Walk body motion", and a roll toward the planted foot about 1.5 times that file's plus or minus 1.7 degrees (our starting values). The 1.2 points of longer stance are under a frame per step; leave the duty.
- **A boss walk reads heavy when it slows, sways and drops.** DevForum feedback on a Roblox boss walk ("Bricklord", 2024, keyed on linear easing only): "more of a delicate sashay than a menacing boss saunter. Try slowing down the movement overall by at least 50% and add more dramatic swings from side to side to feel like Bricklord is stomping instead of tiptoeing". Others asked for a torso bounce, a forward tilt (it leaned back) and a bigger torso twist, and pointed out that a still torso under moving limbs looks wrong. On R6: for a stylised, menacing boss at stock size, build the walk for 0.5 to 0.7 of the brisk walk's speed with a matching WalkSpeed (no slide). Roll the torso 4 to 6 degrees toward each stance foot, drop torso `p.y` into each contact, pitch forward 4 to 8 degrees from the waist and widen the twist to about twice locomotion.md's plus or minus 3.5 degrees (our starting values). locomotion.md, "Walk body motion", warns that a big torso twist reads as a plod: that is the read a heavy boss wants, so keep the stock twist for an ordinary walk. SKILL.md's exaggeration row agrees: push the torso's twist, lean and drop, not the leg lift.
- **Stomps need a cue on the contact frame.** DevForum ("Haha Mech go stomp", 2020): a small sine and cosine sway and up-and-down bounce gave a procedural mech "that big stompy feel"; reviewers asked for footstep sounds, particles on the ground material and camera shake when the foot touches the ground. On R6: put an event on each contact key (for a 0.84 s walk loop, `events = {stepL = 0, stepR = 0.42}`) and fire the footstep sound on the frame the clip clock crosses each time. Poser has no marker dispatcher, so the caller schedules them (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"). Particles and camera shake are VFX and camera work: offer them as a question (SKILL.md, opening paragraph).

## Load carriage

- **A pack bends the trunk and shortens the steps.** Liew, Morris and Netto (2016, Journal of Applied Biomechanics; systematic review of 54 studies): walking with a backpack increased the trunk flexion angle, the hip and ankle ranges of motion, the vertical and horizontal ground forces and the cadence, and reduced stride length. Knapik, Harman and Reynolds (1996, Applied Ergonomics review): heavier pack loads raise ground forces, knee flexion and forward trunk inclination. A load close to the body's centre of gravity has the lowest energy cost, and a double pack (half in front, half behind) produces fewer deviations from normal walking than a backpack. On R6: lean from the combined COM as body-mechanics.md, "Carrying, pushing and lifting", describes. Then shorten the step about 10% and raise the cadence to keep the speed, and deepen the torso dip of each stance by 0.05 to 0.1 studs for the extra knee bend (our starting values). A load split front and back gets less lean than a backpack of the same mass.
- **Added load lengthens contact, shrinks the back kick and adds lean.** Xu et al. (2017, BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders; four women running at their preferred speeds in an adjustable weight vest): with 30% of body weight added, stance rose from 37.8 to 43.2% of the cycle, peak vertical ground force from 2.7 to 3.1 body weights, and peak hip extension fell from 23.2 to 10.5 degrees. All subjects leaned forward to make up for the lost hip extension, shown by peak hip flexion before terminal swing rising from 33.8 to 45.8 degrees; the knee showed no systematic change. On R6: in a run under heavy armour or a loaded vest, keep each foot down about 29% of the cycle instead of 25% (about 1.5 more frames per contact on a 0.68 s cycle). Kick the back leg to about half the stock -50 degrees (the source's ratio is 0.45) and lean the torso 4 to 8 degrees further (our starting value). The source's bigger front swing comes with that lean, so keep the leg's front swing against the torso at the stock limit (SKILL.md, "Runs are judged by the legs").
- **A loaded child stiffens and slows.** Singh and Koh (two 2009 studies of children aged about 10, packs of 10, 15 and 20% of body weight on the upper or lower back): in 15 boys, above 15% the pelvic and trunk rotation fell and upper-body stiffness rose (Journal of Biomechanics). In 17 children at 20%, walking speed, cadence and double support time changed; with the load low on the back, speed and cadence fell and double support rose (Gait and Posture). On R6: under a heavy pack, halve the torso twist, stiffen the upper body with a smaller arm swing, and add about 0.05 to the planted share (our starting values). The backpack review found a higher cadence and these children at 20% a lower one: give a loaded adult the quicker step and a loaded child the slower one (our reading; both kept).

## Strength and effort

- **The lift's duration sells the weight.** Hamilton, Joyce, Flanagan, Frith and Wolpert (2007, Psychological Research): observers judged a box's weight mainly from the duration of the lift (28% of the variance in judged weight, under 1% for the place). The early lift, from lift-off to peak speed, counted more than the late lift (10% against 2%), and the first 125 ms of the lift counted far more than the grasp (15% against 2%). Their standard lift lasted 876 ms and was resampled from 75 to 125%. In the real lifts, the grasp duration predicted the true weight better than the lift duration. On R6: to make a lifted object read heavy, lengthen the hands' acceleration off the surface. Keep the first 8 frames (125 ms) of the lift slow and put the speed peak late, or stretch that segment with a `warp` of 0.8 (1.25 times as long) and leave the slowing into the hold; weapons.md, "Weight: a heavy blade, two hands, a step every swing", loads a heavy blade the same way (0.7 to 0.85). A light object leaves fast. A longer grasp is true to real lifts but barely moves a viewer's judgement, so spend the time on the early lift (our reading).
- **Force breaks the curve; too much drag reads weak.** Tanaka: muscles can only pull, and they stiffen when they work. A path changes only for a reason, and muscle is the main one: a sudden change in the curve shows force, a smooth curve shows a relaxed body. The drag that makes motion supple (nokoshi, a part left behind) makes a character look powerless when overused. On R6: on a strong character's effort beat, break the curve on purpose with a `flat` key or a short named ease into the effort pose, and no `lag` on the working parts (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain", already keeps it off the acting limb). Hold carried-part `lag` at 1 frame (0.017 s) on a strong, tense character and give the 2 to 3 frames and `drag` springs to a relaxed or weak one (our mapping onto SKILL.md's 1 to 3 frame range).

## Children

- **Young children have no clean walk-run switch.** Usherwood et al. (2018): 18 children aged 1.1 to 4.7 years (leg length 0.31 to 0.525 m), free to choose their gait, showed no discrete walk-run transition; their duty factor and the skew of their ground force changed steadily with speed. Taller and older people switch at the dimensionless speed 0.75. On R6: for a toddler-sized character, do not swap a walk clip for a run clip at one speed. Drive one procedural cycle whose planted share falls steadily from about 0.6 to 0.35 as speed rises, reading the speed from `ctx` as the locomotion starter does (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"; our starting values). Keep the swap at the switch speed for adults.
- **New walkers hold the arms high and the feet wide.** Gimunová et al. (2021, International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health; 26 toddlers): after 1 to 5 weeks of independent walking the arm's peak angle from the body (hip, shoulder and wrist points) was 78 to 87 degrees; after 11 to 15 weeks it was 47 to 49 (the "high guard" lowers). Step width fell from 0.19 to 0.12 m while step length stayed 0.18 m, and the left hip's range fell from about 42 to 29 degrees. The trunk's forward-back range went from 8.5 to 5.9 degrees, a change that was not significant. Kubo and Ulrich (2006; six toddlers who showed the high guard, seen twice a month apart): their walking speed rose across the visits as their arm postures lowered. On R6: for a first-steps toddler, translate the high guard through the forearm pose: arms 40 to 60 degrees out and a little forward, each slid up 0.2 to 0.3 studs along the torso side so the arm reads as a raised forearm (r6-craft.md, "Pose the arm as the forearm when the elbow matters"), held there with small wobble (our starting values). The measured 78 to 87 degrees is the read to aim for, not a joint value: a straight R6 arm raised 80 degrees reads as a T-pose (our reading). Set the soles as far apart as a step is long and pitch the torso through about 8 degrees per stride (our mapping). For a child who has walked for months, drop the slide and lower the arms well below the high guard (the measured angle falls to 47 to 49 degrees). Keep each arm touching the torso (SKILL.md, "Feet rule", the arm contact).
- **Children take more steps for their size.** Voss et al. (2020, Gait and Posture; 164 participants aged 5 to 30): absolute stride length and speed rose with age, while cadence (absolute and scaled by size) and speed scaled by size fell. At a fast walk, children aged 5 to 8 had longer stance and double support and shorter swing than 11 to 13 year olds (and, at 5 to 6, than young adults). Thevenon et al. (2015, Annals of Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine; 382 children aged 6 to 12): cadence fell with height, the timing values rose with height from 110 to 130 cm and then levelled off, and overweight children had a longer stance and more double support. On R6: a child on a stock-size R6 reads younger with shorter, quicker steps at the same speed, about 15 to 25% more steps per second, and a slightly larger planted share at a fast walk (our starting values).

## Older adults

- **An older walk: slower cadence, longer double support, wider steps.** Letournel et al. (2025, Bioengineering; 36 community-dwelling adults aged 62 to 91, mean 74, in Xsens suits): cadence 88 and 97 steps per minute (left and right) against an adult mean of 113, and double support 53% of the cycle against about 20% for healthy adults. Steps were 0.2 m wide against norms of 0.08 to 0.10 m (the authors' manual heel and toe marking raises that value), with lower speed and shorter strides than adult norms. The suits slowed the walk itself: 1.2 m/s in the six-minute walk test, 0.5 m/s with the suits on. The paper's table also gives a 2.2 s stride, which does not match that cadence. On R6: step an older NPC slower than a young adult: between about 37 to 41 frames per step (the measured 88 to 97 steps per minute; our conversion) and Williams' 50 frames for "an elderly or tired walk" (locomotion.md, "Frames per step"), the frailer the slower. Plant each foot 65 to 70% of the cycle and widen the soles a little (our starting values); SKILL.md's 55 to 60% ("Walks need double support") holds for a young adult's walk.
- **Older walkers lose hip extension, push-off and arm swing.** Kerrigan et al. (1998, Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation; 31 healthy adults aged 65 to 84 against 31 young adults): the differences that remained at both comfortable and fast speeds were reduced peak hip extension, increased forward pelvic tilt, and reduced ankle plantarflexion and ankle power generation. Khiyara, Sidaway and Hejrati (2026, Sensors, abstract): reduced arm swing is frequent in older adults. They cite experiments in which restricting arm swing shortened the stride and slowed the walk, and in their 12 older adults, haptic cues that enlarged the arm swing raised walking speed and stride length. On R6: cut the back leg's swing behind the hip to 60 to 70% of a young walk's. Pitch the torso 3 to 6 degrees forward from the waist through the whole cycle (the single torso part shows the pelvic tilt as a whole-body pitch), flatten the torso bob a little for the weaker push-off, and halve the arm swing (our starting values). For a frail character add the hunch of r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons".

## Fatigue and limps

- **Tired runners lean, shorten their steps and sway.** Winter, Gordon and Watt (2017, Journal of Sports Medicine and Physical Fitness; systematic review of 12 studies, abstract): the changes reported during fatiguing runs included lower speed, step or stride frequency and length, more trunk flexion, changes in side-to-side acceleration and in the hip and knee ranges, and lower stride regularity; the authors call the evidence limited. Rubio et al. (2026, Scientific Reports; 20 young, healthy women): after 5 km of running, or 5 km of walking with 22.7 kg at 1.5 m/s, peak trunk flexion rose in both conditions, with the same trend in 18 of the 20. Its effect size of 0.80 or more is the authors' mark of a relevant change; in the run it missed their corrected significance threshold. On R6: a tired run lowers the cadence and the stride 5 to 10%, leans 4 to 6 degrees further from the waist, lifts the swing leg into the hip 0.5 to 0.6 studs instead of 0.7 to 0.95, and doubles the torso's side sway (our starting values). Shorter, slower strides cut the clip's speed by up to a fifth, so slow the game speed with it or the feet slide (SKILL.md, "Locomotion speed"). Blend toward it over a long chase from a stamina value in `ctx`, read by a procedural locomotion clip like the starter's (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"; the controller must add this field to `ctx`: new controller code).
- **Fatigue widens the steps and breaks the repeat.** Helbostad et al. (2007, Journals of Gerontology; 22 older people with a mean age of 78, tired by repeated sit-to-stands, against 22 controls with a mean age of 80): preferred speed and step length did not change, but step width, side-to-side trunk acceleration and step-length variability rose. On R6: a fatigued walk keeps its speed, widens each foot target about 0.05 studs and adds side sway (our starting values). A one-stride Poser loop repeats exactly, so author a two-stride loop whose four steps differ by 5 to 10% in length (our starting values). Give each step a duration in proportion to its length, 1 to 2 frames apart, so every planted foot still moves at the root speed (SKILL.md, "Break the default bell", planted feet).
- **A pain limp shortens the stance against the swing.** Auerbach and Tadi (StatPearls, "Antalgic Gait in Adults", abstract): antalgic gait is walking altered by pain into a limp "whereby the stance phase is shortened relative to the swing phase"; healthy gait is cyclical and symmetric. On R6: break the mirror of locomotion.md, "Walk landmarks and mirrors", on purpose. Plant the hurt leg for about 0.45 of the cycle and the good leg for about 0.65 (our reading: the shortened stance is the hurt leg's). Overlap the two windows so one foot is always down, hurry the body off the hurt leg, and drop the torso deeper on the good leg's contact (our starting values). Each `Feet.flat` target can be its own function of time (pipeline.md, "Planted feet"), so the asymmetry needs no new code.
- **Blend a limp in by health.** DevForum ("Low HP limping effect", 2022): a developer blended the normal walk with an edited limping walk, setting the limp's animation weight from health below 50 HP. It blended oddly with the default walk until a reply suggested the Action2 priority, which the poster thanked; another reply said any priority above Movement, the default Animate script's, should work, though the poster found some did not. On R6: Poser plays one clip per rig, so drive the limp from one procedural walk whose leg timing and torso offsets read a hurt value from 0 to 1 in `ctx` (the controller must add this field to `ctx`: new controller code). Or bake both walks and blend them with native Animator weights at a priority above Movement (pipeline.md, "Native Animator playback"). Give both walks the same cycle length and foot-down times, or the blended feet slide (locomotion.md, "Keeping feet planted across speeds and directions", phase sync).

## One weight class across a clip set

- **Fix the weight class first, then derive every clip from it.** Tanaka: estimate a giant's weight from creatures of similar size (a 27 m character at about 40 t from sauropod sizes, a leg at 15% or 6 t) and picture it before animating. Clayton: early motion studies of George at his different sizes, leaping off cars beside a gravity indicator, got the client on board with the idea that the bigger he grew, the slower his jumps and landings would be. On R6: before keying the set, write the class into the clip plan ([clip-plan.md](../templates/clip-plan.md)): S, the build (light, average, heavy), the rate factor 1/sqrt(S), the planted share, the foot lift, the contact sink, the lean, the arm swing and the spring presets. Build one test clip (a start, a stop, a jump and a landing), get it accepted, then take every clip's numbers (idle, locomotion, attacks, hits) from that table so no clip plays lighter than its neighbours (our method). anime-craft.md, "Contrast across a scene", covers breaking the scale on purpose once it is set.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Ankle push-off and plantarflexion changes with age and weight (Kerrigan et al., Ko et al.): there is no ankle; they show only as a shorter back swing and a flatter bob.
- Forward pelvic tilt apart from the chest (Kerrigan et al.): one torso part; only a whole-torso pitch shows it.
- The grasp of the fingers (Hamilton et al.): the R6 hand is the end of the arm block, and the grasp barely moved the observers' judgements.
- Extra knee bend under a load (Knapik et al.): a stance-knee slide that small does not read on a rigid leg (human-motion-data.md, "Does not transfer to R6"); a deeper torso dip stands in.
- Body shape by type: a child's big head, a heavy body's girth, a giant's thick limbs. Parts cannot scale or change shape in a clip; a different build needs different meshes, and the motion must carry the read.
- Muscle that hardens under effort (Tanaka): R6 parts do not deform; only the break in the curve shows force.
- Loose clothes and long hair to show scale (Knabe): R6 has no cloth; only a rig with extra motored parts can swing them on springs.
- Falling dirt, particles, fog and camera shake to sell size (Knabe, the mech thread): VFX, lighting and camera work, not animation; offer them as a question.
