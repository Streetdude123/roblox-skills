# Anime craft: timing, acting and action on six parts

What acclaimed TV anime does with timing, acting and action, taken as taste and translated to R6 clips in Poser: smooth against stepped playback, tame and tsume, ones, twos and threes, contrast across a scene, action beats, restrained acting, personality in movement and small gestures. Frieren: Beyond Journey's End is the main quality reference; nothing here copies a shot. Every finding was checked against its source ([sources.md](sources.md), "Anime craft: timing, acting and action on six parts research"). Film timings are at 24 fps; 60 fps conversions multiply by 2.5 and are marked "our conversion". Numbers marked "our choice", "our example", "our numbers" or "our reading" are starting values, not the source's. Quotes from Japanese sources are our translations. Hold lists, impact frames and quantised twos for stepped clips are in stylized-and-roblox.md, and stepped strikes in combat.md, "Anime-style stepped strikes"; this file does not repeat them. A step is one held pose of a stepped clip, a key gap is the frames between two keys, and frames are 60 fps frames unless marked film frames.

## Smooth playback or stepped

The rule under test: anime timing works best inside smooth 60 fps playback, with full stepped timing only where it suits, such as emotes and finishers. For it: modern action animators work at high drawing rates and build rhythm from spacing, and stepped drawings misalign against a moving camera. Against it: anime staff find full-rate motion too slick for many shots, and limited animation hides subtle motion on purpose. The sources agree that even timing with even spacing fails at any rate. Verdict: keep gameplay clips smooth, as combat.md, "Anime-style stepped strikes", already sets for strikes, and put the anime contrast into spacing and holds. Build stepped versions for fixed-camera emotes and finishers and let the requester pick per clip.

- **Modern action anime gets its rhythm from spacing, not from stepping.** Animétudes on Hiroto Nagata: "an even timing, most often on 1's and 2's". On a Ryuki Hashimoto cut where a girl jumps away from the camera and runs back: the timing is "mostly oscillating between 1s and 2s (with the 3DCG background constantly on 1s)", so "it is the spacing which creates the real sense of rhythm, very close when the action slows down and much faster as it accelerates." Yutaka Nakamura "focuses on rhythm rather than timing" (Animétudes, "The Kanada style now"). On R6: keep `curve = "spline"` and put the rhythm into the spacing. In a lunge, key the torso at equal distances along its path, with gaps that shrink as the body accelerates (8, 5, 3, 2 frames) and widen into the settle (3, 6, 10 frames; our example).
- **Smooth motion fails when it is even.** Animétudes on Yasuomi Umetsu's Robot Carnival work: "the uniform timing on 1s or 2s makes the character animation too fluid and regular, to the point of tedium". With timing that regular and spacing that even, the animation ends up "completely uninteresting"; "without the sense of spontaneity built by the timing, the animation ends up mechanical and jarring." Wave Motion Cannon reports Richard Williams' view that ones look better in nearly every case. Williams grants that ones can look "mushy" and do not look as good with "dumb, mechanical inbetweens". The article's example is a March Comes in like a Lion cut on smooth ones whose evenly spaced in-betweens look "soulless and artificial". On R6: equal key gaps are the metronome that keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing", already forbids, and even spacing inside a spline segment is the same fault. Sample the leading joint's speed per frame from `Poser.posesAt`. In an acted pose-to-pose move, a run of six or more frames at near-constant speed (our threshold) needs a favour key 1 to 2 frames from one end (lesson-videos.md, "4. Bluebiscuits: the practical tricks"), not stepping; planted feet, root travel and the oscillations of loops are exempt (SKILL.md, "Break the default bell"). A `Poser.check` contrast under 2 (SKILL.md, step 4) flags the same fault over a whole one-shot.
- **Full-rate motion can look too slick, so anime staff pick the rate per cut.** Naoki Miyahara, episode director on The First Slam Dunk, a film that fuses CG and hand-drawn animation, in MOVIE WALKER PRESS: the film used everything from full animation to threes, point by point, mainly at a threes tempo. The team first showed director Takehiko Inoue that full animation looks slick ("nurutto"), then showed frame-adjusted versions and let him choose for each place. The article explains that full animation allows delicate expression but often moves too smoothly and looks wrong, and needs many more drawings, so Japanese animation mostly uses threes. Takashi Nakamura animated most of The Portrait Studio on twos because "24 frames per second would be too fluid for the mood in the film, and 8 frames per second would weaken the appeal in the image" (Animation Obsessive). On R6: the slick risk is real in slow, stylized acting on a 3D body. For a fixed-camera moment (an emote with its own camera, a finisher cutscene), build two labelled versions, smooth and stepped, and let the requester pick per clip (process.md, "Picking references and options"). Take the stepped version's hold lengths from stylized-and-roblox.md, "Holds and contrast".
- **A moving camera needs a moving body.** Miyahara: where camera movement and character movement do not mesh, even twos produce a slight misalignment, so those cuts were drawn in full while avoiding a slick look. Wave Motion Cannon: when the camera pans across a background, the animation needs ones, "or else the movement of the background and the animation will not match up"; it adds that anime often does not bother. On R6: the default camera follows the character's Humanoid (creator docs, `Camera.CameraSubject`), so the camera travels whenever the character does. A body that steps every 5 to 8 frames while the root and the camera glide shows this mismatch (our reading). Keep gameplay clips smooth. Step only when the camera is fixed or cut to, and keep root travel smooth even then (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Stepped anime timing").
- **Limited animation hides subtle motion; an R6 hold must not freeze.** Kitamura, Kanamori, Mitani, Fukui and Tsuruno (Kyushu University and University of Tsukuba, IWAIT 2014) model limited animation by two features: it "does not show subtle motion because it omits almost-static inbetween frames", and it exaggerates speed by omitting in-betweens (nakanashi). Their converter works on motion capture subsampled to 24 fps. It holds poses up to three frames where motion is smallest, because repeating more "makes the motion completely stop", and found holding about a third of all frames adequate. On R6: this contradicts the living-hold rule (SKILL.md: frozen 0%, `life` 0.6 to 1.5 degrees; principles.md, "Moving holds and settles"). Keep both, by clip type. Smooth clips keep the drift and `life`. In a hand-posed stepped emote or finisher, a step that carries motion lasts at most 7 to 8 frames (three film frames, our conversion). Change the whole pose a little on every step, as Mitsuo Iso's "full limited" animation treats "every single pose as key" (Sakuga Blog). Deliberate holds and settles follow the hold list in stylized-and-roblox.md, "Holds and contrast". In a stepped clip set `life = 0`, or 0 for each stepped joint in a `life` table, and put no springs on stepped joints: `life` is a clip field, not a key field (`lifeAmp` in `scripts/Poser.lua`). stylized-and-roblox.md, "Stepped anime timing", already bars springs on stepped keys, and `life` noise would make every step drift (our reading). Quantised twos keep `life`, because they run the full stack before quantising.
- **Under fixed simulation, Roblox caps the finest step at the simulation rate.** Creator docs: with `Workspace.UseFixedSimulation`, "character controller updates and joint transforms are performed at a fixed frequency rather than once per frame". In a DevForum report, a project with Server Authority enabled had UseFixedSimulation on as a result, and its animations updated at a fixed frequency instead of every frame. Another developer in the thread saw the character run at 60 FPS in a game running at 165 FPS. On R6: author and bake at 60 fps and treat 1 frame as the smallest step. In such places a high-refresh screen already shows the character steppier than the world, so do not add stepping to gameplay clips there (our reading). Remote characters can update less often under Animator LOD (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Level of detail and masks").
- **Export a stepped clip as a KeyframeSequence, never as Constant keys in a curve animation.** A DevForum bug report (October 2025): Constant keyframes in curve-editor animations "interpolate as if they were linear", so parts visibly move between steps. A Roblox staff member could not reproduce it in July 2026; the reporter still could, and Constant keys in normal keyframe animations still worked for him. On R6: keep a stepped clip in a KeyframeSequence, either with keys only at the step times and Constant easing (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Markers and easing") or as a `Poser.bake` at 60 fps, whose Linear samples change each step within one frame (pipeline.md, "Baking and export"). Do not rebuild it as a curve animation, and check the played track per frame in Play.

## Tame, tsume and nakanashi

- **Tame and tsume name the key the in-betweens crowd toward.** A Ritsumeikan University paper (Entertainment Computing Symposium 2019): limited animation in-betweens a move with few drawings and, instead of even in-betweens, uses tame and tsume. With tame the frames near the first key are drawn close to it; with tsume the frames near the last key are drawn close to it. A strong tame stresses the acceleration, a strong tsume the deceleration; the exaggeration breaks physics but reads as natural "mental motion". On R6: the favour keys in lesson-videos.md, "4. Bluebiscuits: the practical tricks", are a mild tame and tsume. A strong one is a split of frames against travel. For a 120 degree arm swing over 20 frames, a strong tame puts a key at frame 8 at 10% of the travel and a strong tsume a key at frame 12 at 90%, all `auto` (our example). Between those keys the arm turns 96 degrees in 4 frames, 24 degrees per frame, just inside the 25 degree cap for key gaps (principles.md, "Strike speed and retiming").
- **Write the spacing, not the word.** The terms are not used one way. Toru Yoshida (mecha and effects animator, CGWORLD) uses tame for the slowing before a stop. The Ritsumeikan paper uses tame for crowding at the first key. A Japanese wiki entry on in-betweening (douga, Thirdpedia) warns that "tsume" means the opposite in drawn animation and in 3DCG. On R6: in a clip plan or a review note, never write "more tame". Write "frames 0 to 8 cover the first 10% of the swing" or "the last 45 degrees take the last 8 frames".
- **A fast arrival still slows before it stops.** Yoshida: a 3D robot that spins round lacks weight when it moves at the same speed throughout; "even when it flies in with momentum, just before it stops, tame it", and drawn animation spends the most drawings where a part moves little by little. On R6: a turn, spin, slide or dash that ends in a held pose with nothing to hit spends its last quarter of travel over about a third of its frames (our choice). A 180 degree turn over 24 frames covers its last 45 degrees in the last 8 frames, with `auto` keys and `e = "flat"` only on the final pose, which then drifts as a moving hold (SKILL.md, "Moving holds"). When the stop is a contact, the impact rule wins: no ease into an impact (SKILL.md, the "Spacing, slow in and slow out" row).
- **Nakanashi drops the in-betweens at peak speed, a few at most.** The Kitamura et al. converter deletes the frames with the most motion, and the deleted frames tend to be successive: 4 to 6 film frames from the fastest stretch of a pitch, a kick, a tennis swing and a baseball swing. Its clips of 88 to 129 film frames used 4 to 6 deletions. "Around five is just right"; with more than five, "the motion no longer looks continuous". On R6: in a smooth clip, play the fastest stretch faster than authored with `warp`, so fewer real frames show it, and slow the load slightly to keep the length. Here the 0.08 s before a contact key at 0.4 plays in about 2.4 real frames instead of 4.8 (our example):

  ```lua
  clip.warp = {{0, 0.9}, {0.3, 0.9}, {0.32, 2}, {0.4, 2}, {0.41, 1}}
  ```

  Read the real hit time with `Poser.realTime(clip, 0.4)`. On a melee hit, replace the last point with the hitstop segment, which starts on the contact key (combat.md, "Hitstop"). Check the sampled joint turn per real frame against principles.md, "Strike speed and retiming". Use one or two such bursts per clip, never on every beat. The burst is for an authored, stylized strike; a strike copied from a reference keeps its last 0.12 s at authored speed (principles.md, "Retime only before the hit"). In a stepped finisher, delete the strike's middle steps so the fist jumps from the load step straight to the contact step.
- **Two poses can carry half a second.** Yoshida on Yoshinori Kanada: an explosion drawn as two key drawings with no in-betweens fills 12 film frames (0.5 s). The first drawing shows for 5 frames, the motion jumps, the next shows for 7, "and with two drawings it looks like an explosion". On R6: in a stepped finisher, a 30-frame beat can be two steps of about 12 and 18 frames (our conversion of 5 and 7) joined by a jump with no in-between. The longer second step is the result, so give it the stronger silhouette (our reading). In a smooth clip the same beat is a 2 to 3 frame change between two moving holds of those lengths.
- **Find the timing by acting it, filming it and saying its sound.** Yoshida: act the motion yourself, film it, and write both the real timing and the timing that feels right on the timesheet. "There is always a difference", for example "move sharply only at the start, then slowly". Mecha animators "talk in onomatopoeia": a robot that rises with a "juiiin" is 18 frames, and the sound alone sets the timesheet. On R6: keep two beat tables in the clip plan, the reference's real frame counts and the stylized ones, and report the difference next to the reference at hand-off (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference", step 5). An 18-frame rise is 45 frames at 60 fps (our conversion). When a move has a sound effect, make the move as long as the sound's main body and put the key on its peak (our reading); pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays", gives the `PlaybackLoudness` method for cutscenes.

## Ones, twos, threes and modulation

- **Threes are the base; ones are spent where they count.** Yoshida's book, quoted by CGWORLD: 2D anime runs at 24 fps with threes (each drawing shot three times) as the base, and ones, twos and fours chosen by direction. For a missile launch he prefers all-key fours: ones are possible when drawings are plentiful, but usually they are not, and for feel fours are enough. The Ritsumeikan paper: mainstream limited animation draws 8 or 12 drawings a second. Wave Motion Cannon: in a Hayao Miyazaki scene from Samurai Giants most of the action is on threes and only the speed of the pitcher's throw is on twos. A CLIP STUDIO tutorial gives the general split: ones for fast action, twos or threes for normal movement. On R6: the lesson is where anime spends motion, not its rate. A 60 fps spline already plays every beat on ones, so the contrast a viewer sees is spacing (degrees per frame), not the number of keys. In the one beat that carries the read, the watched part reaches the highest peak speed in the clip, and the slow beats run at about a third of it or less (our choice); check with per-frame speeds and the `Poser.check` contrast of 2 to 10 (SKILL.md, step 4). Dense keys (1 to 3 frames apart, against gaps of 8 to 12 elsewhere) are one way to shape that beat's spacing. That beat is the crescendo of keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing".
- **Change the rate inside one move to give it texture.** Animétudes ("Animation and subjectivity"): in a Miyazaki shot a pitcher's slow, deliberate stretch runs on a 3-4 rhythm; when he raises his leg the rhythm oscillates between threes and twos with the acceleration. The rate "may sometimes change two or three times" in a second. A viewer rarely notices, but it "gives the movement its individuality and most of its texture". Wave Motion Cannon: Kanada's irregular rate makes motion "feel more lifelike and organic than they would be if evenly timed". On R6: stylized-and-roblox.md, "Holds and contrast", already sets irregular hold lengths for stepped clips; aim for two or three changes a second. In a smooth clip the same texture is a change of spacing two or three times a second inside one move. For a wind-up and throw, key every 8 to 10 frames through the wind-up, 5 to 7 through the acceleration, 2 to 3 through the release, then 6 and 10 into the settle (our reading).
- **Give the finest timing to the part the eye should follow.** Wave Motion Cannon: modulation can "establish a visual hierarchy of motion". In Mamoru Hosoda's Digimon Adventure the fireball and explosion effects move at different rates from the characters, "which draws your eyes to the most important movement". The electric effects switch from ones to threes, which "pop out on the screen and makes us feel the power of the impact". Keiichiro Kimura's 1969 Tiger Mask opening starts on twos and switches to ones on the cape swirl as the hero transforms. Takashi Nakamura put the water on ones in a film mostly on twos "to give it more dynamism" (Animation Obsessive). On R6: name the part the player should watch in each beat and give only it the dense keys and the overshoot. In a cast, the casting arm sweeps on key gaps of 2 to 3 frames while the torso and head move on gaps of 8 to 10 (our example). SKILL.md already requires the strike to be the fastest motion in its window; this adds a difference of texture.
- **Two subjects, two timings: the difference shows who is who.** Animétudes: in a cut by Hisashi Mori a fireball moves on ones and a giant bird on twos. At the hit the bird, writhing, switches to ones, and the fireball, which has lost energy, drops to threes. In Yoshihiko Umakoshi's Mushishi the mushi creatures are always on ones, "which emphasizes their otherness". Animétudes ties this to the principle "if you want to show the difference between two characters, animate them doing the same thing". Yasuo Otsuka used ones "to emphasize the speed of a thrown axe" and threes "to emphasize the scale of a giant" (Animation Obsessive, on Horus: Prince of the Sun). On R6: give a summoned companion or stand a timing texture unlike its user's (our reading). A spirit can run on continuous spline motion with `life = 1.5` and `drag` springs on its arms, never settling, against a user whose poses settle into short moving holds; a mechanical summon can run on crisp short key gaps with `lead` springs. Poser plays one clip per rig, so each rig carries its own clip and timing. At a hit, let the victim's shake be the fastest motion on screen while the attacker creeps (combat.md, "Hitstop"; our reading).
- **Ones while fast, wider timing as it slows, uneven in the fall.** Animétudes on Hashimoto's dropkick: "the animation begins on 1s, switches to 3s when the movement slows down, and then suddenly becomes irregular when the orc falls, oscillating between 4s, 3s and 2s." On a Shingo Yamashita sequence: "most of the sequence is on 1s, with some parts on 2s and exceptionally 3s on some of the impacts" (Animétudes, "The Kanada style now"). On R6: in a smooth clip, the last 2 to 3 frames before contact are the densest. After contact the key gaps widen (4, 6, 8 frames) as the limb slows. A victim's fall gets an uneven key rhythm (gaps of 10, 6, 9, 5 frames through a stumble, a bounce and a settle) instead of one long ease (our numbers). Yamashita's threes on impacts are a held impact; on R6 that is the hitstop (combat.md, "Hitstop"). Stepped hold lengths for the same beats are in stylized-and-roblox.md, "Holds and contrast".

## Contrast across a scene

- **Keep the burst at real speed and put the time into the preparation.** Sakuga Blog on Hyakuemu (2025): director Kenji Iwaisawa limited each race to "barely over 10 seconds" to put viewers in the athletes' headspace, although longer races would have been easier excitement. The manga's author, Uoto, "treasured the contrast between lengthy preparation and the blink-and-you-miss-it races". On R6: for a showcase move (an ultimate, an emote's payoff), keep the decisive action at real speed and short (a dash or strike of 6 to 12 frames). Give the reading time to the preparation before it (60 to 120 frames) and to the moving hold after it (our choice). stylized-and-roblox.md, "Holds and contrast", allows slow motion around the contact: use slow motion for spectacle, and real time when the player should feel inside the action. Player attacks keep the budgets in combat.md.
- **Quiet clips make the loud ones land.** Real Sound on Frieren: a long TV run needs contrast of pace, not only flashy battles but how ordinary daily life is shown, and Frieren's contrast works very well. Animation producer Takashi Nakame noted that episode 10 had "quite literally half the drawings" of episode 9 and still had to hold viewers (Sakuga Blog). Character designer and animator Franziska van Wulfen, naming Frieren her best show in the Sakugabooru Animation Awards 2023, wrote that what left the strongest impression "isn't so much the certainly impressive action sequences, but rather its character animation". On R6: plan a character's clip set as a whole: quiet, specific idles, walks and interactions, and full intensity only for a few signature moves. Print each clip's peak joint speeds (SKILL.md, step 4) and rank the clips; if most sit near the top, the set has no contrast.
- **Set the real scale, then break it.** Sakuga Blog on Frieren #06, storyboarded by action director Toru Iwazawa: Stark's "weighty walk builds up the tension", and the boards establish "the objective scale of the battle… just to progressively betray it as it explodes into a spectacular setpiece that goes far beyond that physical reality." On R6: in a boss entrance or a finisher, open with grounded motion at real weight. Use a slow, heavy walk (locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk"), the COM over the support (body-mechanics.md, "Balance and weight shift") and jumps no higher than the Humanoid's. Only after 1 to 3 s of that (our choice), let one move break physics: a leap far above JumpHeight 7.2, or a spin faster than body-mechanics.md, "Flips and spins", allows.
- **A ritual is shown with restraint; its bursts come second.** Sakuga Blog on Tsurune (Kyoto Animation): episode 1 shows a sports festival in boisterous animation, then the same artists "dial up the restrain to capture the nobility of kyudo". The shots still carry "bursts of explosive animation" for impact, but that "comes second to the solemn, ritualistic depiction of archery". On R6: a ceremonial action (a bow, a slow sword draw, a shot) moves slowly with small amplitudes, long moving holds and `life = 0.6`. It spends its one fast moment on the release in 2 to 4 frames, then holds the follow-through 30 frames or more while it drifts (our numbers). Keep the slow key gaps uneven (keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing") and the arms untwinned, even in a formal stance.

## Action beats

- **Surprise may drop the anticipation, in a cutscene only.** Animétudes on Takeshi Maenami's 2020 Monster Strike music video: "the way the character suddenly approaches the camera without anticipation and swings her cat is quite impressive and unexpected." On R6: this conflicts with the Anticipation row of SKILL.md and with combat.md, "Telegraphs and reaction time" (an unexpected fast action is missed without a bigger anticipation). Keep both. Drop the pull-back only in a cutscene or an emote where surprise is the point and a set camera frames the move. In gameplay keep at least one frame of pull-back.
- **The length of the impact sets the weight.** Yoshida: the biggest factor in weight is speed; "if the shock of the collision is short, 'don', it looks light; if it is slow, 'dooon', it looks heavy." Weight also shows through surrounding information, such as the ground cracking under a robot's foot and fragments flying. On R6: after a landing or a stomp, the length of the sink and recovery sets the weight. The sink is the torso dropping with the legs sliding up into it, R6's knee (body-mechanics.md, "Takeoff and landing"). A light body sinks for 3 to 4 frames and recovers in 6 to 10. A heavy body sinks for 5 to 8 frames, a little deeper, and recovers in 20 to 30, the arms settling last (our starting numbers). Cracks and debris are VFX: offer them as a question (SKILL.md).
- **Effort shows where the head holds still.** Kouta Morie (Transistor Studio, CGWORLD column): in one in-between before a leg swings down, the hips move but the head stays almost where it was two frames before. That acts "straining to swing the leg down". The same pose sets up the speed of the foot, the drop of the hand and the head lift that follows. On R6: the head rides on the top of the torso, so pivot the torso about its top instead of its centre. For a torso rotation R, add `p += (0, 1, 0) - R * (0, 1, 0)`, the waist offset of r6-mechanics.md, "Lean from the waist", with the pivot at the neck. A torso pitch of 5 to 8 degrees then swings the hips 0.17 to 0.28 studs while the head stays put (our numbers). Counter the Head key so its facing holds too, and let `Feet.flat` keep the soles. Use this pivot in the 2 to 4 frames before a stomp, kick or overhead swing, then release the head 1 to 2 frames after the torso moves on. Key the head on the torso's frames, never with `lag` (SKILL.md, "Offset the chain"). Make each breakdown point at the next beat, as Morie's does, rather than sit halfway.

## Acting with restraint

- **Restraint is a dial set per beat.** Miku Kadowaki (Kyoto Animation), as animation director: "I try to approach my work like a stage director for theater, as if I'm telling the characters, 'You need to cry more in this scene!' or 'Show some more emotional restraint here!'" On Hibike! Euphonium S3, Mayu's voice actress Haruka Tomatsu found the animation said so much that she worked out "how much to subtract when necessary so that things weren't spelled out too clearly before their due time" (Sakuga Blog). On R6: write one number per beat in the clip plan, the share of the full gesture that shows. A sad beat can show 40% early and 100% only on the beat that pays it off (our example). Scale the channels about the first frame to that share (principles.md, "Strike speed and retiming", "More range without losing the posture"). keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", already holds that stifled emotion can hit harder; this makes it a number.
- **Temper each gesture to the person.** Keiichiro Saito, Frieren's director, on Bocchi the Rock! (Sakuga Blog interview): the performance scenes use footage of real actors as the base, and the actors kept the characterization in mind. Hitori's performer tempered the gestures, "with fairly subdued posture and motions, to give the impression of not being used to performing". Ryo, normally quiet, plays "with a lot of enthusiasm". Nijika is characterized through a silent conversation with Ryo mid-performance, with emphasis on eye contact. On R6: author one action at a different size per character (our ranges). A shy or unpractised character moves at 50 to 70% of the reference's arm and torso range, arms 5 to 15 degrees out from the body sides so a gap still shows, head pitched down 5 to 10 degrees. A reserved character who comes alive in action moves at 110 to 120%, with more torso roll. This sets the character's range; the 10 to 20% push of SKILL.md's Exaggeration row still shapes each pose inside it. Two characters who know each other exchange a head turn at a beat change, with no other gesture.
- **Perform the reference bigger than life.** Sakuga Blog on Hyakuemu: Iwaisawa had the actors perform "in emphatic, somewhat exaggerated fashion that would feel more lifelike once stylized". On R6: when filming or choosing reference for an acting clip, ask for a bigger performance than natural, because R6 loses the hands, the face and the spine. Then copy it as authored (SKILL.md, "Always start from a reference", step 4). A bigger performance keeps its own timing; pushing a quiet reference afterwards changes only the poses.
- **Bend textbook gestures with the feeling.** Sakuga Blog on Yubisaki to Renren: sign language supervisor Yonaiyama said not to portray signs "like a series of gestures and poses you'd see in a textbook". Director Murano added "imperfections like making fingers tremble nervously, or even distorting the signs a bit so that they capture the emotion of the characters at the time." On R6: treat a catalog gesture (a wave, a point, a salute) as a starting shape and bend it with the mood (our examples). A nervous wave sits lower (the fist near shoulder height), with half the swing and a small arm tremble ramped in (principles.md, "Moving holds and settles", gives tremble sizes and ramps). A proud salute goes a few degrees past square and holds longer.
- **A hidden feeling leaks through one small part.** Sakuga Blog on Tsurune: the young Nikaido, "not used to suppressing his feelings yet", is shown with "nervous mannerisms when he can't bring himself to tell his master how he feels". In Eupho S3, Kumiko "actually pulls away" when Mayu approaches; Mayu notices the rejection "despite neither of them actually speaking their mind". On R6: a character hiding a feeling keeps the main pose on a slow moving hold and lets one small part leak for 8 to 15 frames (our numbers): the free arm pulses 3 to 6 degrees, the head leaves its target and returns, or the weight shifts. An unwelcome approach first gets a small lean away: the torso slides 0.1 to 0.2 studs away from the other character, with `Feet.flat` keeping the soles, before any larger reaction.
- **Act with the body; the face may not be seen.** Sakuga Blog on Pokemon Sun & Moon: Isao Nanba's acting "gained articulation in a more traditional way, making it convey the right emotion even when his amusing faces aren't visible." On R6: the stock face is a static decal and the player camera usually sits behind, so carry each emotion in torso pitch and roll, head angle and arm shape. Check it from behind: the `rear34` strip with the face decal removed (SKILL.md, Appeal row) must still show the emotion to a fresh reviewer (keyframe-workflow.md, "Review with fresh eyes").

## Personality in movement

- **Give each character signature mannerisms and repeat them.** Sakuga Blog on Eupho S3: over nearly a decade Kyoto Animation built "perhaps the most impressive acting library in anime altogether". Mayu fiddles with her hair when anxious, "in a distinct way than Sally does", and has a "tendency to sidestep when fleeing". Kanade pokes Kumiko in episode 3; five episodes later Kumiko blocks the poke. Behavior that "has become recognizable for characters and audiences alike" is what makes it effective. On R6: give each character two or three signature gestures (a hand raised beside the head when anxious, a sidestep before turning away, a habitual weight shift; our examples). Reuse the same keys across its idles, emotes and reactions, changing size, not shape. Solve the raised arm from the wanted direction (r6-mechanics.md, "Direction and rotation checks") and keep the fist within the overhead reach (SKILL.md, "Overhead reach").
- **Show the baseline before the change.** Sakuga Blog on Frieren #09, directed and storyboarded by Kouki Fujimoto: Fern is deadpan on the surface; "by seeing her normal walk and even some hesitation", tied to her feelings of inferiority, "her confidence and outright swagger in this fight hits even harder." On R6: when a character's arc has a confident payoff, keep its default walk and idle modest: a shorter stride, the head 3 to 5 degrees down, the arms close with a gap (locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk"). In the payoff, change the same parts the other way: head level, chest up 5 to 8 degrees bent at the waist (SKILL.md, "Waist rule"), arms out, a slower, heavier step (our numbers). Build both from the same keys so the attitude is the only difference.
- **An awkward walk reads as innocence.** Sakuga Blog on Pokemon Sun & Moon: co-supervising episode 59 with Yasushi Nishiya, Yasuhiko Akiyama showed "nothing but adorable animation but with more restraint, emphasizing Mao and Suiren's childlike innocence via details like their awkward walking." On R6: for a childlike or awkward walk, make the left and right steps differ by 1 to 2 frames, place the feet closer to the centre line and give the arms a smaller, less regular swing (our reading). Keep each foot planted 55 to 60% of the cycle (SKILL.md, "Walks need double support") and author it from a measured child or toddler reference, not by breaking a symmetric cycle. keyframe-workflow.md, "Contrast and phrasing", keeps short loops symmetric because any oddity shows the loop. A deliberate gait asymmetry is the exception: make it the same on every cycle, so it reads as the gait and not as a glitch at the seam (our reading).
- **Broad moves make a simple body feel rich.** Sakuga Blog on Pokemon Sun & Moon: Masaaki Iwane, the team's ace, with 22 years on the Pokemon anime, uses broad and exaggerated movements, which he "admitted he uses in an attempt to make the audience feel like the animation is more elaborate than it really is." On R6: six rigid parts show little detail, so an outgoing character's gesture travels far. An arm arc of 90 degrees or more carries the torso turn and a weight shift under it, not a small precise move the player's camera cannot see (our numbers). combat.md, "Readability from a pulled-back camera", sets the same for strikes, and stylized-and-roblox.md, "R6 critique from experienced animators", warns that an R6 move without enough exaggeration looks bad. Keep the restraint findings above for reserved characters.

## Small gestures that keep living

- **Small actions are where Frieren lives.** Franziska van Wulfen (Sakugabooru Animation Awards 2023) on Frieren: "There is something so graspable and tactile in a lot of the carefully depicted smaller motions. Every tiny, seemingly unimportant action suddenly becomes precious." In episode #04, an early wake-up for a village job is "an excellent opportunity for animators to compete to draw the cutest slob of an elf" (Sakuga Blog). On R6: give idles and emotes small, task-like actions with a full arc (our numbers). A sleepy slump settles in three stages (torso pitch 4, then 7, then 9 degrees), the head trailing by 2 to 4 frames. A shoulder stretch or a strap hitch gets its own anticipation, travel and settle of 3 to 10 degrees. `life` noise does not replace them (principles.md, "Moving holds and settles").
- **Small irregular secondary motions make a body feel alive.** Animétudes ("The rise of realism") on Mitsuo Iso's mobile suits in the opening of War in the Pocket: "a lot of little gestures and secondary movements which managed to create an impression of well-oiled machinery and life at the same time. The way the arm of the mecha recoils as it lands feels very natural". The same article credits Satoru Utsunomiya, on Windaria's battle scene, with a "sensibility for small, irregular movements". On R6: on a landing or a stop, let the arms recoil and settle, each on its own timing (our reading). Use different spring presets per arm (`follow` on one, `drag` on the other), or hand keys where one arm overshoots 4 to 8 degrees and returns over 5 to 8 frames and the other moves 2 frames later and smaller (our numbers). Vary these small motions between clips so no two repeat exactly.
- **Trembling hands can carry fear and resolve at once.** Sakuga Blog on Frieren #06: the match cut between Stark's trembling hands and his master's shows he inherited "the philosophy to embrace fear as a necessary aspect when facing a battle". Another Sakuga Blog piece notes that his hands "convey the resolve and fear he shares with his master". On R6: R6 has no fingers, so tremble the whole arm. Key alternating small offsets on its lift and side every 2 to 3 frames, or add them in `post`, ramped in and out as principles.md, "Moving holds and settles", describes. Stopping the tremble on a decision beat (a step forward, a grip on the weapon) turns the stillness into the acting beat (our reading); the hold that follows still drifts.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Lip-sync and mouth shapes (Frieren #09's lip-sync; three mouth shapes alternated by the length of the line, Ritsumeikan paper): R6 has no face rig; a face decal swap is runtime code outside Poser.
- Blinks drawn as three eye shapes, open, closed, half, open (Ritsumeikan paper): only as a face decal swap, which needs new runtime code; keyframe-workflow.md, "Acting", says when a blink belongs.
- Loops of hair, and fluttering hair that links cuts (Frieren #09): a stock R6 rig has no hair joints; it needs a rigged accessory (custom-rigs.md).
- Fabric moved by personal mannerisms (Akiko Takase's corrections on Frieren): Poser has no cloth.
- Faces that melt for comedy (Bocchi the Rock!): no face geometry.
- Distorted outlines and limbs smeared under speed (Nagata, Animétudes): parts cannot scale; use trails (combat.md, "Smears and trails").
- Shading folds that merge with smears (Hashimoto, Animétudes): rendering on drawings, not motion.
- Multi-perspective drawing and space twisted for emphasis (Yoshida; Tsurune's assistant CG director Mitsuki Takaki): a 3D view has one perspective; only a cutscene camera can cheat.
- Cutting from one fight to another to connect them, an explosion in one and hair in the other (Frieren #09): editing; the player owns the gameplay camera, and SKILL.md excludes cutscenes unless asked.
- Drawn effect forms (fire, water, lightning; Yoshida): VFX, not body motion; out of scope unless asked.
- Trembling fingers and signing hands (Yubisaki to Renren): no fingers; the arm-level tremble above is the substitute.
