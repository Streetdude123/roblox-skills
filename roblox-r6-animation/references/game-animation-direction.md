# Game animation direction

How lead game animators direct a character's whole animation set rather than one clip: when response wins over weight and for which actions, which clips a set needs and how they join, how one weight and one style hold across the set, what reads from the gameplay camera, how animation, design, effects and sound share one clock, and where the time goes under a deadline. Each finding is translated to R6 with Poser. Every finding was checked against its source ([sources.md](sources.md), "Game animation direction research"). Frame counts are at 60 fps unless a source states another rate; the conversions are ours.

## Responsiveness against weight

- **Reaching the new action fast matters more than natural motion.** Normoyle and Jörg (Motion in Games 2014) tested player controllers in a simple platform game. "Switch" changed motion in 0.02 to 0.03 s. "Blend" crossfaded over 10 animation frames at 30 fps (0.31 to 0.37 s per transition). "MoveTree", built on captured turns, took 1.3 s from stand to jog and 1.41 s for a 90 degree turn; its other transitions took 0.1 to 0.24 s. The least responsive controller hurt players' performance and their sense of control: Switch players reached the goal in significantly less time and lost significantly fewer lives than MoveTree players. Viewers outside the game rated MoveTree the most natural, yet players did not report better motion quality with it. The authors write that their results "strongly imply that responsiveness should always be prioritized over naturalness", while the animation still changes enjoyment. Scaling the turn clips' playback speed, or allowing transitions from transitions, would have made MoveTree more responsive but less realistic. On R6: show the new action within a few frames of the press. Poser's `play` defaults `fadeIn` to 0.12 s; the caller computes distance / 1000 deg/s, clamps it to 0.08 to 0.2 s and passes it (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). Either fade is shorter than the study's crossfade, and the inertial blend moves the new clip from its first frame. Never make the player wait for a start, turn or stop clip to end. A new `Rig:play` may replace a clip mid-blend, because the fade starts from the pose the rig last wrote. When a start or turn clip feels slow, raise its `speed` in `play` before you cut frames.

- **Rank every action by how fast it must answer.** Stellar Blade's director Kim Hyung-tae told 4Gamer (reported by VGC, April 2024) that about 0.5 s passes between the press and the skill showing on screen. The skill activates on the press, but the movement follows, because an instant movement "would not fit the game's style of animation". The day-one patch, according to the game's official X account, fixes "the input time for basic attacks, as well as the timing of blocking and parrying to be more responsive". Jonathan Cooper (gameanim.com, "Basics: Fast vs Slow", 2005) puts the player character's feel before the look of its animations. He lists Devil May Cry's instant control reactions and Resident Evil's deliberate unresponsiveness as successful fast and slow approaches. Control "should be tempered by an appropriate dose of unresponsiveness within the context of the project". Richard Lico (Bungie, Destiny; gameanim.com interview, 2017): reactive gameplay needs an anticipation that "is mostly just a pose with a short blend duration". A game punch is more about its follow-through, because "'What happened' tells the story of your previous button press". The Aztez blog (2010) counts 4 to 6 frames of anticipation on average for Bayonetta's opening attacks, before 3 to 30 frames of attack (frame rate not stated). On R6: sort the set into tiers before keying. Block, parry, dodge, dash and M1 answer first, with a readable pose within 1 to 4 frames (our starting values). Start such a clip from a first key equal to the idle or to the previous clip's end pose, with `fadeIn` near 0 (stylized-and-roblox.md, "The Animator: fades, priorities and weights"). The pose-distance fade of 0.08 to 0.2 s in pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract", is for a clip whose first key differs from the pose on screen. Heavies and skills may load longer (combat.md, "Frame budgets: startup, active, recovery"). Only ultimates, emotes and finishers hold the player (our choice). Put the weight after the press (principles.md, "Game feel: anticipation, contact, hitstop, recovery, cancel").

- **Cancel once the action has done its job, and layer weight over the response.** Peyman Massoudi (Game Developer blog, 2015, on Shadow Blade: Reload) writes that "when you want to have responsiveness, you have to kill some naturalness". His example grapple "can do its job in its 50% of length". Players cancel attacks and grapples once the enemies are dead, so every cancellable animation gets a safe blend interval from which run, jump, dash or other attacks may cancel it. A landing never interrupts: the fall blends straight into the run or an attack, while an additive land animation shows the momentum in the upper body (the standing idle has its own land animation). An additive "windmill" on the spine and hands plays when the character stops and starts running in quick succession. On R6: combat.md, "Cancels and the first actionable frame", already places the `cancel` event and keeps the settle after it; in Massoudi's case it sits near half the clip, once the move's job is done. Poser plays one clip per rig, so an additive landing over the run needs a composite clip or new runtime code that adds an offset clip to the playing one. In the composite (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"), the land's torso dip and pitch go in as an offset bent at the waist, the arms come from the land under a weight envelope, and the legs stay on the run.

## Designing the animation set

- **Plan the set from the states that overlap, not from a move list.** Lico (gameanim.com, 2017) on Destiny: the third-person player animations had to look good "while simultaneously aiming up 90 degrees, crouch-running diagonally back & left at a speed in between run & walk, blending out of a grenade throw, while firing, and starting a melee. All while remaining instantly responsive, yet fluid in motion". It took years and "lots of intelligent micro-solutions from numerous team members". On R6: list every pair of states the place lets overlap (M1 while walking, block while strafing, a skill during a dash, an emote cut by a hit). For each pair, decide which clip owns which joints. Build the pair as a composite (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays") and test the pairs in Play, not the clips alone. A pair that cannot be composed on R6 gets its own moving variant: an attack that twists RootJoint swings the walking legs (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Level of detail and masks").

- **Inertia decides the turn, stop and lean clips.** Cooper ("Basics: Making Character-Driving Fun", gameanim.com, 2005) separates desired speed from actual speed. Inertia delays the full run after a full stick push and the stop after a release. That makes room for a "turn 180", a "stop" and a "run-lean" animation. Deceleration "will typically be required to be faster than acceleration", because "players do not want to die running off cliffs". A turn-rate limit in degrees per second gives the feel of steering, and a run-lean animation blended by turn rate keeps control with the animator. Ninja Gaiden and Devil May Cry "would suffer greatly" from a disproportionate amount of inertia, while the Mario series "thrives on it". His Mario 64 case study (2005) credits that game with the original 90 and 180 degree turns and turn-leaning. In his opinion Mario Sunshine, with an immediate 180 degree turn and no loss of speed, "suffered all the more for it". On R6: read the place's movement code first (WalkSpeed, any acceleration or turn-rate code). With near-instant control, turns and stops are short clips that never hold the root, such as the one-frame facing snap covered by a turn clip (locomotion.md, "Starts, stops and turns"). With acceleration or a turn-rate limit, build a 180 degree turn clip, a braking stop and a lean that a procedural joint reads from the turn rate in `ctx` (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"; the controller must add this field to `ctx`: new controller code). Match the stop clip to the place's deceleration, which Cooper sets faster than its acceleration.

- **The camera's facing mode decides the locomotion clips.** In Roblox's player scripts (BaseCamera, Roblox-Client-Tracker), first person and mouse lock (shift lock) set `Enum.RotationType.CameraRelative`. The creator-docs say that with it the body "continuously aligns with the camera's look direction"; with the default `MovementRelative` the body turns to face its movement. On R6: a place that allows shift lock or forces camera-relative facing shows strafes and backpedals all the time. The R6 Animate script plays its one walk clip at every speed above 0.01 and has no strafe clip (r6-craft.md, "Locomotion, idle and emote sets"). A sideways move then plays the forward walk while the body travels sideways (our inference). There, build left and right strafe cycles and a backpedal (locomotion.md, "Keeping feet planted across speeds and directions"). The place's own locomotion controller picks them from the move direction against the facing; the default Animate script cannot. A place with only movement-relative facing spends that effort on turn and stop clips instead.

- **The idle stays calm against the run, and a battle idle stays neutral in a melee set.** Lico (80.lv, 2017): a run "requires a large amount of noisy, repetitive motion. So making the idle calm and subtle helps to balance the rhythm". Idle variants "that have a clear, discernible action" add contrast to the subtle idle. Blizzard's Overwatch 2 animation team ("Weekly Recall: Animation Station") writes that most heroes "are poised to attack in their idle pose" (Cassidy with Peacekeeper at the ready, Reaper waving his shotguns). The personality an idle carries (Gibson's talk on Overwatch) and Wuyang's staff held to the side are in polish-and-critique.md, "Line of action, silhouette and reversals". On R6: stylized-and-roblox.md, "Rhythm, idle and readability in action games", keeps the idle neutral, never aggressive, because every move starts and ends in it. That rule holds for a melee set; a poised idle suits a ranged weapon that fires from the idle pose, as Cassidy's and Reaper's do (our reading). In a place with a combat state, the neutral idle (hubs, lobbies) carries the personality. The battle idle (weapon out, or R6's toolnone, r6-craft.md, "Locomotion, idle and emote sets") stays calm and low in amplitude (principles.md, "Idle or held stance", has the breath and lean sizes). Keep the battle idle's largest joint difference to every move's first key under about 80 degrees, so the fade the caller sizes from the pose distance and passes (distance / 1000 deg/s, pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract") stays at its 0.08 s floor. Measure it with `Poser.posesAt` and pull any outlier toward the idle.

## Weight class and character identity

- **Give each character an archetype and fix its timing rules early.** Cooper ("Basics: Fast vs Slow"): games build on three archetypes, "Slow but strong, fast but weak, and an average of the two", and speed attributes are "directly affected by the animation". Combat moves split into anticipation, apex and recoil for balancing. Anticipation is generally longer for NPCs, to give the player time to react, and fast for the player character. "It is a good idea to create a set of timing 'rules' early in the prototyping phase, ie. average PC punch or kick anticipation, around which additional animations can be balanced as fast or slow". Sakurai (GDC 2008, in Cooper's notes) found that turning each move's four stages into numbers "greatly aided the designers". On R6: body-types-and-scale.md, "One weight class across a clip set", writes the build into the clip plan before keying. Add the timing rules to that table. Starting values inside the battlegrounds M1 range (contacts on frames 10 to 14, r6-craft.md, "How battlegrounds sets are built and timed"): a light class makes contact on frame 10, with the first actionable frame (`cancel`) 8 frames later; medium on 12, cancel 10 to 12 later; heavy on 14, cancel 14 to 16 later (our starting values). The chain window still opens at the contact (combat.md, "Cancels and the first actionable frame"). Store each clip's contact and cancel as `events` and let the game config read them (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays").

- **Weight is part of the character and must agree everywhere.** Sakurai (in Cooper's notes): parameters, motions and "character essence" must all be consistent, and they are the most important elements of all; the weight of Mario and Samus makes them fall differently. On R6: a class's stats and its clips tell the same story. If the place gives a heavy class a lower jump or a faster fall, its jump and land clips use the heavier landing tier for the same drop (principles.md, "Locomotion controller"), and its hit reactions and knockback travel less (our reading). Mass in the motion itself (slow to start and stop, smooth arcs) is in body-types-and-scale.md, "Giants and small bodies: mass, momentum and detail".

- **Contrast the player's speed with the enemies'.** Massoudi: the player character Kuro is very agile, but the enemies' "animations have much more frames". Slower enemy animations help "to get the focus out from the player" while the eye settles on one enemy. On R6: give player clips the light or medium class and NPC grunts the heavy class with longer telegraphs (combat.md, "Telegraphs and reaction time"; Cooper's longer NPC anticipation above). Rank the clips by peak joint speed across the player and NPC sets together (anime-craft.md, "Contrast across a scene"). The player's attacks sit at the top.

- **A move's shape follows the character's role, not the move's type.** Blizzard's Overwatch 2 animation team works with the hero's strike team, "especially our narrative designers", on "what kind of movements would fit that Hero's history and personality": "if we put Soldier: 76's well-trained, stiff gestures on a Hero like Mercy, it wouldn't make sense". For Wuyang's slam, "the dramatic over-the-head pose Reinhardt takes also didn't fit Wuyang's agile style". The team "added a spin into the third-person animation to reflect Wuyang's swift battle style and his support Hero mindset": Reinhardt "wouldn't care about quickly repositioning to avoid another hit", while Wuyang "would likely focus on casting an ability quickly and dodging a retaliatory attack". A rough draft borrowed from other heroes goes to playtests first; "If the strike team isn't feeling it, we go back to the drawing board". On R6: give each class its own version of a shared move type. polish-and-critique.md, "Contact, weight and impact", has the heavy weapon that stays down and the light one that bounces. A heavy class's slam raises the weapon arm straight over its shoulder, as high as R6 allows (SKILL.md, "Overhead reach": no two-handed grip above the head). A light or support class's slam arcs over the shoulder and turns its follow-through into a 360 degree spin that carries the body a step back and ends facing the target, ready to dodge. Key the spin as a RootJoint yaw over 24 to 30 frames on an ease-out spline, peaking at 20 to 25 degrees a frame (within the 25 degrees per frame retime cap in SKILL.md, "Measure before you look"), and drive the step from a root travel curve (pipeline.md, "Networked actions, hits and knockback") (our starting values). The rigid legs turn with the torso, so either pivot the feet on `Feet.flat` yaw targets for the whole spin (SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", toe yaw), or let them leave the floor in one hop of 0.15 to 0.25 studs (the hop size in SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", no toes) that lasts the fastest 8 to 12 frames, with the COM on a parabola (SKILL.md, "Rotate about the COM"). In an upright pose the COM lies on the torso's vertical axis, 0.64 studs below its centre, so a pure yaw leaves it in place (our derivation from SKILL.md, "R6 rules that bite", centre of mass).

- **The walk is part of the character's identity.** Inven Global's report on Matt Boehm's GDC 2017 talk on Overwatch's first-person animation: a close look showed that "each character walks differently", adjusted "to better convey the character's personality and physical traits". Genji "walks at a faster pace than other heroes, but his upper body remains calm and unshaken"; Junkrat "constantly limps" because his right leg is artificial. Riot's clarity article (2021): Lee Sin's generic shape means "a lot of his silhouette is carried by his animations", and Ivern's "goofy walk also adds to his recognizability", although animation is ideally "more of a bonus differentiator rather than a main one". On R6: every stock R6 body is the same six blocks, so animation carries more of the identity than Riot wants in League (our reading), and the walk carries much of it. Change at least one measurable channel per character: cadence, torso bob, torso roll, arm swing range or lean (locomotion.md, "Mood in a walk"). Put the difference into the contact and passing poses (acting-and-performance.md, "Performance in games"). A Genji-like walk takes a quick cadence under a quiet torso: a small bob and roll, a small arm swing, the head level, low `life`. A limp shortens the hurt leg's stance (body-types-and-scale.md, "Fatigue and limps").

## One style across the set

- **Write a style sheet for each character.** Cooper's notes on Andy Schmidt's Pixar talk (Ratatouille, gameanim.com, 2007): each character was given "a signature movement style to differentiate themselves from one another". "Pose character sheets were devised to illustrate various correct-vs-incorrect methods for animating the characters – essentially style sheets setting a brand bible for each character". Cooper ("The Game Animation Test", 2016): new hires "are required to be consistent with the rest of the team's animation". That covers how far a studio exaggerates mocap or keeps it ultra realistic, and whether keyed poses are "popping and jumping between them or keeping everything fluid with lots of overlap"; "Much of the style comes from time-constraints". On R6: keyframe-workflow.md, "Plan before keying", sets one style dial for all of a character's clips. Make the sheet numbers plus captures: the golden poses as joint values, the push level, how far a moving hold drifts, the `lag` and `springs` presets per joint, the `life` level, and the peak joint speed of ordinary moves against signature moves. Add two `rear34` captures per pose, one right and one wrong. Check every new clip against the sheet before review.

- **Compare poses across the set from one fixed side view.** Sakurai showed each attack's four stages to his animation and design team with side-on photographs of poseable "Microman" figures. He dictated every pose but said the final quality was down to the animator. Enforcing the poses this way reduced the number of animation changes for balancing. On R6: lay out the stage poses of every attack with `_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "side"})` (pipeline.md, "Source transfer and review tools"; the facings are listed in scripts/EditStrip.lua) at the same spacing, one strip per clip, and compare the strips side by side. Two attacks whose strips look alike will read as one move (our reading of principles.md, "Game feel: anticipation, contact, hitstop, recovery, cancel": two moves that share one clip read as the same move).

- **Check the whole set by script, not clip by clip.** JC Delannoy (EA software engineer, gameanim.com reprint, 2016) runs data verification and fixup scripts over a subset or all of the animation assets. He advises fixing data problems "proactively and thoroughly"; fixing them as they come up becomes a complex game of "whack-a-mole". On R6: keep one Studio script that loops over the set and prints, per clip, `Poser.check(clip).text`, the `_G.feet` numbers and the peak joint speed. Add the largest joint difference between the clip's first and last pose and the idle (from `Poser.posesAt`). Re-run it after every batch of changes. The script is new code; the helpers exist.

## Readability from the gameplay camera

- **Know what the default camera shows.** In Roblox's player scripts, BaseCamera starts 12.5 studs from the subject (clamped to the player's zoom limits), aims 1.5 studs above an R6 HumanoidRootPart and limits pitch to 80 degrees up or down. `Camera.FieldOfView` is vertical and defaults to 70 (creator-docs). In shift lock, MouseLockController's offset `Vector3.new(1.75, 0, 0)` is applied along the camera's right vector (ClassicCamera). Our derivation: at 12.5 studs the view is 17.5 studs tall, so the 5-stud R6 body fills about 29% of the screen height, about 62 pixels per stud on a 1080-pixel screen. Shift lock sees the back from about 8 degrees to its right. A target 40 studs ahead is about 52.5 studs from the camera: about 73 pixels tall, 15 pixels per stud. On R6: at combat range only whole-body shapes read: torso lean and twist, an arm clear of the torso outline, the leg split. A 10 degree head turn moves a point half a stud from the neck axis about 0.09 studs: about 5 pixels at 12.5 studs and about 1 at 40 (our derivation). From behind, a thrust along the facing reads only as the arm getting shorter. Give strikes an arc across the screen (combat.md, "Readability from a pulled-back camera"), and give the load a shoulder-line change of 30 degrees or more (our starting value). Capture at 12.5 studs in Play and at 40 studs before calling a move readable.

- **A pose needs 10 to 16 frames to be seen.** Lico (80.lv, 2017): "The way I approach readability is to balance the amount of motion with how extreme my posing needs to be". It takes "the average person 5 to 8 frames at 30fps to really 'see' a pose", but people "can easily feel a subtle motion consisting of as little as 2 frames". For responsive, overt posing such as Destiny's supers he keeps the actions quick and smooth, "while 2 or 3 strong key poses leverage a moving hold with a jitter for emphasis". On R6: at 60 fps, a pose the player must recognise (a telegraph, a finisher's golden pose, a taunt) holds 10 to 16 frames as a moving hold. The golden pose holds longer than the clip's hitstop: 10 to 16 frames, and at least hitstop + 4 frames (about 20) in a heavy finisher (our starting value). A motion that is only felt (the strike itself, a flinch) may last 4 frames, but other players may miss a read that rests on 1 or 2 frame keys (stylized-and-roblox.md, "Level of detail and masks"). A signature move gets 2 or 3 such held poses, each with a small shake on its hold. SKILL.md, method step 3, "Recoveries", holds a chainable move's over-extended pose 6 to 12 frames. Keep that where the player chains, because response matters more than recognition there, and use 10 to 16 where the pose must be recognised.

- **The pose shows identity and direction at a glance.** Riot (clarity article, 2021): "Silhouettes are the single most important thing for champion recognition in League". Silhouette size can hint at playstyle: "larger champions with bulky armor are probably tanks or fighters". And "it should be clear which direction a champion is facing from their silhouette alone. You should know whether someone is charging towards you or away at a glance". On R6: the parts never change size, so the stance carries what a bigger silhouette would. Give each class a stance it returns to. A heavy class stands wide and low: legs slid out to the feet-rule limit, the torso dropped so the legs slide up into it. A light class stands narrow and high. Make a charge read from the front: the torso pitched forward 15 to 20 degrees from the waist, the head held toward the target (our starting values). Check opponents' views with `_G.editStrip(clip, times, {facing = "front34"})` as well as `rear34`.

- **Attention follows importance, and the hit area follows the visual.** Riot: "the amount of attention an ability grabs should match its level of importance"; spells that do more damage "should draw more attention". Visual noise "adds up quickly and can make it difficult to react properly in teamfights". Abilities frustrate players when hitboxes "don't line up with their effects". Taric's stun is a good example: bright, with "an audio wind-up" and "a well-defined hitbox". "Projectiles should convey the direction they're traveling, even without motion". Cosmetic animations must not give a competitive advantage: Pool Party Renekton's model appears to move upward in his recall, but "his hitbox never changes". On R6: principles.md, "Game feel: anticipation, contact, hitstop, recovery, cancel", gives stronger moves a bigger wind-up silhouette. Rank the set by that rule: M1 smallest, skills larger, ultimates largest (Cooper, 2019, also ties longer anticipations to higher damage as risk against reward). At contact, put the striking fist or blade tip at the hitbox's edge: find its position by forward kinematics from `Poser.posesAt` at the contact time and compare it with the server's hitbox. A skin or emote variant keeps the original's event times and contact positions, and never carries the body away from the HumanoidRootPart.

- **An enemy attack has five phases with minimum lengths.** Jerry Zhang (Game Developer blog, 2022, on Dark Souls 3) splits an enemy attack into Opening Pose, Attack Signal, Attack, End Pose and Return. The opening pose's "silhouette should be discernable and naturally hints the incoming attack". Attack Signal and Attack together "need to have a guaranteed duration of at least 340ms", longer when the player meets a new move. An End Pose followed by the Return must last at least 170 ms "for the player to confirm the end of this attack"; an End Pose used as bait, at least 240 ms. He measured the game's input response at about 60 to 100 ms. On R6: in an NPC attack, give at least 21 frames from the start of the signal to the end of the active window. Hold the end pose at least 11 frames before the return, or 15 when it baits the player before a follow-up. combat.md, "Telegraphs and reaction time", asks 26 frames from clip start to contact for an attack defenders answer on reaction; Zhang's 340 ms is the floor for the signal and the attack alone.

- **A wind-up has a ceiling, and players see the hit pose every time a hit lands.** Sakurai (GDC 2008, Super Smash Bros. Brawl, in Cooper's notes) breaks every attack into four parts. StandBy is "essentially the first frame" (the idle pose, or the fall for mid-air attacks). WindUp "should never take longer than 0.75 seconds". Strike is "the strongest pose, seen often by players as the action pauses here when a hit lands". FollowThrough "must not look like the StandBy, and typically is the longest part of the move". The animations were exaggerated so the player had enough visual feedback after a key press and the opponent was warned to dodge. On R6: cap a gameplay wind-up at 45 frames. Hitstop holds the contact pose 8 to 16 frames (combat.md, "Hitstop"), so make the contact a complete pose that reads in silhouette. keyframe-workflow.md, "Plan before keying", names the load or the follow-through extreme as the golden pose, not the contact. Keep the longest moving hold on that golden pose, longer than the hitstop (at least hitstop + 4 frames, about 20 after a 16 frame hitstop; Readability from the gameplay camera, "A pose needs 10 to 16 frames to be seen"), and make the contact strong enough to survive the hitstop. anime-craft.md, "Contrast across a scene", gives a showcase move 60 to 120 frames of preparation. The 45-frame cap holds for attacks an opponent must answer; the long preparation suits moves that take control away (an ultimate cut-in, an emote). The follow-through rule is in combat.md, "Cancels and the first actionable frame" (the recovery must not look like the idle).

- **Design fixes the cast time; the animation fits inside it.** Rory Alderton (Riot, 2014, in Surrender at 20's collection of Riot posts): Yasuo's ultimate had a "one second - that's 30 animation frames – cast time", and "For gameplay purposes it was absolutely vital the animation did not exceed this time". Ultimates "need to be larger than life and highly readable". On R6: one second is 60 frames at Poser's rate. Take each skill's lock time from the design and fit the clip's locked part inside it. Set the release event so `Poser.realTime(clip, t)` equals the design time: retime with `warp` before the release and keep the last 0.12 s before it at rate 1 (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays"). Frames after the lock are cancellable filler (combat.md, "Cancels and the first actionable frame"). Poses that vanish behind effects are covered in acting-and-performance.md, "Silhouette, distance and the player camera".

## First and last frames

- **No vital pose before the blend has built up.** Delannoy: a clip that starts with its anticipation loses it under a long crossfade, because the screen still shows mostly the previous animation. Author assets "so that there aren't any vital poses in the animation until the current animation has reached a large enough weight in the blend". With a long blend into a baseball swing, do not begin the swing too early in the asset. On R6: Poser's inertial blend removes the offset on a smoothstep over `fadeIn`. Key a clip's first vital pose (the anticipation extreme, the first step's push) at or after `fadeIn`: frame 5 for 0.08 s, frame 12 for 0.2 s. When a player move must load in 1 to 3 frames, make its first key equal the idle or the previous clip's end pose and play it with `fadeIn` near 0 (stylized-and-roblox.md, "The Animator: fades, priorities and weights").

- **A blend needs the outgoing motion to keep moving.** Delannoy: for a smooth transition "it is essential for the outgoing/slave animation to still update and move according to its momentum for the duration of the blend". If the outgoing motion runs out first, the blend uses "a static pose with no defined velocity", often read as zero speed, and the blended speed can jump. On R6: Poser's `inertial` and `cross` blends both start from the pose the rig last wrote, and neither carries the old clip's velocity (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). Make the incoming clip's first 4 to 6 frames continue the outgoing motion's direction and speed (our starting values). That also keeps the pose distance small, so the fade the caller sizes from it and passes stays near its 0.08 s floor. This continuation applies to blends into locomotion and other non-urgent clips; for block, parry, dodge, dash and M1 the response rule wins (Responsiveness against weight, "Rank every action by how fast it must answer"). A long blend that keeps sampling the outgoing clip needs new runtime code.

- **Start a moving attack from the run's own frames.** Massoudi: for run attacks "the animators have concatenated one loop of run before it and created the run attack just after that". The speed blend then works, and the run's speed and responsiveness carry into the attack. On R6: author a run attack with one run cycle copied at its head and the attack keyed after it. Play it with `startAt` set to the running clip's current phase time and `fadeIn = 0`, so its first frame equals the frame on screen. Keep the root moving at run speed until the strike's braced plant.

- **Add travel by scaling the speed curve, never by an even offset.** Delannoy: when an animation must cover 20% more distance, dividing the extra motion evenly over every frame keeps the character moving even in its idle pose at the end. Scaling the speed graph up by 20% speeds only the moving portions and keeps "zero velocity" where the animation rests; extra motion on still segments creates foot sliding. On R6: when a dash or lunge must cover more studs, scale the root travel curve that the place's mover reads (pipeline.md, "Networked actions, hits and knockback", root travel from the clip) by the ratio of the distances. Never add a constant per frame. The end hold keeps 0 studs/s, and `_G.feet` must still show slide 0.05 or less (SKILL.md, method step 4).

## One clock for animation, design, effects and sound

- **Animation sets the timing in the game; sound and effects lay over it.** Jon Mummery (animation lead, Perfect Dark Zero, Rare, 2006 interview): the animator "has to be able to communicate with everyone from programmers, artists, audio, particles, and the concept guys on a daily basis". Designers need animations "to be a certain length or move a certain distance", and this "generally goes back and forward until we settle on something mutual". For a weapon he animated the shooting and reloads first and put the asset in the game. "Once happy with how a weapon feels in-game and its position on screen", he sent a movie of the animation to the audio department to add their sounds on top. He could also edit the game's xml files to tweak animation speeds. On R6: settle each clip's length, travel and `events` with design in the place first. Then freeze the event table (contact, cancel, step, effect cues) and hand sound and effects a capture plus the real event times, from the one set of event times in pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays". Any later retime re-sends both.

- **Key poses on the beat when the game has a tempo.** Tango Gameworks (Hi-Fi Rush, CEDEC 2023 talk by John Johanas and Masaaki Yamada, in a gameanim.com summary): the game "was animated for 120BPM (beats-per-minute) at 60 frames per second and dynamically modified thereafter. Key poses hit on the beat". All elements "were matched to the BPM and could be sped up and down in sync". On R6: at 120 BPM a beat is 30 frames. Put key poses on frames 0, 30 and 60 (15-frame steps on eighth notes), and play at `speed = bpm / 120` to follow another tempo. Keys, lag and `events` stay in clip time, so effects scheduled from the clip clock move with the speed. Use this for emotes and rhythm moves. For music without a fixed tempo, place the beats on loudness peaks (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays").

- **Each client fires a move's effects from its own copy of the clip.** A DevForum developer of the Roblox combat game Combat Legends (2026) describes a client to server to all clients pattern. The server checks that the attack is allowed, then fires to every player. Each client plays the animation on the attacker and listens "to the animation events on said animation to display visuals and sfx". That keeps combat smooth "even if the attacker is laggy". In a 2020 thread, effects fired by a remote at the attacker's marker appeared on other clients before the animation reached that marker; marker signals did fire on observers' copies of another player's track. In a 2024 thread a reply recalls that the events do not fire if the animation fails to load. Reviewers of a 2022 combat demo saw particles appear before the animation played. On R6: each observer already plays the clip locally (pipeline.md, "Locomotion and overlays": Motor6D.Transform is not replicated). Drive each client's effects and sounds from that client's own clip clock crossing the clip's `events` times, never from the arrival of the attacker's remote. Poser has no marker dispatcher, so the place's scheduler detects the crossings (same section). For baked native tracks, preload every Animation at spawn (pipeline.md, "Native Animator playback"), since their markers cannot fire before the asset loads.

## Blocking in the place and reviewing in play

- **Rough the whole set into the place before polishing any clip.** Cooper ("The 12 Principles of Animation (In Video Games)", gameanim.com, 2019): keeping work "in a pose-to-pose or unfinished state as long as possible" wastes less and lets the animator rough out more animations in the same time; "many animations blending together creates a better and more fluid game character than a single beautifully animated animation". Jay Hosfelt (Epic Games, GDC 2014, in a gameanim.com summary) covered quick "gray box" animation assets that give designers quick iteration cycles and let animators take part in early prototyping. Mummery: animators sometimes animated temporary models they made themselves before the final model was finished. On R6: polish-and-critique.md, "Game polish: priorities, blends and loops", already orders the work by screen time and puts every move in the game as a placeholder first. keyframe-workflow.md, "Block in whole poses", gives the stepped blocking. Add one rule: keep the clip names and the `events` names fixed while the keys change, so the gameplay code never changes with the animation.

- **Review from the angles the animation was not made for.** Inven Global's report on Boehm's talk: the audience saw Overwatch's first-person animations from a third-person point of view "to discover the things they were not aware of". In Reinhardt's second melee attack his right hand went through his left hand "as if it were a ghost". Winston's left arm, which he uses to walk and jump around along with his two legs, never touches the ground in that view: he "actually walks only with his two legs". On R6: the owner sees the back from 12.5 studs, while opponents see the front and sides at any distance. A choice made for the owner's camera (a blade turned flat to it, combat.md, "Readability from a pulled-back camera") also shows from there. Capture `front` and `front34` strips at the contact and the recovery of every combat clip, and look for limbs through each other or through the torso.

- **Expect the first clips of a new character to be wrong.** Lico (80.lv, 2017): "my first animations with a new character rarely feel right". He adjusts as he spends more time with the character, and lots of feedback helps. Mikhail Vershinin (Game Developer blog, 2024): in many companies with streamlined production, a piece of work that gets feedback more than four times passes to another person. On R6: block the character's idle, walk and M1 first (the screen-time order in polish-and-critique.md, "Game polish: priorities, blends and loops"). Review them in Play, and rework them once the rest of the set exists. Track the review rounds per clip in the clip plan. A clip on its fifth round needs a new reference or a new golden pose, not a sixth tweak (our adaptation).

## Polish under a deadline

- **Spend the time on poses; beautiful motion alone does not make the game.** Vershinin: create the poses on stepped interpolation with hair and clothing turned off, then add breakdowns ("Still no interpolation!"). Start interpolating only when the art manager or lead animator has approved all poses, and add the overlaps on hair and clothing last; "good preparation accounts for 60-70 percent of the work". "Too beautiful animation is not a guarantee of gameplay success or a game feature. Everything must be balanced." On R6: keyframe-workflow.md, "Block in whole poses" and "Layer by layer", already gives this order. Under a deadline the poses, the timing and the `events` come first. In Poser the motion layers are cheap: `lag`, the `springs` presets and `life` are one field each (pipeline.md, "Poser clip contract"). So cut extra rounds of arc and offset polish on secondary parts (keyframe-workflow.md, "Polish: arcs, offsets, retiming"), never those layers, but every clip still passes SKILL.md's unison and arc checks on the leading part (our reading), and still spline every gameplay clip (SKILL.md, "Smooth in gameplay by default").

- **Secondary motion is cheap, but test that it helps.** Cooper (2019): follow-through, overlap and drag on cloth or soft parts done procedurally in the engine are "a fantastic way to increase the quality of a character's animation with little extra work, not least because their motion will continue whatever the following animation the player performs". Massoudi: secondary motion such as cloth simulation helps where responsiveness lowers naturalness; Kuro's scarf "helps us showing more acceptable motions". Normoyle and Jörg found the opposite in one test: a controller identical to Blend except for a mass-spring cape was enjoyed significantly less, and the authors "do not have insights into why". On R6: put springs on loose parts that have their own joints (capes, hair, tails, chains; principles.md, "Follow-through and springs") and play-test the character with and without them. Poser's spring state resets on `play`, so a loose part does not carry its swing from one clip into the next, unlike Cooper's engine-side drag. Carrying it needs new runtime code.

## Mistakes leads see most

- **The mistakes leads name most.** Vershinin: animators "immediately working with interpolation, merging the blocking and spline stages". Lico: "It's way too easy to craft poses/actions that defy physics and anatomy". Gibson (in Eva Sokolova's notes on his GDC talk): "total symmetry looks stiff". Delannoy: extra motion spread over still frames, and vital poses placed so early that the blend hides them. Cooper (2019): "A common mistake most junior animators make is to have all elements of a character start or arrive at the same time". A DevForum reviewer of a combat clip (2022): the character "recovers from punching as if it was using as little force as possible". On R6: each has a check already. Block in steps first (keyframe-workflow.md, "From stepped to spline"). Keep the COM over the support (SKILL.md, method step 4). Break twins (principles.md, "Poses first: the posing checklist"). Spread the stops (`Poser.check` unison). Show the force in an over-extended recovery (principles.md, "Game feel: anticipation, contact, hitstop, recovery, cancel"). The two blend mistakes are in "First and last frames" above.

## Stylised and realistic direction

- **A realistic project dials the exaggeration down, not off.** GameRevolution on Bruno Velasquez's GDC 2019 talk (animation director, Sony Santa Monica): 90 percent of the old God of War games were animated by hand, and the remaining 10 percent were motion captured in cinematics. Grounding Kratos inspired the team to look more into motion capture. His old "comic book, over the top" proportions "worked for the gameplay and the far away camera angles", but not once those were no longer the focus. Velasquez admits the new Kratos is still over the top, but "a little bit more grounded than it used to be before". His walk is a "dynamic, strong, and grounded" jaunt that shows his power without overexaggerating it. In the article's words, kills still had to be brutal but not overly so, and the returning dual swords were reined in so Kratos did not jump around as much with them. On R6: a grounded R6 game (horror, military, a slow melee duel) pushes poses at the low end of SKILL.md's 10 to 20% (about 10%). It keeps attacks on the ground, takes timings from human-motion-data.md and keeps one flourish per signature move. A battlegrounds set pushes to 20% and lets a signature move break physics after the real scale is set (anime-craft.md, "Contrast across a scene").

- **Stylised work keeps real body mechanics under the exaggeration.** Lico on Polyarc's Moss (gameanim.com, 2017): he aims for "all that texture and accuracy of proper body mechanics", mixed with "the style of classic Disney". Disney's 9 Old Men pushed exaggeration "while also showing exquisite detail like how a hip reacts when the foot plants". He merges realism and exaggeration instead of choosing one. On R6: in a stylised set, push the poses and the timing contrast, but keep the mechanics checks unchanged: the COM over the support, planted soles flat, a torso drop when a foot takes the weight (body-mechanics.md). The exaggeration lives in the poses and the spacing, never in a body that floats.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Stretched limbs and blades (Inven's report on Boehm's talk: Genji's Dragonblade stretches "like rubber", Zenyatta's leg stretches to "an abnormal length", McCree stretches his arm out to fire): R6 parts cannot scale, and sliding a limb out past torso contact breaks the feet and arm rule. The nearest substitute is a piston along the limb within the contact limit (r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons").
- Root squash under 20% (Vershinin): R6 parts cannot scale; a compact pose stands in for squash (SKILL.md, the principles table, "Squash and stretch").
- Eyelid animation (Vershinin's "crazy eyes"): R6 has no face rig by default.
- First-person reload rules (Boehm: keep reloads clear of the player's sight around the reticle; Soldier: 76's reload moved to the left hand): they govern first-person view arms, which an R6 body does not have. They apply only to a viewmodel the place builds.
- Motion matching (Naughty Dog's switch for The Last of Us Part II, GDC 2021): Poser plays authored clips and has no matching runtime. The talk's 3.8 GB slides were not read.
- Joystick input stabilisation (Delannoy): input code, not animation; it belongs to the place's control scripts.
