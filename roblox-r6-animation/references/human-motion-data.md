# Human motion data: gait, running, strikes and jumps

Measured human joint angles, timings, speeds and weight shifts for walks, runs, starts, stops, turns, punches, throws, kicks and jumps, each translated through the six R6 parts into Poser keys. Real data is the target for timing, weight and balance; the translation decides what reads on rigid blocks. Every finding was checked against its source ([sources.md](sources.md), "Human motion data: gait, running, strikes and jumps research"). Human-to-R6 scaling uses 5 studs for a 1.75 m body (2.86 studs per metre), as [locomotion.md](locomotion.md) does; numbers marked "our derivation" are computed here from the sourced ones. `p` x, `p` y and `p` z are the components of a key's `p` offset ([pipeline.md](pipeline.md), "Poser clip contract").

The knee rule used throughout: an R6 leg is 2 studs from hip to sole. A human leg with equal thigh and shank, folded at knee angle k, has a hip-to-foot chord that reads on R6 as a whole-leg `lift` of (hip angle - k/2) and a slide s up into the torso of 2(1 - cos(k/2)) studs (our derivation: 0.03 studs at 20 degrees, 0.12 at 40, 0.27 at 60, 0.59 at 90, 1.0 at 120). The slide runs along the leg's own axis: Poser's `p` is in the torso's axes, so `p = s * (R * Vector3.new(0, 1, 0))` with R the leg's rotation (for a pure `lift` a in radians, `Vector3.new(0, s * math.cos(a), s * math.sin(a))`). It equals `p` y only for a near-vertical leg; on a leg swung far forward a `p` y offset moves the leg across its length instead. Use the chord on stance and contact frames, where the sole must land where the human foot lands (`Feet.flat`), and through a walk's swing, whose slide is the knee cue (r6-mechanics.md, "Walk and run legs"). For a lifted or airborne leg whose knee must read (a knee strike, a tuck, the front swing of a run), give the leg the shin's angle (hip angle - k) instead (r6-craft.md, "Faking joints with slides and pistons"). Fighting-game frame budgets stay in [combat.md](combat.md); the walk and run recipes stay in [principles.md](principles.md).

## Walking: the legs through the cycle

- **The leg swings further back than forward, because the knee folds at push-off.** Brunnekreef et al. (2005, Table 3) tabulate normal sagittal angles by phase of the gait cycle. The table splits late stance (30 to 60%) for the hip only and gives the knee one value for all of it. On R6, with the chord of the knee rule above (our derivation; positive `lift` is forward):

  | Phase (% of the cycle) | Hip | Knee | R6 `lift` | R6 slide (studs) |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 0 to 10 (early stance) | 25 flexed | 20 flexed | +15 | 0.03 |
  | 10 to 30 (midstance) | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
  | 30 to 50 (late stance) | 10 extended | 40 flexed | -30 | 0.12 |
  | 50 to 60 (late stance) | 0 | 40 flexed | -20 | 0.12 |
  | 60 to 70 (early swing) | 15 flexed | 60 flexed | -15 | 0.27 |
  | 70 to 100 (late swing) | 25 flexed | 0 | +25 | 0 |

  In a 60-frame (1.0 s) cycle the percentages are frame numbers. Key only the swing leg's `lift` and slide on those phases; the stance values are what `Feet.flat` should give from the foot track, so use them as a check. The thigh never goes more than 10 degrees back, but the R6 leg must go 20 to 30, which is why hand-keyed legs swing further back than forward (principles.md, Walk).
- **Show the swing-knee bend.** In the same table the knee bends 60 degrees in early swing (60 to 70% of the cycle) and is straight again in late swing. On R6, slide the swing leg up about 0.27 studs (`p` y), peaking at frames 36 to 42 of a 60-frame cycle, and back to full length before contact. The R6 references measured 0.32 to 0.37 (principles.md, Walk; r6-mechanics.md, Walk and run legs), a little past the human value, in line with pushing poses past natural (SKILL.md, Exaggeration). The 20 degree stance bend after contact does not transfer (see below).
- **Slowing a walk shrinks the knee more than the hip.** The meta-analysis of Fukuchi, Fukuchi and Duarte (2019; 20 studies, 587 people) found that in young adults, walking slower than comfortable cut knee flexion with a large effect (effect size -0.90), hip flexion and extension with small effects (-0.34 and -0.45) and ankle plantarflexion with a moderate one (-0.54); cadence and step length fell with large effects. Walking faster raised hip flexion and ankle plantarflexion with small effects (0.41 and 0.32) and cadence and step length with large ones. On R6, when you author a slower walk, shrink the swing leg's slide more than its `lift` range, and lower the cadence and the stride as well. When you author a faster walk, add hip swing and step length. This is for authoring a new cycle; when one cycle plays slower at runtime, locomotion.md, Keeping feet planted across speeds and directions, scales the swing lift with the stride.
- **Keep a walk's legs low and its torso high.** On the Roblox DevForum, a reply to an R6 animation set (2022) said that, in the poster's opinion, any R6 walk that raises the legs above the waist instead of a normal swing looks very weird; two other replies said the walk's torso sat too low. Human hips flex only 25 degrees in a walk (Brunnekreef et al., above). On R6, keep a walk's forward `lift` near 25 to 30 degrees and save high knees for runs. Keep the torso near standing height with only the small bob of the next section.

## Walking: pelvis, chest, head and arms

- **The pelvis turns forward on the stepping side; put that turn in the legs.** Brunnekreef et al. (2005) give 5 degrees of forward pelvic rotation in early stance and late swing and 5 backward in late stance and early swing. University of Oklahoma gait notes, after Saunders, Inman and Eberhart's determinants, say that in double support the pelvis is rotated forward on the loading side and back on the push-off side; this lengthens both legs and limits the drop of the centre of gravity. The notes add that later research questions whether the determinants matter for the reasons first proposed. An inertial-sensor study of 155 young adults (Jung and Kang, 2026) measured about 20 degrees of pelvic rotation range in walking, fast walking and running alike (20.4, 20.5 and 21.8, no significant change). The R6 torso carries the hips, so do not twist it for the pelvis. This sizes the leg slides in locomotion.md, Walk body motion: slide each leg's `p` z forward about 0.04 to 0.09 studs at its forward extreme and back by the same at its back extreme (our derivation: 5 to 10 degrees on a 0.5 stud hip radius). It lengthens the step without a bigger leg angle.
- **The chest turns about 8 degrees, nearly opposite the pelvis, and the head turns less.** Pontzer et al. (2009; 10 adults) measured at 1.5 m/s a shoulder rotation of 8.1 degrees, a head yaw of 5.2 degrees and a pelvis-to-shoulder phase difference of 149 degrees (nearly opposite). The torso twist itself is in locomotion.md, Walk body motion. On R6, give the `Head` a `twist` of about -0.36 times the torso twist on the same frames, so the head turns about two thirds as far as the chest in the world (our derivation from 5.2 / 8.1, assuming the head turns in phase with the chest).
- **Walking arms swing almost by themselves, opposite the legs.** Collins, Adamczyk and Kuo (2009; 10 walkers at 1.25 m/s) found that normal arm swing required minimal shoulder torque, with the arms' angular momentum opposite the legs'. Holding the arms still raised the metabolic cost by 12%, and the vertical ground reaction moment rose 63% without arm swing; swinging the arms opposite to normal magnified that moment and raised the cost by 26%. In their passive model, slow walking gave a very low arm swing at twice the leg frequency, which the authors say is similar to what humans show. Pontzer et al. (2009) found the front and back deltoid firing together rather than in turn to drive the arm. principles.md, Walk, already trails the arms through lags or springs instead of copying the torso; this is the reason, so never key the arms as a separate sine. For a very slow shuffle, give the arms a small swing once per step instead of once per stride. With the arms folded across the chest (as in a two-handed carry), Pontzer et al. found no significant change in walking shoulder rotation (8.6 against 8.1 degrees), so keep the walk's torso twist; the head yaw rose a little (6.0 against 5.2).
- **Arm swing grows with speed: about 30 degrees walking, 40 brisk, 45 jogging.** Jung and Kang (2026) measured a shoulder sagittal range of 27 to 32 degrees in walking, 38 to 42 in fast walking (130% of normal speed) and 43 to 48 in running (150%), and an elbow range of 21 to 27 degrees walking and 30 to 36 fast walking. On R6, set the arm's total `lift` range to about 30 degrees for a stroll, 40 for a brisk walk and 45 for a realistic jog; this sorts the 30 to 40 degrees of principles.md, Walk, by speed. The R6 runs that read well used a free arm near plus or minus 55 to 62 (principles.md, Run), about two and a half times the human jog's range (our derivation). Use that for a stylized run read from the player camera, and the human range for a realistic jog.
- **A slow walk sways more and bobs less; a fast walk bobs more and sways less.** Orendurff et al. (2004; 10 adults at 0.7 to 1.6 m/s) measured side-to-side centre of mass motion falling from 6.99 cm at the slowest speed to 3.85 cm at the fastest, while the vertical motion rose from 2.74 to 4.83 cm. On R6 this sizes the rule in body-mechanics.md, Walk mechanics (our derivation): a slow walk gets about 0.20 studs of total torso `p` x sway and 0.08 of `p` y bob; a fast walk about 0.11 of sway and 0.14 of bob. locomotion.md, Walk body motion, gives the values for a normal walk.

## Running: timing by speed

- **A jog's cycle barely shortens with speed; stride and stance do the work.** Jiménez et al. (2026; 25 recreational runners) give the men's medians at 7, 9, 11 and 13 km/h: cycles of 0.75, 0.74, 0.72 and 0.69 s; stance of 0.34, 0.30, 0.28 and 0.26 s (46, 42, 39 and 37% of the cycle); strides of 1.46, 1.85, 2.19 and 2.50 m; a vertical oscillation of 0.06 m at every speed. On R6 a jog cycle is 41 to 45 frames, with each foot down 16 to 20 frames. Build a faster jog with a longer stride and fewer stance frames, not a shorter cycle, and keep the bob near 0.17 studs at every speed. These speeds scale to 5.6 to 10.3 studs/s; at real duty the stance covers 1.9 to 2.5 studs up to 11 km/h (8.7 studs/s), about the 2.4 stud R6 stance (our derivation). SKILL.md's run rule (each foot down about 25% of the cycle) is for the fast, stylized run; use 37 to 46% only for a realistic jog. principles.md, Run, saw a 0.9 s cycle with duty 0.34 and a 15 degree lean read as a walk, so keep a jog's cycle near 0.7 s and its run poses (the swing slide, the back kick, the lean), and check the read in the strips.
- **Up to about 7 m/s runners lengthen the stride; above it they step faster.** Dorn, Schache and Pandy (2012; 9 runners, 3.5 to 9.0 m/s) found stride length growing more than stride frequency up to 7 m/s and the reverse above it; ground contact time fell steadily with speed, and flight time peaked at 7 m/s. Weyand et al. (2000; 33 runners with top speeds of 6.2 to 11.1 m/s) found the average ground force per body weight 1.26 times higher at 11.1 than at 6.2 m/s, while the swing time did not change. Within one character, locomotion.md, Runs, already gives the rule: stride first, cadence only at the R6 stride limit. Between characters of different top speed, keep the swing leg's frames the same and give the faster one a harder push in stance (a sharper `p` y dip and drive), not a faster swing.
- **A sprint touches the ground for about 5 frames.** Nagahara et al. (2014; 12 male sprinters over 60 m) measured the shortest support time, 0.090 s, at the 20th step, a step frequency up to 4.87 Hz, a top speed of 10.04 m/s at the 23rd step and steps up to 2.14 m. On R6 a top-speed sprint step is about 12 frames with 5 to 6 contact frames, so each foot is down about 22% of the cycle; at that contact time the 2.4 stud stance plants cleanly at 25 to 27 studs/s (our derivation). pipeline.md, Locomotion and overlays, builds a 25 studs/s sprint near 15 studs/s and plays it faster because a stance cannot plant at a natural cadence; that holds near a jog's three steps a second. locomotion.md, Runs, gives about 17 frames per step for a distance runner's sprint. Use the 12-frame step with 5-frame contacts only for an all-out sprint.
- **Sprint acceleration changes at about the 4th and the 14th step.** Nagahara et al. (2014) found two breakpoints, at step 4.4 and step 14.1 on average. Step frequency rose until the 4th step and then held; support time fell fast to the 4th step and then slowly. Before the first breakpoint the foot landed behind the centre of gravity and the knee did not flex during support. The centre of gravity rose fast to step 4, slowly to step 14, then held; the head angle rose until about step 15; the upper trunk reached 75.7 degrees from horizontal (about 14 from vertical) by step 25. locomotion.md, Runs, already lands the first steps behind the body. On R6, for a dash start: in steps 1 to 4 keep the stance leg at full length (no `p` y slide after contact) and raise the cadence each step; from step 5 plant ahead of the torso and start the stance slide; raise the torso gradually until about step 14; from about step 15 run the steady cycle with the head upright.

## Running: pelvis, chest, arms and head

- **A running pelvis turns 14 to 19 degrees and drops 14 to 17; keep most of it out of the torso.** Kovše et al. (22 female recreational runners at 10 to 14 km/h) measured a pelvic rotation range of 14 to 19 degrees, an obliquity range of 14 to 17 degrees and a tilt range of about 8 to 9 degrees. Rotation and obliquity ranges grew with speed; the tilt range did not. Toe-off came near 40% of the cycle. On R6 a 15 degree torso roll would tilt the shoulders as much, so keep the run's torso roll near the 4.5 degree start in principles.md, Run, and let the swing leg's slide carry the drop. Show the pelvic rotation as each leg's `p` z slide of about 0.06 to 0.08 studs (our derivation: 7 to 9.5 degrees on a 0.5 stud hip radius).
- **In a run the chest turns three times as far as in a walk, and the head barely turns.** Pontzer et al. (2009) measured at 3.0 m/s a shoulder rotation of 23.75 degrees (8.1 walking), a head yaw of 6.1 degrees and a pelvis-to-shoulder phase difference of 94 degrees (149 walking), with a 0.75 s stride. On R6, give a run about three times the walk's torso `twist`, with its extremes about a quarter cycle away from the legs' extremes (10 to 11 frames of a 42-frame cycle) rather than on the contact frames. The source gives the size of the offset, not which part leads, so try both and compare them in the strips. Give the `Head` a `twist` of about -0.74 times the torso twist, so it turns about a quarter as far as the chest in the world (our derivation from 6.1 / 23.75). On the Roblox DevForum (2019), reviewers of an R6-style run said the near-still torso and head made it look blocky and stiff, and one suggested twisting the torso left and right.
- **When the arms cannot swing, the chest and head turn more.** In the same study, running with the arms folded across the chest raised the shoulder rotation from 23.75 to 35.68 degrees and the head yaw from 6.1 to 11.3 degrees. In walking the shoulder rotation did not change significantly (8.6 against 8.1); the head yaw rose a little (6.0 against 5.2). On R6, a run with the arms busy (a two-handed weapon, a carried object) gets about 1.5 times the torso twist and a `Head` twist of about -0.68 times the torso twist, so the head turns nearly twice as far in the world as in a free-armed run (our derivation). A walk keeps its twist.

## Starts, stops and turns

- **Starting a walk: the backward pressure shift peaks about 27 frames in.** Storniolo et al. (2025; 16 trained men starting from a stand) measured the centre of pressure moving back by 46.6 mm, its first backward peak coming 449 ms after the shift began; most showed a forward "diving" strategy. The authors note that starting to walk requires pushing the centre of gravity forward. On R6, for an NPC starting from a stand, spend about 27 frames tipping the torso forward with `p` z and a small lean, together with the sideways shift onto the stance foot (body-mechanics.md, Balance and weight shift), before the first foot lifts. A player's start cannot wait that long: compress the tip to a few frames (a starting value).
- **The first braking step absorbs; the later ones plant ahead.** Hitchens et al. (2026; 19 team-sport athletes stopping from 5.1 to 7.1 m/s) measured early decelerations of 4.3 to 5.1 m/s^2 and peak vertical forces of 4.1 to 5.3 body weights; the authors note that the first three steps make up about half of a stop from high speed. In step 1, more hip and knee flexion and a lower centre of mass went with more braking force. In steps 2 and 3, a lower centre of mass at touchdown, the foot further ahead of it and the shank inclined back went with more braking force. On R6, make the first braking step a deep absorb: the torso drops and the planted leg slides up into it. Plant the later steps further ahead of the torso with the torso kept low and leaning back. locomotion.md, Starts, stops and turns, gives the number of steps, the duration and the drop.
- **A turn on the spot starts at the head, then the chest, the hips and the feet.** Khobkhun and Thanakamchokchai (2023; 50 adults turning 180 degrees in 1.5, 2 and 3 s) measured the onsets of the fast turn in the same order in men and women. Men's medians: head 0.328 s, thorax 0.366, pelvis 0.430, leading foot 0.459, trailing foot 0.540. Women's: 0.323, 0.340, 0.393, 0.514 and 0.705. The head led the thorax by a median peak of 24 to 27 degrees, and the fast turn took 2.5 steps. R6 has one torso for thorax and pelvis. The measured head-to-thorax onset gap, about 1 to 2 frames, is a lower bound: at 60 fps the head and torso look as if they start together from the player camera (our reading), so the turn reads from the head's angle lead. Start the `Head` `twist`, let it reach its lead within the first 4 to 6 frames, and start the torso 3 to 6 frames after the head (starting values). Show the pelvis's later turn as the planted feet pivoting (`Feet.flat` target yaw as a function of time) 3 to 4 frames after the torso starts. Then lift the leading foot and the trailing foot (men's medians: about 2 and then 5 frames apart; women's: about 7 and then 11). Keep the head up to about 25 degrees ahead of the torso, as principles.md, Attack or combo, does for a spin, and turn 180 degrees in two or three steps.
- **A late-cued turn is usually a step turn off the outside foot.** Tillman, Molino and Zaferiou (2023; young adults making 90 degree walking turns) report that late-cued turns were 73% step turns, while pre-planned turns split evenly between step and spin turns. In step turns, the largest push toward the new direction came during the outside foot's single support. In spin turns, walkers leaned so their centre of mass was often outside the inside foot. On R6, for a turn the player triggers mid-walk, move the torso's `p` x toward the new heading while the outside foot carries the weight, then step the inside foot's `Feet.flat` target out along the new heading with its yaw turned. Keep the spin turn (a pivot on the inside foot with the torso leaning into the turn) for planned, flowing turns.
- **The head leads a walking turn by 10 to 20 degrees, more when the turn is a surprise.** Weiss, Moudy and Wood (2025) report that for planned 90 degree walking turns the head led the trunk by 10 to 20 degrees (citing Sreenivasa et al.), and measured a median of 38.4 degrees in their own turn, made mid-walk on a verbal cue. On R6, keep the `Head` `twist` 10 to 20 degrees ahead of the torso in a planned turn and up to about 38 degrees for a sudden, reacting turn; locomotion.md, Starts, stops and turns, has the head-first rule for run turns.

## Punches

- **A full-power straight takes 27 frames with the lead hand and 40 with the rear.** Wan and Liu (2026; 17 elite boxers) measured 0.442 s for lead and 0.667 s for rear straights from the lowest knee bend to contact, a trunk rotation range of 42.7 against 73.0 degrees and a peak forward trunk speed of 220 against 270 deg/s. On R6, a full-effort lead straight runs about 27 frames and a rear straight about 40 from the load to contact. Turn the torso 43 degrees for the lead and 73 for the rear, peaking at about 3.7 and 4.5 degrees per frame. A high-speed camera study of boxing and sanda athletes (Xu, Mao and Xi, 2024) measured 10.4 to 13.0 rad/s (600 to 740 deg/s) for the hip line, trunk and shoulder line in a cross, so the torso's peak speed depends on the study: 4 to 12 degrees per frame. These are human timings for an NPC power punch or a cinematic one; a player's light attack stays on combat.md, Frame budgets: startup, active, recovery.
- **The fist peaks before contact and arrives about 20% slower.** Wan and Liu (2026) measured peak fist speeds of 6.76 m/s (lead) and 7.75 m/s (rear) and contact speeds of 5.24 and 6.18 m/s, a 20 to 23% loss before the hit (our derivation). Xu, Mao and Xi (2024) measured 7.3 to 8.5 m/s in the cross and 9.9 to 11.0 m/s in the uppercut. On R6 the fist peaks near 19 to 24 studs/s in a straight (0.32 to 0.40 studs per frame) and up to 31 studs/s in an uppercut (0.52 per frame; our scaling). Keep this loss only for a realistic NPC or cinematic straight: print the fist speed by forward kinematics (SKILL.md, Measure before you look); the fastest frame comes before contact, and the contact frame keeps about 80% of that speed on an `auto` key. SKILL.md's "no ease into an impact" forbids a stop at the contact; a 20% loss from the peak is not that stop. In a gameplay strike the contact frame or the frame before it carries the peak speed (r6-craft.md, "How battlegrounds sets are built and timed": bring the fist into the contact at rising speed; SKILL.md, the Game feel row).
- **The elbow drives the cross; the shoulder drives the hook and the uppercut.** Dinu and Louis (2020; 15 elite and 8 junior boxers, measured at impact) found the elbow contributing most to the cross (39% elite, 27% junior) and the shoulder most to the hook and uppercut (50 to 71%). Juniors used more shoulder (29% against 16%) and trunk (16% against 7%) in the cross. Punch speed tended to be highest in the hook. On R6, put the cross's speed into the arm's piston slide over the last frames with the arm angle nearly held (principles.md, Attack or combo: 0.3 to 0.6 studs). Put the hook's and the uppercut's speed into the arm's swing (`lift` and `side`) with the torso turn. A cross that swings the whole arm from the shoulder looks like a junior's technique.
- **A cross moves the weight toward the lead foot; an uppercut keeps it centred.** Dinu and Louis (2020) estimated the ground force split at impact from where the centre of mass sat between the feet: lead to rear 61 to 39% in the cross, 59 to 41% in the hook and 50 to 50% in the uppercut (elite); only the cross split was significant. Xu, Mao and Xi (2024) measured the centre of mass moving 56 to 86 mm forward and back during a cross. Wan and Liu (2026) found each punch loading the leg on its own side harder (rear leg 1132 N in rear punches, lead leg 1005 N in lead punches). On R6, move the centre of mass (body-mechanics.md, Centre of mass on R6) about 0.16 to 0.25 studs forward over a cross, mostly with the torso's `p` z, and end it about 60% of the way from the rear sole to the lead sole. Keep an uppercut's centre of mass midway between the feet.
- **The rear leg is straight on the cross's fastest frame; the uppercut stays bent.** Xu, Mao and Xi (2024) took all joint angles on the right side and discuss the knee as the back leg's push-off. They measured the knee at 168 to 176 degrees (nearly straight) at peak cross speed and 152 degrees at peak uppercut speed. The angle between upper arm and trunk was 105 degrees in the cross and 64 to 65 in the uppercut. On R6, on the cross's fastest frame give the rear leg no slide (the drive is done) and the striking arm about 105 degrees of `lift` against the torso (pointing forward and slightly up). On the uppercut's fastest frame keep the legs slid up about 0.06 studs (our derivation from a 28 degree bend) and the arm near 65 degrees of `lift`, still climbing.

## Throws

- **A pitch peaks the pelvis first, the trunk second and the arm last.** Escamilla et al. (2025; 18 college pitchers) measured, on a scale from lead-foot contact (0%) to ball release (100%), a peak pelvis speed of 569 to 581 deg/s at 24 to 25% and a peak upper-trunk speed of 1088 to 1096 deg/s at 46 to 47%. Elbow extension reached 2541 to 2563 deg/s and shoulder internal rotation 6415 to 6468 deg/s in arm acceleration. The stride was 78 to 79% of body height, and the lead knee rose to 56 to 61% of body height. R6 has one torso for pelvis and chest. Start the turn at foot contact by pivoting the rear foot's `Feet.flat` target yaw toward the target while the torso still faces back. Peak the torso `twist` near the middle of the interval at up to about 18 degrees per frame, and put the arm's fastest frames last, just before release. A human stride scales to 3.9 studs, far more than planted R6 legs reach with flat soles (SKILL.md, Feet rule): take the stride the rule allows and sell its length with the torso's travel toward the target and its lean. For the leg kick, lift the lead leg to about 90 degrees of `lift` and raise it 0.7 to 0.95 studs with `p` y in torso axes, as the decoded run raises its swing leg at knee drive (idle-run-land.md, "How the run works"). This raise moves the leg across its length, not along it (the knee rule above); its centre line then ends 2.7 to 2.95 studs up, 54 to 59% of the 5-stud body, near the pitchers' knee height (our derivation).
- **Only the order pelvis, trunk, arm is reliable.** Scarborough et al. (2021; 14 pitchers, 71 curveballs, plus fastballs from 12) found that none of the 71 curveballs followed the full five-segment order pelvis, trunk, arm, forearm, hand; they cite the same for the fastball patterns of their earlier study. The closest and the most common orders kept pelvis, trunk, then arm, with the forearm and hand together or reversed; 49% of curveball and 43% of fastball sequences had an altered distal order. On R6 the forearm and hand live inside the Arm part, so only the feet's pivot, the torso, then the arm must stay in order: the torso's peak strictly before the arm's. A held prop may peak with the arm.
- **A pass coils the chest 40 degrees back, keeps the hips about 20 degrees ahead, then turns about 60.** Labbe et al. (2025; a pilot study of 8 quarterbacks with inertial sensors) measured the trunk turned 40 degrees toward the throwing side at foot contact, reversing during acceleration to a maximum of 21 degrees the other way (about 60 degrees in all). Hip-shoulder separation stayed about 20 degrees, the hips ahead in cocking and the trunk ahead in the follow-through, dropping to 11 at release. The trunk leaned 2 to 9 degrees away from the throwing arm, and the stride knee stayed bent (29 to 46 degrees). On R6 the hips and chest are one part, so show the separation in the legs. At foot contact, yaw the planted feet's `Feet.flat` targets about 20 degrees further toward the target than the torso (inside SKILL.md, Toe yaw). Turn the torso about 60 degrees through release with a 2 to 9 degree `side` roll away from the throwing arm, and let the feet's yaw trail the torso by about 20 degrees in the follow-through. Keep the lead leg slid up 0.06 to 0.16 studs (our derivation from 29 to 46 degrees). principles.md, Attack or combo, turns the chest away 26 degrees for a wide fling across the body; use 40 for an overhand pass.

## Kicks

- **In an elite roundhouse, speed grows down the leg: hip about 2.4 m/s, knee about 6.7, foot about 14.** Vagner et al. (2023; systematic review) report roundhouse-kick foot speeds into a target of 6.5 m/s (novice), 11.4 (sub-elite) and 14.3 (elite), with the elite hip at 2.4 m/s, the knee at 6.7 m/s and knee extension at 1516 deg/s. Front-kick foot speeds into a target ran from 7.7 m/s (novice) to 9.6 (elite). A whole roundhouse took 0.84 s (elite) to 1.06 s (novice) as the review defines it. On R6 an elite roundhouse foot scales to about 41 studs/s, which a 2-stud leg reaches turning about 20 degrees per frame; an elite front kick near 27 studs/s needs about 13 degrees per frame (our derivation). Both stay under the 25 degrees per frame cap (principles.md, Strike speed and retiming).
- **The thigh stops so the shank can whip; on R6 the slide does the whip.** Kellis and Katis (2007; review of the instep kick) describe the thigh reaching its peak angular speed (about 516 to 573 deg/s) just before the knee starts to extend, then slowing to almost zero at ball contact while the shank and foot reach their peak; in the backswing the hip extends up to 29 degrees. A rigid leg cannot slow its top while its end speeds up. On R6, swing the leg about 29 degrees behind the torso, chamber it by sliding it along its own axis into the torso (0.7 to 0.95 studs, the run's knee slide; `p = s * (R * Vector3.new(0, 1, 0))`, the knee rule above) as the `lift` swings through, then in the last 2 to 3 frames before contact slide it back along its axis to full length while the `lift` slows, so the foot gains speed along the leg as the swing eases. A `p` y slide would not do this: on a leg lifted far forward, removing it drops the foot instead of extending it. Never slide it past full length: the leg must touch the torso on every frame (r6-mechanics.md, Joint gaps). On a leg lifted far forward the slide also carries its top end back, so check in the side strip that the top end stays inside the torso.
- **A pivoting support foot powers the hip turn.** Razm et al. (2026; narrative review of 23 studies) report that pivoted support-leg positions, as in taekwondo, allowed more hip rotation and joint power, while non-pivoted or narrower stances limited force transfer and balance; the pivot also loads the support knee. On R6 this is SKILL.md, Toe yaw: turn the support foot's `Feet.flat` target yaw with the torso through the kick (a target may be a function of time).
- **A round kick lands about 34 frames after the first muscle fires; a double kick's second hit 15 to 16 frames after its first.** Chen et al. (2025; 28 experienced taekwondo athletes reacting to a light) measured 560 ms from the first muscle activity (a lead-fist clench) to the round kick's contact, and 610 ms when more kicks were to follow. A double kick's second contact came 251 to 269 ms after its first, and a turning kick landed 885 ms after the double kick's last contact. The premotor time, from the light to the first muscle activity, was 149 to 191 ms. On R6: a real-speed round kick is about 34 frames to contact (37 when it opens a chain), a double kick's second hit lands 15 to 16 frames after its first, and a turning kick that follows a chain lands about 53 frames after the previous hit. An NPC reacting to a cue starts about 9 to 11 frames after it; this is a simple reaction to a light, not the player's window in combat.md, Telegraphs and reaction time. These are human timings for NPCs and cutscenes; a player's kick stays on combat.md, Frame budgets: startup, active, recovery.

## Jumps

- **A countermovement drops about a fifth of body height, more than R6 legs can show.** Ho et al. (2026; 62 active adults, arms crossed) measured a countermovement depth of 0.36 m for a 0.32 m jump, a takeoff speed of 2.48 m/s, a peak downward speed of 1.43 m/s, a hip range of 85 degrees and a peak hip extension speed of 512 deg/s. Its 0.78 s to takeoff and 0.29 s push (47 and 17 frames) fit the phase timing in body-mechanics.md, Takeoff and landing. On R6 the depth scales to about 1.0 stud, more than the 0.75 to 0.86 stud deep crouch that still reads (r6-mechanics.md, Planted feet on rigid legs). Drop the torso about 0.8 studs and get the rest of the load from a forward torso lean and the arms swung back. The dip falls at most about 58% of the takeoff speed (1.43 / 2.48), so drive down firmly but leave faster.
- **Swinging the arms adds height.** Spires et al. (2026; 30 recreationally active adults) measured mean jump heights of 39.7 with arm swing against 30.7 without (the paper gives no unit for jump height; its body heights are in inches), about 30% higher, and cite earlier reports of about 10 to 30%. On R6 a Humanoid jump's height is set by physics, so the arm throw only sells the power: swing the arms back in the dip and up on the push frames, as body-mechanics.md, Takeoff and landing, and principles.md, Jump and landing, already say.

## Does not transfer to R6

- Ankle rocker and toe-off (10 degrees of plantarflexion in early stance, 10 of dorsiflexion in midstance, 20 of plantarflexion in late stance; Brunnekreef et al.): there is no foot joint, and planted soles stay flat (SKILL.md, Feet rule).
- The 20 degree stance-knee bend of a walk: on R6 it is a 0.03 stud slide, below what reads. Put that give into the torso's `p` y dip instead (body-mechanics.md, Walk mechanics).
- Running pelvic obliquity as a torso roll (14 to 17 degrees, Kovše et al.): the single torso part would tilt the shoulders as much.
- Separation between pelvis and chest inside the body (about 20 degrees in a pass, the pelvis peaking before the trunk in a pitch): one torso part; only the feet's yaw against the torso can show it.
- Pitching shoulder internal rotation (about 6400 deg/s) and elbow extension (about 2500 deg/s): there is no forearm, a twist about the arm's long axis barely moves the hand (body-mechanics.md, Walk mechanics), and the speeds are 1.7 to 4.3 times the 25 degrees per frame cap (our derivation).
- The order of forearm and hand peaks in throws: both live inside one Arm part.
- The thigh braking while the shank whips in a kick: one rigid leg; the slide in Kicks stands in.
- The foot's angle at contact in running (men's medians of 10 to 15 degrees in Jiménez et al.): no foot joint.
- Lumbar extension separate from the chest (23 degrees at the quarterback's maximal shoulder external rotation, Labbe et al.): there is no spine. A whole-torso lean back from the waist (SKILL.md, Waist rule) shows a lean, not an arch.
- Elbow range in walking (21 to 27 degrees walking, 30 to 36 fast walking; Jung and Kang): there is no elbow, and a slide that small does not read.

## Not covered

- The timing in milliseconds of the peak pelvis, trunk, upper-arm and fist speeds within punches and throws: the open sources found gave peak values, segment contributions and normalized timing (percent of foot contact to release), not milliseconds.
- Head vertical motion and head pitch stabilization in walking and running: no open source with numbers was found.
- Trunk forward lean by jogging speed: only the sprint values above and in locomotion.md were found.
- Landing absorption after a jump beyond the drop-landing data in body-mechanics.md, Takeoff and landing.
- Trunk lean and the penultimate braking step in sidestep cutting: the sources found gave no usable numbers in their text.
- The number of steps a walk takes to reach steady speed from a stand.
- Dorn, Schache and Pandy's per-speed tables and Orendurff et al.'s full text: their text statements and the abstract were used.
- Whether Pontzer et al.'s rotation "amplitude" is a full or a half range: the text read did not define it, so the R6 rules above use its ratios only.
